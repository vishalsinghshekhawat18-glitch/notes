11:T630,Macroeconomics identifies three primary engines of inflation:

### 1. Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too Much Money Chasing Too Few Goods")
- Occurs when **Aggregate Demand ($AD$)** expands beyond the economy's potential productive capacity at full employment ($Y^*$).
- **Triggers**: Fiscal deficit expansion, loose monetary policy (excessive money growth), surging consumer credit, or export booms.
- **Graphically**: The $AD$ curve shifts rightward along an upward-sloping or vertical Aggregate Supply ($AS$) curve, pulling price level from $P_1$ to $P_2$.

### 2. Cost-Push Inflation (Supply-Shock / Cost-Inflation)
- Occurs when the aggregate costs of production increase, shifting the **Short-Run Aggregate Supply ($SRAS$)** curve leftward.
- **Triggers**: Imported commodity shocks (e.g., global Brent crude surges), supply chain blockages, minimum wage hikes exceeding productivity growth, indirect tax hikes (GST), or agricultural crop failure.
- **Wage-Price Spiral**: Workers demand higher nominal wages to offset cost-of-living increases $\implies$ firms raise output prices to protect profit margins $\implies$ triggers a self-reinforcing upward spiral.

### 3. Structural Inflation (Myrdal / Prebisch Developing Economy Model)
- Prevalent in developing economies like India due to structural supply bottlenecks (e.g., poor cold storage, inadequate transport infrastructure, agricultural marketing rigidities, and balance-of-payments import dependencies).
- Structural inflation cannot be permanently extinguished solely by monetary contraction without severely damaging output.12:T70b,Economics classifies inflation along two distinct analytical dimensions:

```
                             PRICE LEVEL DYNAMICS
                                       │
         ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                           ▼
   SPEED / INTENSITY                                         DIRECTION / PHENOMENON
• Creeping (< 3% p.a.)                                      • Inflation (Price level rising)
• Walking (3% to 10% p.a.)                                  • Disinflation (Inflation rate falling, e.g. 7% -> 4%)
• Running (10% to 20% p.a.)                                 • Deflation (Price level falling, negative inflation)
• Galloping (20% to 1000% p.a.)                             • Reflation (Deliberate stimulus to recover from deflation)
• Hyperinflation (> 50% per month / Weimar/Zimbabwe)        • Stagflation (Stagnant Growth + High Inflation + High Unemployment)
```

### Detailed Trajectory Definitions
1. **Disinflation vs Deflation**:
   - **Disinflation**: Inflation rate drops from $+8\%$ to $+3\%$. Prices are **still increasing**, but at a slower speed. Real purchasing power still erodes, but more slowly.
   - **Deflation**: Inflation rate becomes negative (e.g., $-2\%$). Prices are **falling**. Leads to a dangerous deflationary spiral as consumers postpone consumption expecting lower future prices.
2. **Stagflation**:
   - The breakdown of the classical Phillips Curve trade-off. Output contracts ($GDP \downarrow$), unemployment rises ($U \uparrow$), while inflation remains high ($P \uparrow$). Exemplified by the 1970s OPEC oil crises.13:T41f,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Disinflation vs Deflation**: In disinflation, prices **DO NOT fall**; the *rate of inflation* falls. In deflation, the absolute price level falls.
- **Trap 2: Core vs Headline Inflation**: Headline inflation includes volatile components (Food and Fuel). **Core Inflation** strips out volatile Food and Fuel components (\text{Core} = \text{Headline} - [\text{Food} + \text{Fuel}]). In India, RBI targets **Headline CPI-Combined**, but monitors Core CPI for underlying demand trends.
- **Trap 3: Stagflation Policy Dilemma**: Standard monetary policy faces a policy paralysis during stagflation: raising interest rates to fight inflation worsens unemployment, while cutting interest rates to boost output accelerates inflation.

$$\text{Inflation Rate}_t = \left(\frac{\text{CPI}_t - \text{CPI}_{t-12}}{\text{CPI}_{t-12}}\right) \times 100$$

The **Base Effect** occurs because the denominator $\text{CPI}_{t-12}$ dictates the percentage change independently of month-on-month ($MoM$) price movements:

### 1. High Base Effect (Favourable / Deflationary Base)
- If CPI spiked abnormally in July 2025 due to a heatwave ($\text{CPI}_{\text{Jul 2025}} = 180$),
- Then in July 2026, even if prices remain elevated ($\text{CPI}_{\text{Jul 2026}} = 183$),
- The recorded YoY inflation will be just $\frac{183 - 180}{180} \times 100 = 1.67\%$.
- *Outcome*: Inflation drops statistically even though the actual cost of living has not fallen.

### 2. Low Base Effect (Unfavourable / Inflationary Base)
- If CPI collapsed in November 2025 due to a bumper harvest ($\text{CPI}_{\text{Nov 2025}} = 160$),
- Then in November 2026, normal seasonal prices ($\text{CPI}_{\text{Nov 2026}} = 172$) produce an alarming recorded inflation of $\frac{172 - 160}{160} \times 100 = 7.5\%$.
- *Outcome*: Statistical inflation surges without abnormal demand pressure.16:T4ec,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Services in WPI**: WPI does **NOT** include services. If an exam option states "WPI captures inflation in healthcare and transport services", it is **strictly false**.
- **Trap 2: RBI Target Transition**: Prior to 2014, RBI used WPI for policy guidance. Following the **Urjit Patel Committee Report (2014)**, RBI formally adopted **CPI-Combined (Headline)** as its sole nominal anchor for Flexible Inflation Targeting under the 2016 statutory amendments.
- **Trap 3: Housing in Rural CPI**: The "Housing" sub-group ($10.07\%$ weight in CPI-Combined) is surveyed and included **only in Urban CPI**. In Rural CPI, the weight for Housing is $0\%$.
- **Trap 4: Imported Goods in GDP Deflator vs CPI**: CPI includes imported consumer goods (e.g., imported edible oil or electronics). The **GDP Deflator completely excludes all imported goods** because it only measures prices of domestically produced output.

- **UPSC APFC, RPSC RAS & Banking Exams**: Compiling ministries (MoSPI vs DPIIT), base years (2012 vs 2011-12), and exclusion of services in WPI.17:T48e,The evolution of the Phillips Curve represents a cornerstone of modern monetary macroeconomics:

### 1. Short-Run Phillips Curve (A.W. Phillips, 1958)
- Originally demonstrated an empirical trade-off: Higher Inflation $\implies$ Lower Unemployment.
- **Mechanism**: When aggregate demand expands, firms hire more workers (lowering unemployment) while bidding up wages and prices (higher inflation).
$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta(u_t - u_n) + v$$
Where $\pi_t$ is inflation, $\pi_t^e$ is expected inflation, $u_t$ is unemployment, $u_n$ is the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), and $v$ is a supply shock.

### 2. Long-Run Vertical Phillips Curve (Friedman & Phelps, 1968)
- **Natural Rate Hypothesis**: In the long run, workers and firms adjust their expectations completely ($\,\pi_t^e = \pi_t\,$).
- When expectations catch up, real wages adjust back to equilibrium, and unemployment returns to the **Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)** regardless of inflation rate.
- **Conclusion**: Central banks **cannot** permanently buy lower unemployment by tolerating higher inflation. Attempting to do so only generates higher inflation expectations with no output gain.18:T72a,Macroeconomics separates the welfare costs of inflation into anticipated and unanticipated components:

```
                         COSTS OF INFLATION
                                 │
        ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                 ▼
   ANTICIPATED INFLATION                             UNANTICIPATED INFLATION
• Shoe-Leather Costs (wasted trips to bank)         • Arbitrary wealth transfer from
• Menu Costs (cost of updating price lists)           creditors to debtors
• Tax Bracket Creep (fiscal drag)                   • Real wage erosion for fixed earners
• Relative Price Distortions                        • Uncertainty suppressing capex
```

### 1. Unanticipated Inflation: The Creditor-Debtor Transfer
- A borrower takes a loan at a nominal interest rate of $10\%$, expecting inflation to be $4\%$ (expected real interest rate = $6\%$).
- If inflation unexpectedly surges to $12\%$, the actual ex-post real interest rate becomes: $10\% - 12\% = -2\%$.
- **Winner**: The **Debtor** (repays the loan with heavily degraded rupees).
- **Loser**: The **Creditor / Saver** (receives purchasing power worth less than the original principal).

### 2. Anticipated Inflation: Friction Costs
- **Shoe-Leather Costs**: High inflation makes holding non-interest-bearing cash costly, forcing consumers to keep funds in interest accounts and make frequent bank/ATM trips.
- **Menu Costs**: Resources wasted constantly repricing catalogs, changing POS software, and reprinting menus.
- **Bracket Creep**: Progressive tax systems tax nominal income gains, pushing individuals into higher tax brackets without any real increase in purchasing power.19:T416,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Who Gains from Unexpected Inflation?**: **Borrowers / Debtors gain**, while **Lenders / Creditors / Savers lose**. Fixed pension earners lose; equity investors and real estate owners are partially hedged.
- **Trap 2: Shape of Phillips Curve in Long Run**: The Short-Run Phillips Curve is downward sloping, but the Long-Run Phillips Curve is **strictly vertical** at NAIRU.
- **Trap 3: RBI Inflation Expectations Surveys**: RBI conducts two distinct surveys:
  1. **IESH (Inflation Expectations Survey of Households)**: Captures qualitative and quantitative 3-month and 1-year ahead household inflation perceptions.
  2. **SPF (Survey of Professional Forecasters)**: Captures institutional and academic economic forecasts.

- **RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A**: Mathematical equations of expectations-augmented Phillips curve, NAIRU, and debtor-creditor transfers.
