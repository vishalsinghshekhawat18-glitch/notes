Inflation Theories and Typologies: Demand-Pull, Cost-Push, Structural Inflation, Disinflation, Deflation & Stagflation

The core theoretical foundations of price level inflation: definitions of persistent price rises, the causal dichotomy between Demand-Pull ($AD > AS$) and Cost-Push ($AS$ contraction), the structuralist inflation model for developing economies, speed-based classifications (creeping to hyperinflation), and distinct price path phenomena (disinflation, deflation, reflation, and stagflation).

Intuition & Context

The Price Thermometer: Measuring Purchasing Power Erosion

Inflation is the persistent decrease in the purchasing power of money. If a basket of groceries costs ₹1,000 today and ₹1,060 next year, the purchasing power of ₹1,000 has eroded by 6%6%6%.

Crucially, inflation is not a one-time increase in the price of tomatoes due to seasonal monsoon disruptions, nor is it a rise in the price of smartphones while clothing and food prices fall. Inflation is a broad-based, sustained increase in the general price index across the economy over time.

Core Principle

Causal Theories: Demand-Pull, Cost-Push & Structural Inflation

Macroeconomics identifies three primary engines of inflation:

1. Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too Much Money Chasing Too Few Goods")

  • Occurs when Aggregate Demand (ADADAD) expands beyond the economy's potential productive capacity at full employment (Y∗Y^*Y∗).
  • Triggers: Fiscal deficit expansion, loose monetary policy (excessive money growth), surging consumer credit, or export booms.
  • Graphically: The ADADAD curve shifts rightward along an upward-sloping or vertical Aggregate Supply (ASASAS) curve, pulling price level from P1P_1P1​ to P2P_2P2​.

2. Cost-Push Inflation (Supply-Shock / Cost-Inflation)

  • Occurs when the aggregate costs of production increase, shifting the Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRASSRASSRAS) curve leftward.
  • Triggers: Imported commodity shocks (e.g., global Brent crude surges), supply chain blockages, minimum wage hikes exceeding productivity growth, indirect tax hikes (GST), or agricultural crop failure.
  • Wage-Price Spiral: Workers demand higher nominal wages to offset cost-of-living increases   ⟹  \implies⟹ firms raise output prices to protect profit margins   ⟹  \implies⟹ triggers a self-reinforcing upward spiral.

3. Structural Inflation (Myrdal / Prebisch Developing Economy Model)

  • Prevalent in developing economies like India due to structural supply bottlenecks (e.g., poor cold storage, inadequate transport infrastructure, agricultural marketing rigidities, and balance-of-payments import dependencies).
  • Structural inflation cannot be permanently extinguished solely by monetary contraction without severely damaging output.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Velocity & Trajectory Typology of Price Dynamics

Economics classifies inflation along two distinct analytical dimensions:

                             PRICE LEVEL DYNAMICS
                                       │
         ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                           ▼
   SPEED / INTENSITY                                         DIRECTION / PHENOMENON
• Creeping (< 3% p.a.)                                      • Inflation (Price level rising)
• Walking (3% to 10% p.a.)                                  • Disinflation (Inflation rate falling, e.g. 7% -> 4%)
• Running (10% to 20% p.a.)                                 • Deflation (Price level falling, negative inflation)
• Galloping (20% to 1000% p.a.)                             • Reflation (Deliberate stimulus to recover from deflation)
• Hyperinflation (> 50% per month / Weimar/Zimbabwe)        • Stagflation (Stagnant Growth + High Inflation + High Unemployment)

Detailed Trajectory Definitions

  1. Disinflation vs Deflation:
    • Disinflation: Inflation rate drops from +8%+8%+8% to +3%+3%+3%. Prices are still increasing, but at a slower speed. Real purchasing power still erodes, but more slowly.
    • Deflation: Inflation rate becomes negative (e.g., −2%-2%−2%). Prices are falling. Leads to a dangerous deflationary spiral as consumers postpone consumption expecting lower future prices.
  2. Stagflation:
    • The breakdown of the classical Phillips Curve trade-off. Output contracts (GDP↓GDP \downarrowGDP↓), unemployment rises (U↑U \uparrowU↑), while inflation remains high (P↑P \uparrowP↑). Exemplified by the 1970s OPEC oil crises.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps, Distinctions & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Disinflation vs Deflation: In disinflation, prices DO NOT fall; the rate of inflation falls. In deflation, the absolute price level falls.
  • Trap 2: Core vs Headline Inflation: Headline inflation includes volatile components (Food and Fuel). Core Inflation strips out volatile Food and Fuel components (\text{Core} = \text{Headline} - [\text{Food} + \text{Fuel}]). In India, RBI targets Headline CPI-Combined, but monitors Core CPI for underlying demand trends.
  • Trap 3: Stagflation Policy Dilemma: Standard monetary policy faces a policy paralysis during stagflation: raising interest rates to fight inflation worsens unemployment, while cutting interest rates to boost output accelerates inflation.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Focus on demand-pull vs cost-push supply shocks, wage-price mechanisms, and core vs headline inflation.
  • RPSC RAS, SBI/IBPS PO & UPSC APFC: 2-mark definitions of Stagflation, Disinflation, Reflation, and Creeping/Hyperinflation.

Inflation Measurement in India: CPI (Combined, Rural, Urban) vs WPI vs GDP Deflator, Weighting Schemas & The Base Effect

The institutional and statistical architecture of price indices in India: Consumer Price Index (CPI-Combined, Rural, Urban with Base 2012 by MoSPI/NSO), Wholesale Price Index (WPI with Base 2011-12 by DPIIT), GDP Deflator, index item basket composition, weighting bifurcations (Food weight dominance), and the statistical arithmetic of the Base Effect.

Intuition & Context

Retail Basket vs Wholesale Mandi: Why Price Indices Diverge

Why does the newspaper report that WPI inflation is −1%-1%−1% (deflation) while your monthly grocery bill rose by +6%+6%+6% (CPI inflation)?

The difference lies in who is buying and what is inside the basket:

  • Wholesale Price Index (WPI) measures the bulk price of physical goods traded between businesses at the factory gate or wholesale mandi (dominated by manufactured goods and global crude metals, with 0%0%0% services).
  • Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the retail cost of living experienced by households, heavily dominated by food ($45.86%$ of the basket), housing, education, healthcare, and services.

When global metal and crude oil prices collapse, WPI plummets; but if domestic tomato and onion prices surge, CPI spikes.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Base Effect: Mathematical Mechanics & Distortions

Inflation is calculated as the percentage change in the price index over a 12-month period (Year-on-Year / YoY):
Inflation Ratet=(CPIt−CPIt−12CPIt−12)×100\text{Inflation Rate}t = \left(\frac{\text{CPI}t - \text{CPI}{t-12}}{\text{CPI}{t-12}}\right) \times 100Inflation Ratet​=(CPIt−12​CPIt​−CPIt−12​​)×100

The Base Effect occurs because the denominator CPIt−12\text{CPI}_{t-12}CPIt−12​ dictates the percentage change independently of month-on-month (MoMMoMMoM) price movements:

1. High Base Effect (Favourable / Deflationary Base)

  • If CPI spiked abnormally in July 2025 due to a heatwave (CPIJul 2025=180\text{CPI}_{\text{Jul 2025}} = 180CPIJul 2025​=180),
  • Then in July 2026, even if prices remain elevated (CPIJul 2026=183\text{CPI}_{\text{Jul 2026}} = 183CPIJul 2026​=183),
  • The recorded YoY inflation will be just 183−180180×100=1.67%\frac{183 - 180}{180} \times 100 = 1.67%180183−180​×100=1.67%.
  • Outcome: Inflation drops statistically even though the actual cost of living has not fallen.

2. Low Base Effect (Unfavourable / Inflationary Base)

  • If CPI collapsed in November 2025 due to a bumper harvest (CPINov 2025=160\text{CPI}_{\text{Nov 2025}} = 160CPINov 2025​=160),
  • Then in November 2026, normal seasonal prices (CPINov 2026=172\text{CPI}_{\text{Nov 2026}} = 172CPINov 2026​=172) produce an alarming recorded inflation of 172−160160×100=7.5%\frac{172 - 160}{160} \times 100 = 7.5%160172−160​×100=7.5%.
  • Outcome: Statistical inflation surges without abnormal demand pressure.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps, Base Year Parameters & PYQ Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Services in WPI: WPI does NOT include services. If an exam option states "WPI captures inflation in healthcare and transport services", it is strictly false.
  • Trap 2: RBI Target Transition: Prior to 2014, RBI used WPI for policy guidance. Following the Urjit Patel Committee Report (2014), RBI formally adopted CPI-Combined (Headline) as its sole nominal anchor for Flexible Inflation Targeting under the 2016 statutory amendments.
  • Trap 3: Housing in Rural CPI: The "Housing" sub-group ($10.07%$ weight in CPI-Combined) is surveyed and included only in Urban CPI. In Rural CPI, the weight for Housing is 0%0%0%.
  • Trap 4: Imported Goods in GDP Deflator vs CPI: CPI includes imported consumer goods (e.g., imported edible oil or electronics). The GDP Deflator completely excludes all imported goods because it only measures prices of domestically produced output.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Precise weights of CPI and WPI, Urjit Patel committee recommendations, GDP deflator calculations, and base effect.
  • UPSC APFC, RPSC RAS & Banking Exams: Compiling ministries (MoSPI vs DPIIT), base years (2012 vs 2011-12), and exclusion of services in WPI.

Inflation Expectations, The Phillips Curve (Short-Run vs Long-Run) & Economic Costs of Anticipated vs Unanticipated Inflation

The role of forward-looking inflation expectations in macroeconomic wage-setting and price determination: adaptive vs rational expectations, the Short-Run Phillips Curve (trade-off between inflation and unemployment) vs the Friedman-Phelps Long-Run Vertical Phillips Curve at Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), and the economic welfare costs of inflation (shoe-leather costs, menu costs, tax distortions, arbitrary wealth redistribution).

Intuition & Context

The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: Why Inflation Expectations Matter

Inflation is unique because what people expect to happen tomorrow directly drives what actually happens today.

If factory workers expect inflation to be 8%8%8% next year, they demand an 8%8%8% wage increase today. To pay those higher wages without losing profit, factory owners immediately raise output prices by 8%8%8%. The expectation of 8%8%8% inflation created 8%8%8% inflation! This is why central bankers are obsessed with anchoring inflation expectations—if the public trusts the central bank's inflation target, expectations remain steady and inflation remains contained.

Core Principle

The Phillips Curve: Short-Run Trade-off vs Long-Run Vertical Reality

The evolution of the Phillips Curve represents a cornerstone of modern monetary macroeconomics:

1. Short-Run Phillips Curve (A.W. Phillips, 1958)

  • Originally demonstrated an empirical trade-off: Higher Inflation   ⟹  \implies⟹ Lower Unemployment.
  • Mechanism: When aggregate demand expands, firms hire more workers (lowering unemployment) while bidding up wages and prices (higher inflation).

πt=πte−β(ut−un)+v\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta(u_t - u_n) + vπt​=πte​−β(ut​−un​)+v
Where πt\pi_tπt​ is inflation, πte\pi_t^eπte​ is expected inflation, utu_tut​ is unemployment, unu_nun​ is the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), and vvv is a supply shock.

2. Long-Run Vertical Phillips Curve (Friedman & Phelps, 1968)

  • Natural Rate Hypothesis: In the long run, workers and firms adjust their expectations completely ( πte=πt ,\pi_t^e = \pi_t,πte​=πt​).
  • When expectations catch up, real wages adjust back to equilibrium, and unemployment returns to the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU) regardless of inflation rate.
  • Conclusion: Central banks cannot permanently buy lower unemployment by tolerating higher inflation. Attempting to do so only generates higher inflation expectations with no output gain.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Economic Costs of Inflation & Distributional Transfers

Macroeconomics separates the welfare costs of inflation into anticipated and unanticipated components:

                         COSTS OF INFLATION
                                 │
        ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                 ▼
   ANTICIPATED INFLATION                             UNANTICIPATED INFLATION
• Shoe-Leather Costs (wasted trips to bank)         • Arbitrary wealth transfer from
• Menu Costs (cost of updating price lists)           creditors to debtors
• Tax Bracket Creep (fiscal drag)                   • Real wage erosion for fixed earners
• Relative Price Distortions                        • Uncertainty suppressing capex

1. Unanticipated Inflation: The Creditor-Debtor Transfer

  • A borrower takes a loan at a nominal interest rate of 10%10%10%, expecting inflation to be 4%4%4% (expected real interest rate = 6%6%6%).
  • If inflation unexpectedly surges to 12%12%12%, the actual ex-post real interest rate becomes: 10%−12%=−2%10% - 12% = -2%10%−12%=−2%.
  • Winner: The Debtor (repays the loan with heavily degraded rupees).
  • Loser: The Creditor / Saver (receives purchasing power worth less than the original principal).

2. Anticipated Inflation: Friction Costs

  • Shoe-Leather Costs: High inflation makes holding non-interest-bearing cash costly, forcing consumers to keep funds in interest accounts and make frequent bank/ATM trips.
  • Menu Costs: Resources wasted constantly repricing catalogs, changing POS software, and reprinting menus.
  • Bracket Creep: Progressive tax systems tax nominal income gains, pushing individuals into higher tax brackets without any real increase in purchasing power.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps, Survey Architecture & PYQ Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Who Gains from Unexpected Inflation?: Borrowers / Debtors gain, while Lenders / Creditors / Savers lose. Fixed pension earners lose; equity investors and real estate owners are partially hedged.
  • Trap 2: Shape of Phillips Curve in Long Run: The Short-Run Phillips Curve is downward sloping, but the Long-Run Phillips Curve is strictly vertical at NAIRU.
  • Trap 3: RBI Inflation Expectations Surveys: RBI conducts two distinct surveys:
    1. IESH (Inflation Expectations Survey of Households): Captures qualitative and quantitative 3-month and 1-year ahead household inflation perceptions.
    2. SPF (Survey of Professional Forecasters): Captures institutional and academic economic forecasts.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Mathematical equations of expectations-augmented Phillips curve, NAIRU, and debtor-creditor transfers.
  • RPSC RAS, APFC & SBI/IBPS PO: 2-mark definitions of NAIRU, Phillips Curve, menu costs, and winners/losers of inflation.
