### Detailed Breakdown of Channels
1. **Interest Rate Channel (Keynesian)**:
   - Policy Rate $\downarrow \implies$ Money market & bank lending rates $\downarrow \implies$ Real cost of borrowing $\downarrow \implies$ Consumer durables spending ($C \uparrow$) and business capital investment ($I \uparrow$) rise.
2. **Bank Lending / Credit Channel**:
   - Policy Rate $\downarrow$ & Liquidity injection $\implies$ Commercial bank reserves $\uparrow \implies$ Bank credit supply expands, providing loans to credit-constrained MSMEs and households.
3. **Asset Price Channel (Wealth Effect & Tobin's $q$)**:
   - Policy Rate $\downarrow \implies$ Discount rate on future cash flows falls $\implies$ Equity and real estate prices rise $\implies$ Household perceived wealth increases ($C \uparrow$) and Tobin's $q > 1$ stimulates corporate investment ($I \uparrow$).
4. **Exchange Rate Channel**:
   - Policy Rate $\downarrow \implies$ Domestic interest rate differential narrows $\implies$ Capital outflows $\implies$ Rupee depreciates $\implies$ Export competitiveness improves ($X \uparrow$) and imports become costlier ($M \downarrow$), expanding Net Exports ($NX \uparrow$).
5. **Expectations Channel**:
   - Credible policy signaling anchors long-term inflation expectations, preventing price-wage spirals before they materialize.15:T5fa,### Monetary Policy Stances Defined
- **Accommodative Stance**: Central bank is willing to cut interest rates or keep liquidity surplus to support growth, ruling out rate hikes in the near term.
- **Neutral Stance**: Central bank can move rates in either direction (rate hike, pause, or rate cut) depending strictly on incoming inflation and growth data.
- **Calibrated Tightening**: Rate cuts are off the table; the central bank will either pause or hike rates in calibrated steps.
- **Withdrawal of Accommodation**: Post-crisis normalization stance where the central bank steadily absorbs excess liquidity and raises rates back to neutral levels to prevent inflationary overheating.

### Historical Transmission Frictions in India & The External Benchmark Solution
Historically, when RBI cut the repo rate, commercial banks took 6 to 12 months to pass rate cuts to retail borrowers. Why?
1. **High Share of Fixed-Rate Term Deposits**: Banks pay fixed interest on existing 1–3 year deposits; reducing loan rates immediately would compress Net Interest Margin (NIM).
2. **Administered Small Savings Rates**: High interest rates on PPF/Sukanya Samriddhi prevented banks from reducing savings deposit rates without losing depositors.
3. **The External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) Reform (Oct 1, 2019)**: RBI mandated all commercial banks to link all new floating-rate retail and MSME loans directly to an external benchmark (such as the Policy Repo Rate or 3-Month T-Bill yield). This revolutionized transmission speed in India.16:T551,Central banks deploy several advanced unconventional tools:

### 1. Quantitative Easing (QE)
- Central bank creates electronic central bank reserves to buy massive quantities of long-term government bonds and private securities directly from financial markets.
- **Goal**: Expands the **size of central bank balance sheet**, lowers long-term bond yields, flattens the yield curve, and injects vast liquidity into commercial banks.

### 2. Forward Guidance
- Explicit verbal and written communication by the central bank committing to keep policy rates low for a specified duration or until specific economic thresholds (e.g., unemployment $< 6.5\%$ or inflation reaching target) are met.
- **Goal**: Anchors long-term market interest rates by eliminating uncertainty about future policy.

### 3. Yield Curve Control (YCC - Bank of Japan / Reserve Bank of Australia)
- Rather than buying a fixed quantity of bonds, the central bank commits to buying unlimited bonds at a specific price to peg a specific long-term yield (e.g., capping 10-year sovereign bond yields at $0.25\%$).

### 4. Negative Interest Rate Policy (NIRP - ECB / BoJ / Swiss National Bank)
- Central bank charges commercial banks a negative interest rate (e.g., $-0.50\%$) on their excess reserves deposited at the central bank, forcing banks to lend money to businesses rather than hoard cash.17:T86f,While RBI never reached the zero lower bound, it creatively engineered unconventional tools to overcome domestic liquidity bottlenecks:

### 1. Operation Twist (Simultaneous Special OMOs - Dec 2019)
- **Mechanism**: RBI simultaneously **bought long-term G-Secs** (10-year paper) worth ₹10,000 Crore while **selling short-term G-Secs** (1-year paper) worth ₹10,000 Crore.
- **Effect**: Pushed long-term bond prices up $\implies$ 10-year benchmark yields fell by ~40 bps $\implies$ lowered long-term corporate borrowing costs without increasing aggregate system liquidity or expanding RBI's balance sheet size.

### 2. Long-Term Repo Operations (LTRO & TLTRO - 2020)
- Standard repo operations provide 1-day to 14-day liquidity. Under LTRO, RBI offered **1-year and 3-year funding at the prevailing policy repo rate**.
- Under **Targeted LTRO (TLTRO)**, banks were mandated to invest the borrowed funds into investment-grade corporate bonds, commercial paper, and debentures issued by stressed sectors and NBFCs, preventing systemic corporate debt freezes during pandemic lockdowns.

### 3. G-Sec Acquisition Programme (G-SAP - 2021)
- RBI provided explicit upfront commitments to purchase specific quantums (e.g., ₹1 Lakh Crore per quarter) of government bonds via OMOs, calming market fears of heavy sovereign debt
