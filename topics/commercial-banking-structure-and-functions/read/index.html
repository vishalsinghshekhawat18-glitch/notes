Commercial Banking: Structure, Functions & Balance Sheet

Operational foundations of commercial banks: core statutory functions under the Banking Regulation Act 1949, deposit typology (Demand, Savings, Current, Term/Fixed, Recurring), CASA ratio dynamics, credit facilities (Overdraft, Cash Credit, Term Loans), and fee-based agency/utility services.

Commercial Banking: Structure, Functions & Balance Sheet · Concept 1 of 2

Commercial Bank Functions: Statutory Definition, Deposit Typology, CASA Ratio & Credit Facilities

The foundational operational model of commercial banking under Section 5(b) and Section 6 of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949: accepting deposits repayable on demand or otherwise and lending/investing. Examines the full deposit hierarchy (Current Account, Savings Account, Term/Fixed Deposits, Recurring Deposits), the strategic significance of the CASA ratio in determining Net Interest Margin (NIM), credit product structures (Cash Credit, Overdraft, Term Loans, Letters of Credit, Bank Guarantees), and agency/utility services.

Intuition & Context

The Dual Engine: Deposit Mobilization and Credit Allocation

A commercial bank is a financial business operating on a spread. It sources raw material (money) from depositors at a low cost of interest, packages and monitors that money, and sells it to borrowers as loans at a higher rate of interest.

The difference between the interest earned on loans/investments and the interest paid on deposits is the bank's Net Interest Income (NII). To maximize this spread without taking reckless risks, banks focus on mobilizing cheap, sticky deposits (Current and Savings Accounts / CASA) and deploying them into well-collateralized, high-yielding loan products.

Core Principle

Deposit Typology & The Strategic Architecture of CASA

Commercial bank deposit liabilities are bifurcated into Demand Deposits and Time/Term Deposits:

1. Deposit Product Hierarchy

  • Demand Deposits (Withdrawable at any time without prior notice):
    • Current Account: Maintained by businesses and corporates for high-frequency transactions. Earns 0%0%0% interest (RBI prohibits interest on current accounts, with narrow exemptions).
    • Savings Account: Maintained by households for savings and daily retail payments. Earns low nominal interest (e.g., 2.5%−3.5%p.a.2.5% - 3.5% p.a.2.5%−3.5%p.a.).
  • Term / Time Deposits (Withdrawable after a contracted maturity period):
    • Fixed Deposit (FD): Lump-sum deposit locked for a fixed duration (7 days to 10 years). Earns higher interest (e.g., 6.5%−7.5%p.a.6.5% - 7.5% p.a.6.5%−7.5%p.a.). Premature withdrawal incurs penal charges.
    • Recurring Deposit (RD): Fixed regular monthly installments deposited over a specified tenure.

2. The CASA Ratio Metric

CASA Ratio=(Current Account Deposits+Savings Account DepositsTotal Deposits)×100\text{CASA Ratio} = \left(\frac{\text{Current Account Deposits} + \text{Savings Account Deposits}}{\text{Total Deposits}}\right) \times 100CASA Ratio=(Total DepositsCurrent Account Deposits+Savings Account Deposits​)×100

  • Why CASA Matters: CASA deposits are low-cost/zero-cost liabilities. A bank with a 45%45%45% CASA ratio has a far lower Cost of Funds (CoF) than a bank with a 20%20%20% CASA ratio relying on expensive wholesale term deposits. This structural advantage drives higher Net Interest Margins (NIM):

NIM=Interest Income−Interest ExpendedTotal Average Earning Assets\text{NIM} = \frac{\text{Interest Income} - \text{Interest Expended}}{\text{Total Average Earning Assets}}NIM=Total Average Earning AssetsInterest Income−Interest Expended​

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Credit Facility Structures: Funded vs Non-Funded Advances

Bank lending is structured into two fundamental categories:

                      COMMERCIAL BANK CREDIT FACILITIES
                                      │
       ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                             ▼
FUNDED FACILITIES (Cash Outflow)                             NON-FUNDED FACILITIES (Contingent)
• Cash Credit (CC): Working capital against stock/debtors    • Letter of Credit (LC): Importer/Exporter guarantee
• Overdraft (OD): Drawing beyond current account balance     • Bank Guarantee (BG): Performance/Financial guarantee
• Term Loans: Fixed repayment schedule for capital assets    • Co-acceptance of Bills: Bill discounting backing
• Retail Loans: Housing, auto, personal, education loans     • Yield: Fee / Commission income (No immediate cash)

Funded vs Non-Funded Mechanics

  1. Cash Credit (CC) & Overdraft (OD): Running credit limits where the borrower is charged interest only on the actual amount drawn on a daily basis, not on the entire sanctioned limit.
  2. Term Loans: The full sanctioned amount is disbursed upfront or in scheduled tranches. Interest is charged on the entire outstanding loan balance.
  3. Letters of Credit (LC): A documentary commitment issued by a bank on behalf of a buyer, assuring the seller that payment will be made provided shipping documents conform to terms. If the buyer pays, the bank disburses zero cash and pockets a fee. If the buyer defaults, the LC "devolves" into a funded liability of the bank.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Statutory Definition of Banking: Section 5(b) of the Banking Regulation Act requires BOTH accepting deposits AND lending/investing. An institution that only lends (like an NBFC) or only accepts deposits without lending (like a pure custodian) is not a bank under Section 5(b).
  • Trap 2: Interest on Current Accounts: Under RBI regulations, commercial banks are strictly prohibited from paying interest on Current Accounts (subject to specific exemptions like RRB balances or deceased depositor claims).
  • Trap 3: Funded vs Non-Funded: LCs and Bank Guarantees are non-funded facilities that appear as Contingent Liabilities (Off-Balance Sheet items), generating fee income rather than interest income.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • IIBF DBF & SBI/IBPS PO: Mastery of deposit rules, CASA ratio computation, cash credit vs overdraft, and off-balance sheet items.
  • RBI Grade B & RPSC RAS: Statutory definition under Section 5(b) BR Act, Net Interest Margin (NIM) dynamics, and cost of funds.

Commercial Bank Functional Architecture:

  1. Statutory Definition: Section 5(b) BR Act 1949.
  2. Permitted Businesses: Section 6 BR Act 1949 (Borrowing, lending, bill discounting, guarantees, safe custody, foreign exchange).
  3. Deposit Framework:
    • Demand Deposits: Current Account (zero interest, high turnover), Savings Account (moderate interest, transaction caps).
    • Time Deposits: Fixed Deposits (tenure 7 days to 10 years), Recurring Deposits (monthly SIP into term deposit).

  4. Credit Advances Framework:
    • Funded Advances: Cash Credit (against stock/debtors), Overdraft (against current account), Term Loans (capital investment).

The Commercial Bank Balance Sheet: Assets, Liabilities, Net Worth & Asset-Liability Management (ALM)

The first-principles financial accounting and risk structure of a commercial bank balance sheet under Form A (Third Schedule of Banking Regulation Act, 1949): the fundamental distinction between Bank Liabilities (Deposits, Borrowings, Capital & Reserves) and Bank Assets (Cash & RBI Balances, Money at Call, Investments in G-Secs/Bonds, Advances/Loans, Fixed Assets). Analyzes the mechanics of Asset-Liability Management (ALM), structural liquidity gaps, and interest rate sensitivity.

Intuition & Context

The Inverted Balance Sheet: Why What You Own Is What The Bank Owes

To understand bank accounting, you must flip your everyday perspective upside down.

When you check your banking app and see ₹50,000 in your savings account, that ₹50,000 is your asset. But to the bank, that ₹50,000 is a Liability—an obligation the bank owes to you and must repay upon your demand.

Conversely, if you take a ₹25,00,000 home loan, that loan is your liability (debt you owe), but it is the bank's primary Earning Asset—a legally enforceable contract entitling the bank to 20 years of monthly interest payments.

Core Principle

The Anatomy of a Bank Balance Sheet (Third Schedule Form A)

The Fundamental Balance Sheet Equation

Total Assets=Total Liabilities+Bank Capital (Equity)\text{Total Assets} = \text{Total Liabilities} + \text{Bank Capital (Equity)}Total Assets=Total Liabilities+Bank Capital (Equity)
Bank Capital (Net Worth)=Total Assets−Total Liabilities\text{Bank Capital (Net Worth)} = \text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}Bank Capital (Net Worth)=Total Assets−Total Liabilities
Bank Capital functions as the shock-absorbing equity cushion protecting depositors from loan default losses.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Asset-Liability Management (ALM) & Structural Maturity Gaps

Because bank assets (long-term loans) and liabilities (short-term deposits) have different maturities and repricing dates, banks face two primary balance sheet risks:

1. Structural Liquidity Gap Analysis

Banks slot all cash inflows (maturing assets) and cash outflows (maturing liabilities) into standardized time buckets (e.g., Next Day, 2–7 Days, 8–14 Days, 15–30 Days, 1–3 Months, 3–6 Months, 6–12 Months, 1–3 Years, 3–5 Years, Over 5 Years):
Maturity Gapt=Maturing Assetst−Maturing Liabilitiest\text{Maturity Gap}_t = \text{Maturing Assets}_t - \text{Maturing Liabilities}_tMaturity Gapt​=Maturing Assetst​−Maturing Liabilitiest​

  • Negative Gap (Deficit): Maturing liabilities exceed maturing assets. Bank must scramble to borrow fresh short-term funds or refinance.
  • Regulatory Prudential Limits: RBI mandates strict tolerance limits on negative mismatches (e.g., maximum 20%20%20% cumulative mismatch in the 1–14 day bucket).

2. Interest Rate Risk: Net Interest Income (NII) Sensitivity

  • Asset-Sensitive (Positive Gap): Rate-sensitive assets (RSARSARSA) exceed rate-sensitive liabilities (RSLRSLRSL). A policy rate hike increases NIM.
  • Liability-Sensitive (Negative Gap): RSL>RSARSL > RSARSL>RSA. A policy rate hike squeezes NIM because deposit costs rise faster than loan yields.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Deposits as Assets vs Liabilities: The most common beginner trap is thinking bank deposits are assets. Deposits are strictly LIABILITIES of the bank; Advances/Loans are ASSETS.
  • Trap 2: Cash with RBI (CRR): Cash balances maintained with RBI to satisfy CRR are classified under Schedule 6 (Cash and Balances with RBI) as an asset of the commercial bank.
  • Trap 3: Schedule 12 (Contingent Liabilities): Letters of Credit (LCs) and Bank Guarantees (BGs) do not appear on the main balance sheet; they are listed separately under Schedule 12 (Contingent Liabilities) as off-balance sheet obligations.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • IIBF DBF & RBI Grade B: Mastery of Schedules 1 to 12, ALM time buckets, maturity mismatch limits, and NII interest rate sensitivity.
  • SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Distinguishing bank assets from liabilities, role of bank capital as a loss buffer, and contingent liabilities.
