The Gandhian Mass Movements (1917–1942) · Concept 1 of 4

The Advent of Mahatma Gandhi: South African Laboratory, Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918), Kheda (1918) & Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)

The entry and crystallization of Gandhian mass satyagraha technique in India: Return from South Africa (Jan 9, 1915 - Pravasi Bharatiya Divas), Gopal Krishna Gokhale as political guru, Sabarmati Ashram; Champaran Satyagraha (1917, Bihar, first Civil Disobedience, Rajkumar Shukla, Tinkathia 3/20th system of indigo cultivation); Ahmedabad Mill Strike (March 1918, first Hunger Strike, 35% wage bonus demand for plague allowance); Kheda Satyagraha (1918, Gujarat, first Non-Cooperation, crop failure remission, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel); Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act 1919, "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"); Rowlatt Satyagraha; and the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919, General Dyer, Hunter Commission, Rabindranath Tagore renouncing Knighthood).

Intuition & Context

The South African Crucible & The Awakening of a Moral Titan

How did an introspective, London-trained barrister transform into the indisputable mass leader of the largest anti-colonial struggle in human history?

Mahatma Gandhi did not arrive in India in 1915 as an untested political philosopher. He arrived having completed a twenty-year laboratory experiment in South Africa (1893–1914):

  • In South Africa, confronting severe racial discrimination against Indian indentured labourers and merchants (poll tax, Asiatic Registration Act, invalidation of non-Christian marriages), Gandhi forged the radical technique of Satyagraha (Satya = Truth, Agraha = Firm adherence / Soul-force).
  • Influenced by Leo Tolstoy (The Kingdom of God is Within You), John Ruskin (Unto This Last), and Henry David Thoreau (Civil Disobedience), Gandhi rejected both the "polite mendicancy" of the Indian Moderates and the "individual terror" of the Revolutionaries.
  • On the advice of his political guru, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Gandhi spent his first two years in India (1915–1916) travelling in third-class train compartments across the subcontinent with his "ears open and mouth shut", absorbing the lived agony of ordinary peasants. When he finally struck, he intervened not on lofty constitutional abstractions, but on immediate, localized, visceral economic oppressions.

Sparked massive nationwide demonstrations; led to widespread British repression and the catastrophic Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

2. The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919)

  • Immediate Context: Arrest of popular nationalist leaders Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal in Amritsar under the Rowlatt Act on April 10, 1919, leading to local riots.
  • The Atrocity (April 13, 1919 - Baisakhi Day):
    • A peaceful gathering of 20,000 men, women, and children gathered in the walled enclosure of Jallianwala Bagh to protest the arrests and celebrate Baisakhi.
    • Acting Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer marched in with Gurkha and Baluchi riflemen, sealed the only narrow exit, and without giving any warning, ordered his troops to fire 1,650 rounds into the trapped crowd until ammunition was exhausted.
    • Officially 379 dead; independent estimates by the Congress inquiry committee established over 1,000 dead.
  • National Repercussions:
    • Rabindranath Tagore renounced his British Knighthood ("The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in the incongruous context of humiliation...").
    • Gandhi renounced his Kaiser-i-Hind gold medal and called off the Rowlatt Satyagraha on April 18, 1919, describing launching mass civil disobedience with an untrained populace as a "Himalayan Blunder".
    • Hunter Commission (Disorders Inquiry Committee): British official inquiry; Dyer was censured and removed from command but exonerated of murder and hailed as the "Savior of the Punjab" by the British House of Lords and British public (who presented him a purse of £26,000 and a jewelled sword).
    • Michael O'Dwyer: The Lieutenant Governor of Punjab who endorsed Dyer's firing was later assassinated on March 13, 1940 at Caxton Hall, London by revolutionary Udham Singh (Shaheed Ram Mohammad Singh Azad).
Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Gandhian Triad: Satya, Ahimsa and the Moral Subversion of Colonialism

The Mechanics of Mass Conversion via Suffering

Gandhian Satyagraha operated on a psychological mechanism that paralyzed the British colonial apparatus:

  1. The Tactical Disarmament of Imperial Coercion:
    Colonial Hegemony=Physical Brutality×Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")\text{Colonial Hegemony} = \text{Physical Brutality} \times \text{Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")}Colonial Hegemony=Physical Brutality×Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")

    • When an empire uses batons and bullets against armed rebels, it easily justifies itself as the protector of public order.
    • But when British police beat unarmed, non-violent, praying men and women who refuse to retaliate or flee, the moral justification for empire shatters in the eyes of world opinion and the British electorate itself.

  2. The Technique of the 25% Refund at Champaran:

    • Why did Gandhi agree to a mere 25% refund of the illegally extorted money from the Champaran planters, when the ryots demanded 100%?
    • Gandhi explained: The monetary amount was secondary; what mattered was that the European planters—who had behaved as hereditary feudal lords above the law—were forced to surrender their prestige, status, and legal invulnerability. Within a decade, all European planters packed up and abandoned Champaran forever.

Exam Application & Traps

High-Frequency Traps, Chronological Sequences & Examiner Pitfalls

⚠️ Top 5 High-Frequency Traps for UPSC & State PCS

  1. Chronology of 1918 (The Invariable Prelims Trap):

    • Trap: Thinking Kheda happened before Ahmedabad.
    • Fact: Ahmedabad Mill Strike occurred in March 1918, whereas Kheda Satyagraha occurred from March to June 1918. Remember the sequence: CAKE (Champaran 1917 →\to→ Ahmedabad 1918 →\to→ Kheda 1918).

  2. The "Firsts" in India Classification:

    • First Civil Disobedience (Savinay Avagya): Champaran (1917)
    • First Hunger Strike (Bhookh Hartal): Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918)
    • First Non-Cooperation (Asahyog): Kheda Satyagraha (1918)
    • First Pan-Indian Mass Satyagraha: Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)

  3. Dyer vs O'Dwyer Confusion:

    • Reginald Dyer: Acting Brigadier-General who physically ordered the firing at Jallianwala Bagh on April 13, 1919.
    • Michael O'Dwyer: Lieutenant Governor of Punjab who approved the action and imposed martial law; he was shot dead in London in 1940 by Udham Singh.

  4. Hunter Commission Distinction:

    • Trap: Confusing the 1882 Hunter Commission with the 1919 Hunter Committee.
    • Fact: The 1882 Hunter Commission (chaired by William Wilson Hunter) investigated Indian Education. The 1919 Hunter Committee (Disorders Inquiry Committee, chaired by Lord William Hunter) investigated the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

  5. Title Renunciations:

    • Rabindranath Tagore: Renounced his Knighthood.
    • Mahatma Gandhi: Renounced his Kaiser-i-Hind medal (awarded for ambulance services in the Boer War).

The Gandhian Mass Movements (1917–1942) · Concept 2 of 4

The First Pan-Indian Mass Upsurge: Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922), The Khilafat Alliance & Chauri Chaura

The unprecedented forging of Hindu-Muslim unity and mass mobilization against colonial rule: The Khilafat Question (dismemberment of Ottoman Caliphate by Treaty of Sevres, Ali Brothers - Shaukat Ali and Mohammad Ali, Maulana Azad); Special Calcutta Session (Sept 1920) and Nagpur Session (Dec 1920, C. Vijayaraghavachariar president: Congress goal transformed to Swaraj through peaceful means, linguistic provincial congress committees, ₹1 crore Tilak Swaraj Fund); Programme of Non-Cooperation (surrender of titles, boycott of government schools, law courts, foreign cloth, picketing of liquor shops); massive participation of students, women, and peasants (Eka and Awadh Kisan Sabhas); Baba Ramchandra; and the abrupt withdrawal of the movement following the Chauri Chaura incident (Feb 5, 1922, 22 policemen burnt alive in Gorakhpur), leading to the Bardoli Resolution and Gandhi's imprisonment.

Intuition & Context

The Convergence of Two Torrents: Unprecedented Hindu-Muslim Solidarity

How did Mahatma Gandhi transform the Indian National Congress from an elite, English-speaking debating society into a roaring, pan-Indian mass movement that terrified the British Empire in 1920?

The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922) was made possible by the confluence of three distinct rivers of anti-colonial fury:

  1. The Punjab Wrongs: Nationwide outrage over the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the whitewashing Hunter Commission report.
  2. The Constitutional Betrayal: Deep dissatisfaction with the hollow Government of India Act 1919 and post-war economic misery (hyper-inflation, plague, crop failure).
  3. The Khilafat Question: The humiliation of the Ottoman Sultan (who was revered as the Khalifa or spiritual head of Sunni Muslims worldwide) following the dismemberment of Turkey by the Allies through the Treaty of Sèvres (1920).

Gandhi recognized this as a once-in-a-century historical opportunity to fuse Hindu and Muslim aspirations into an indomitable anti-imperialist front: "An opportunity of uniting Hindus and Muslims as would not arise in a hundred years". By bringing the entire organizational weight of the Congress behind the Khilafat Committee, Gandhi forged the first truly pan-Indian mass upsurge across religion, region, and class.

Core Principle

The Architecture of Non-Cooperation: From Nagpur Resolutions to Bardoli Retreat

1. The Twin Congress Sessions of 1920

  • Special Calcutta Session (September 1920):
    • Presided over by Lala Lajpat Rai.
    • Gandhi moved the historic Non-Cooperation resolution. Despite initial opposition from stalwarts like C.R. Das, Bipin Chandra Pal, and Annie Besant (who feared council boycotts and radicalization), the resolution was passed with an overwhelming majority.
  • Regular Nagpur Session (December 1920) — The Watershed Moment:
    • Presided over by C. Vijayaraghavachariar.
    • C.R. Das dramatically dropped his opposition and himself moved the main Non-Cooperation resolution!
    • Radical Structural Transformation of Congress:
      1. Goal Redefined: The objective of the Congress was altered from self-government within the British Empire through constitutional means to "the attainment of Swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means".
      2. Working Committee Established: Created a 15-member Congress Working Committee (CWC) to act as a permanent, year-round executive steering body.
      3. Linguistic Provincial Committees: Reorganized Provincial Congress Committees (PCCs) on a linguistic basis (demolishing colonial administrative boundaries and taking politics to the regional vernacular).
      4. Mass Membership: Reduced annual membership fee to 4 annas (25 paise), opening Congress doors to peasants, artisans, and industrial workers.
      5. Tilak Swaraj Fund: Announced following the demise of Bal Gangadhar Tilak (died August 1, 1920, the day NCM formally began); aimed to collect ₹1 Crore to finance the movement, which was oversubscribed within months!

2. The Comprehensive Programme: Boycott & Constructive Work

3. Regional Outbursts & Subaltern Mobilization

The Non-Cooperation Movement was not merely an urban middle-class protest; it triggered volcanic subaltern peasant uprisings across the country:

  • Awadh Peasant Movement (UP): Led by Baba Ramchandra (a wandering sanyasi who carried the Ramcharitmanas); organized peasants against illegal cesses (Nazrana) and evictions (Bedakhali). Awadh Kisan Sabha formed in Oct 1920 with Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Eka Movement (1921, UP): Led by Madari Pasi in Hardoi, Bahraich, and Sitapur; peasants pledged to pay only recorded rents and refuse forced labour (Begar).
  • Mappila (Moplah) Rebellion (1921, Malabar, Kerala): Muslim tenant cultivators rose in armed rebellion against oppressive upper-caste Hindu landlords (Janmis) and British police. Initially aligned with Khilafat, it unfortunately acquired tragic communal overtones.
  • Assam & Bengal Strikes: Massive tea-garden coolie walkouts in Assam and the legendary strike on the Assam-Bengal Railway and river steamer services organized under the leadership of J.M. Sengupta.
  • Akali Movement (Punjab): Peaceful agitation to liberate historical Sikh Gurudwaras from corrupt, pro-British hereditary priests (Mahants), leading to the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925.

4. Chauri Chaura & The Abrupt Retreat (February 1922)

  • The Incident (February 4/5, 1922):
    • At Chauri Chaura, a village in Gorakhpur district, United Provinces (UP), police fired upon a peaceful procession of Congress-Khilafat volunteers protesting liquor sales and food prices.
    • An enraged crowd of peasants retaliated, chased the police into the police station, locked the doors, and set it ablaze. 22 policemen were burnt alive.
  • The Bardoli Resolution (February 12, 1922):
    • Horrified by the eruption of violence, Gandhi immediately summoned the Congress Working Committee at Bardoli and unilaterally called off the entire Non-Cooperation Movement.
    • Gandhi maintained that Indians were not yet spiritually trained in non-violence, and that allowing the movement to become violent would give the British armed state the moral and military justification to crush it with artillery.
  • National Shock and Division:
    • Nationalist leaders were devastated. Subhas Chandra Bose called the withdrawal a "national calamity". Jawaharlal Nehru and Motilal Nehru expressed deep dismay from prison.
    • Taking advantage of the retreat, the British government arrested Gandhi on March 10, 1922. In a historic trial, Judge C.N. Broomfield sentenced him to 6 years imprisonment.
    • The collapse of NCM fractured the Hindu-Muslim alliance, led to the disintegration of the Khilafat movement (especially after Mustafa Kemal Atatürk abolished the Ottoman Caliphate entirely in 1924), and spawned the formation of the Swaraj Party in 1923 by C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru.

The Sociological Mechanism of Non-Cooperation: Demystifying Colonial Invincibility

How Non-Cooperation Pierced the "Colonial Consent"

Colonial rule never depended solely on British bayonets; it rested upon the psychological cooperation and perceived indispensability of the Raj in the minds of the colonized:

Colonial Power=Military CoercionVoluntary Native Compliance\text{Colonial Power} = \frac{\text{Military Coercion}}{\text{Voluntary Native Compliance}}Colonial Power=Voluntary Native ComplianceMilitary Coercion​

  1. The Principle of Withdrawing Consent:

    • In Hind Swaraj (1909), Gandhi wrote: "The English have not taken India; we have given it to them. They are not in India because of their strength, but because we keep them. If we cease to cooperate, British rule will collapse in a year."
    • By refusing to attend courts, schools, council chambers, and revenue offices, Non-Cooperation demonstrated in practice that the British Raj was an illusion maintained by Indian collaboration.

  2. The Eradication of Fear (Nirbhayata):

    • As Jawaharlal Nehru remarked in The Discovery of India, the supreme gift of Gandhi in 1920 was fearlessness. Going to jail (Jail Bharo), previously considered a catastrophic social stigma, was transformed into an exalted badge of patriotic honour.

Exam Application & Traps

High-Frequency Traps, Chronological Sequences & Examiner Pitfalls

⚠️ Top 5 High-Frequency Traps for UPSC & State PCS

  1. The 1920 Sessions Presidency Trap:

    • Special Calcutta Session (Sept 1920): Presided over by Lala Lajpat Rai (passed NCM resolution).
    • Nagpur Regular Session (Dec 1920): Presided over by C. Vijayaraghavachariar (ratified NCM and transformed Congress constitution).
    • Trap: Thinking Gandhi or Motilal Nehru presided over the 1920 Nagpur session!

  2. The Chauri Chaura Date and Location:

    • Date: February 4/5, 1922 (UP state records observe Feb 4; historical texts cite Feb 5).
    • Location: Gorakhpur District, United Provinces (UP), NOT Bihar or Bengal.
    • Withdrawal Site: Formally called off by the CWC meeting at Bardoli (Gujarat) on February 12, 1922 (hence known as the Bardoli Resolution).

  3. Tilak's Role in NCM:

    • Trap: Believing Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak led or opposed the Non-Cooperation Movement in action.
    • Fact: Tilak passed away on August 1, 1920, the very morning the movement was formally launched. The Tilak Swaraj Fund was established in his honour.

  4. Peasant Leadership Attribution:

    • Awadh Kisan Sabha (1920): Led by Baba Ramchandra along with Jawaharlal Nehru.
    • Eka Movement (1921): Led by Madari Pasi in Hardoi, Bahraich, and Sitapur.

Intuition & Context

The Alchemy of Salt: Mobilizing a Subcontinent Through the Most Universal Commodity

Why did Mahatma Gandhi choose common salt—a chemical compound of sodium chloride—to launch the greatest challenge to the British Empire since 1857?

When the Congress Working Committee authorized Gandhi to launch Civil Disobedience following the historic Purna Swaraj declaration at Lahore (December 1929), many senior leaders like Motilal Nehru and Jawaharlal Nehru were deeply perplexed by Gandhi's obsession with the Salt Tax. Salt seemed trivial compared to sweeping demands for constitutional sovereignty or land revenue abolition.

Yet, Gandhi's choice was a masterstroke of political genius:

  1. Universal Biological Necessity: Salt was indispensable to every living human and domestic animal. The colonial government's state monopoly and tax on salt fell with regressive cruelty on the poorest of the poor, the landless peasant, and the urban labourer.
  2. Moral Clarity vs Legal Tyranny: By taxing a gift of nature freely crystallizing along thousands of miles of coastline, the British state exposed itself as an extortionist predator. Breaking the salt law carried zero moral ambiguity; it pitted human survival against imperial revenue greed.
  3. Inclusive Non-Violent Mobilization: Manufacturing illegal salt required no arms, no capital, and no foreign training. A handful of seawater boiled on an earthen stove turned every Indian peasant, villager, and homemaker into an active constitutional rebel against the King-Emperor.

From the 240-mile march to Dandi to the brutal lathi charges at Dharasana, and ultimately to the constitutional negotiations of the Round Table Conferences and the Yerwada fast that forged the Poona Pact (1932), this era redefined Indian nationhood, caste representation, and mass defiance.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Constitutional & Representation Mechanics: Separate vs Joint Electorates & Poona Pact Voting Formula

How the Poona Pact Altered Electoral Architecture

The intellectual and constitutional debate between Mahatma Gandhi and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar at Poona was one of the most consequential turning points in modern Indian jurisprudence:

  1. The Mechanism of Separate Electorates (The MacDonald Formula):

    • Under separate electorates, a legislative constituency is divided purely along communal/caste lines.
    • Only voters belonging to the Depressed Classes could vote to elect a Depressed Class candidate.
    • Gandhi argued that this would legally segregate untouchables from mainstream Hindu society forever, perpetuating social untouchability by giving it a permanent constitutional boundary.

  2. The Mechanism of Joint Electorates with Reservation (The Poona Pact Formula):

    • Under joint electorates, the entire electorate (caste Hindus, Depressed Classes, and others) votes together.
    • However, the seat is reserved exclusively for a candidate from the Depressed Classes.
    • The Primary Election Sub-system: To ensure genuine representation, the Poona Pact devised a two-tier electoral filter:
      Depressed Class Voters Only→Primary ElectionSelect a panel of 4 candidates→General Joint ElectorateFinal Winner\text{Depressed Class Voters Only} \xrightarrow{\text{Primary Election}} \text{Select a panel of 4 candidates} \xrightarrow{\text{General Joint Electorate}} \text{Final Winner}Depressed Class Voters OnlyPrimary Election​Select a panel of 4 candidatesGeneral Joint Electorate​Final Winner
    • The candidate securing the highest votes from the entire joint community in the final election was elected.
    • Quantitative Outcome:
      • Provincial Assembly seats for Depressed Classes doubled: from 71 under the Communal Award to 147 seats under the Poona Pact (e.g., Bengal 30, Madras 30, UP 20, Central Provinces 20, Bombay 15, Bihar/Orissa 18, Punjab 8, Assam 7).
      • Central Legislative Assembly allocation: 18% of the general seats were reserved for Depressed Classes.

    • Trap: Stating that Mahatma Gandhi signed the Poona Pact.

    • Presided over by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (NOT Jawaharlal Nehru).

The August Kranti: The Quit India Movement (1942), Parallel Governments & Total Mass Mobilization

The climactic final mass rebellion of the Gandhian era: Failure of the Cripps Mission (March 1942, "post-dated cheque on a crashing bank"); Japanese advance to the borders of Assam; All-India Congress Committee (AICC) meeting at Gowalia Tank Ground, Bombay; adoption of the Quit India Resolution (August 8, 1942); Gandhi's immortal call to the nation: **"Do or Die" (*Karo ya Maro*)**; Operation Zero Hour (midnight Aug 8–9 arrest of entire top Congress leadership including Gandhi, Nehru, Patel); spontaneous leaderless mass eruption across India; underground resistance network (Aruna Asaf Ali hoisting the flag at Gowalia Tank, Ram Manohar Lohia, Jayaprakash Narayan escaping Hazaribagh jail, Usha Mehta running Congress Secret Underground Radio); establishment of **Parallel Governments (*Prati Sarkar*)**: Ballia (Chittu Pandey, first parallel govt), Tamluk (Jatiya Sarkar in Midnapore, Bengal, Matangini Hazra martyrdom), and Satara (Nana Patil, longest running Prati Sarkar); and violent British state repression involving machine-gun strafing from aircraft.

Intuition & Context

The August Eruption: From Disciplined Satyagraha to Uncompromising Open Rebellion

In August 1942, Mahatma Gandhi took a radical, unprecedented departure from his earlier philosophies of cautious, incremental mass mobilization. In 1922, he had abruptly halted the Non-Cooperation Movement over the solitary death of 22 policemen at Chauri Chaura. In 1930, he meticulously scripted every step of the Salt Satyagraha to maintain non-violent discipline.

Why, then, in August 1942, did Gandhi proclaim to the nation: "Leave India to God. If that is too much, then leave her to anarchy... I am not going to be satisfied with anything short of complete freedom. Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you: Do or Die (Karo ya Maro). We shall either free India or die in the attempt"?

The catalysts for this historic transformation were urgent and existential:

  1. The Cripps Mission Debacle (March–April 1942): Sir Stafford Cripps brought an offer of post-war Dominion Status, constitutional provisions for provinces to secede, and zero immediate transfer of defence power. Gandhi famously dismissed it as a "post-dated cheque on a crashing bank". The failure proved British insincerity.
  2. The Looming Japanese Invasion: Imperial Japanese forces had swept through Southeast Asia, capturing Malaya, Singapore, and Burma (Rangoon fell in March 1942), reaching the borders of Assam. The British practiced a scorched-earth policy in coastal Bengal (confiscating country boats and rice supplies, paving the way for the horrific 1943 Bengal Famine). Gandhi recognized that British presence was an invitation for Japan to invade India.
  3. Total National Readiness: Popular frustration, war inflation, and soaring food shortages reached a boiling point. Gandhi understood that if the leadership did not channel this fury, the nation would sink into demoralized passive slavery.

When the British launched Operation Zero Hour before dawn on August 9, arresting the entire Congress Working Committee, they assumed the movement would die in the womb. Instead, without leaders, without instructions, and without a central high command, 300 million people rose in the fiercest open rebellion the British Raj had faced since 1857.

Core Principle

The August Kranti Matrix: Cripps Failure, Underground Networks & Parallel Governments

1. Key Triggers & The Gowalia Tank Declaration

  • Wardha Resolution (July 14, 1942): The Congress Working Committee met at Wardha and authorized Gandhi to take charge of a non-violent mass movement.
  • AICC Gowalia Tank Meeting (August 7–8, 1942): The All-India Congress Committee met at Gowalia Tank Ground (now August Kranti Maidan) in Bombay.
    • Quit India Resolution: Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru and seconded by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel. Passed on August 8, 1942.
    • The Call to Action: Gandhi delivered his historic 2-hour speech proclaiming "Karo ya Maro" (Do or Die). Specific instructions were issued:
      1. Government servants: Do not resign, but declare your allegiance to the Congress.
      2. Soldiers: Do not leave the army, but refuse to fire on your fellow countrymen.
      3. Students: Give up studies if you can maintain firmness until independence.
      4. Peasants: If zamindars are pro-government, pay no rent; if anti-government, pay agreed mutual rent.
      5. Princely States rulers: Accept the sovereignty of your own people, not the Paramount Power.
  • Operation Zero Hour (Pre-dawn, August 9, 1942):
    • British police arrested Gandhi (imprisoned at Aga Khan Palace, Poona alongside Kasturba Gandhi and Mahadev Desai, both of whom died there).
    • The entire Congress Working Committee (Nehru, Patel, Azad, Rajendra Prasad) was arrested and locked in Ahmednagar Fort and Bankipore jail (Patna).
    • Congress was declared an unlawful association, its funds frozen and offices seized.

4. Spectrum of Opposition to Quit India (UPSC High-Frequency Focus)

Not all political organizations supported the 1942 August rebellion:

  • The Hindu Mahasabha (V.D. Savarkar): Boycotted the movement, ordering its members to stay at their administrative posts and join the armed forces to get military training.
  • The Communist Party of India (CPI): Boycotted the movement. Following Nazi Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa, June 1941), the CPI changed its characterization of WW2 from an "Imperialist War" to a "People's War", aligning with the British-Soviet Allied war effort.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar: Opposed the movement, criticizing it as untimely, irresponsible, and detrimental to national defence; served as Labour Member on the Viceroy's Executive Council.
  • C. Rajagopalachari: Resigned from the Congress Working Committee because he opposed launching a mass campaign during wartime and favoured accepting the League's demand for Pakistan to build a united front.

[Phase 2: Rural Guerrilla War & Parallel Govts (Late Aug–End 1942)]
       │ (Destruction of railways, telegraph lines, post offices; Ballia, Tamluk, Satara)
       ▼
[Phase 3: Underground Cell Resistance & Sabotage (1943–1944)]
         (Secret Congress Radio, Azad Dasta, Jayaprakash Narayan, Lohia)

  1. Phase 1: Urban Mass Eruption (First few weeks of August 1942):

    • Spontaneous general strikes (hartals), street battles, and student demonstrations in Bombay, Delhi, Calcutta, Patna, and Ahmedabad.
    • Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) workers at Jamshedpur went on a 13-day patriotic strike refusing to produce steel for the British war effort. Textile mills in Ahmedabad remained shut for over 3 months ("Ahmedabad Stalingrad").

  2. Phase 2: Peasant Guerrilla War & Sabotage of Infrastructure (Mid-August to late 1942):

    • Movement shifted to rural hinterlands, particularly in Eastern UP, Bihar, Midnapore, and Coastal Orissa.
    • Systematic destruction of the apparatus of British colonial communication: over 250 railway stations destroyed or damaged, 500 post offices attacked, 150 police stations burned, and thousands of telephone/telegraph lines severed.
    • Bihar became the storm centre; British authority evaporated across entire districts (Azamgarh, Ballia, Ghazipur, Munger, Darbhanga).

  3. Phase 3: Underground Resistance (1943 to 1944):

    • Organized by sophisticated underground networks supplying funds, bombs, detonators, and underground literature.
    • British state deployed unprecedented terror: aircraft machine-gunned crowds from the air (in Patna, Bhagalpur, Nadia, Talcher, and Gorakhpur); collective fines exceeding ₹90 Lakh were extorted from villages; public whipping was legalized; over 10,000 freedom fighters were killed or executed.
    • Gandhi's 21-Day Fast (Feb 1943): When Viceroy Linlithgow demanded that Gandhi condemn the mass violence, Gandhi turned the moral tables, refusing to condemn popular fury and laying the entire guilt on the "leonine violence" of the British state.

Spatial & Strategic Dynamics:

Exam Application & Traps

High-Frequency Examiner Traps & Historiographical Insights

Traps Frequently Tested in Competitive Examinations

  1. The Slogan Authorship Trap (#1 UPSC/State PSC Trap!):

    • Trap: Believing Mahatma Gandhi coined the phrase "Quit India".
    • Truth: The slogan "Quit India" was coined by Yusuf Meherally, an Indian socialist and trade union leader who was then Mayor of Bombay! (He also coined the immortal slogan "Simon Go Back" in 1928). Gandhi enthusiastically approved and adopted the phrase.

  2. The Matangini Hazra Martyrdom Trap:

    • Matangini Hazra (known affectionately as Gandhi Buri or "Old Lady Gandhi", aged 73) was martyred on September 29, 1942 during the march on the Tamluk police station in Midnapore, Bengal. She was shot three times by British police while chanting Vande Mataram and holding the Indian flag high.

  3. Parties Opposing Quit India:

    • If an exam question asks which organizations supported Quit India, remember that Muslim League, Hindu Mahasabha, Communist Party of India, and Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) did NOT participate.
    • The movement was predominantly driven by the Congress rank-and-file, Congress Socialists (CSP), peasants, students, and independent nationalist masses.

  4. Longest vs Shortest Parallel Governments:

    • Shortest: Ballia (Chittu Pandey, barely 7 to 10 days).
    • Longest: Satara Prati Sarkar (Nana Patil & Y.B. Chavan, lasted nearly 3 years, from mid-1943 until 1945–46).

🏛️ 5-Minute Architecture & Key Revision

Structural Dynamics of the 1942 August Revolution: 1) Radical Strategic Rupture: Unlike 1920 and 1930, Gandhi did not make absolute non-violence a pre-condition; he placed primary moral responsibility for violence on the British state ("leonine violence"). 2) Leaderless Insurgency: The immediate arrest of the central high command decentralized operational initiative to local peasant cadres and socialist youth (CSP). 3) Decisive Destruction of Imperial Invincibility: Although ruthlessly crushed by military force (including aircraft strafing and collective fines), Quit India demonstrated to the British war cabinet that rule by consent was permanently extinguished, rendering British departure inevitable once the war concluded.
