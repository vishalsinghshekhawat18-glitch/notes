Direct Taxation in India: Income Tax (New vs Old Regime), Corporate Tax Reforms & Capital Gains Architecture

The statutory framework, rate structures, and economic mechanisms of Direct Taxation in India administered by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) under the Income Tax Act, 1961. Analyzes Personal Income Tax under the default New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC) vs Old Regime, Corporate Tax reforms (Taxation Laws Amendment Act 2019: Section 115BAA 22% base rate for existing domestic firms, Section 115BAB 15% for new manufacturing companies), and Capital Gains taxation (bifurcation into Short-Term / STCG and Long-Term / LTCG across listed equity, debt, and real estate).

Intuition & Context

Direct Taxes: Taxing the Flow of Wealth at Its Source

Why does the government prefer collecting direct taxes?

Because direct taxes are the ultimate tool for social equity. If a society only has sales taxes (GST), a daily wage worker and a multi-millionaire pay the exact same tax when buying a mobile phone.

Direct taxes allow the state to look at a citizen's or corporation's net balance sheet: those who earn more pay progressively higher rates, while the lowest earners pay zero tax. Furthermore, corporate income tax ensures that profits generated using national infrastructure, public resources, and judicial protections contribute directly to the public treasury.

Core Principle

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Default Tax Regime: The New Tax Regime under Section 115BAC is the DEFAULT regime in India. If a taxpayer does not actively opt into the Old Regime, the New Regime applies automatically.
  • Trap 2: Section 87A Rebate Threshold: Under the New Regime, income up to ₹7,00,000 pays ZERO tax due to the Section 87A full tax rebate.
  • Trap 3: Corporate Tax Rate Under Section 115BAA: The base rate is 22%22%22%, but with mandatory 10%10%10% surcharge and 4%4%4% Health & Education cess, the effective corporate tax rate is $25.17%$.
  • Trap 4: Capital Gains Rationalization (2024): Listed equity STCG is 20%20%20% (raised from 15%15%15%); LTCG is $12.5%$ (raised from 10%10%10%) with an exemption limit of ₹1,25,000 per year (raised from ₹1,00,000).

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Corporate tax reform sections (115BAA/115BAB), Section 115BAC slabs, capital gains rationalization, and direct tax-to-GDP ratio.
  • SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Income tax basic exemption (₹3 Lakh), Section 87A zero tax limit (₹7 Lakh), standard deduction (₹75,000), and CBDT leadership.

Constitutional Distribution of Taxation Powers: 7th Schedule Lists & Levying vs Collecting vs Devolution Framework

The constitutional architecture governing taxation rights in India under Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule. Analyzes the strict assignment of tax bases between the Union List (List I: Corporation tax, Income tax other than agricultural income, Customs, Union Excise on petroleum/tobacco), State List (List II: Land revenue, Stamp duty on property, State excise on alcoholic liquor, Motor vehicles tax, Electricity duty), and the absence of overlapping indirect taxes post-101st Constitutional Amendment (Article 246A special concurrent power for GST). Covers the constitutional taxonomy of Articles 268, 269, 269A, and 270 (The Divisible Pool).

Intuition & Context

Who Has the Power to Tax What? The Federal Balance

Imagine if both the Central Government and your State Government simultaneously demanded a 30%30%30% income tax on your salary, and then your City Municipal Corporation demanded another 20%20%20%. You would have only 20%20%20% of your income left!

To prevent this destructive chaos, the framers of the Indian Constitution designed a Strict Division of Tax Powers in the Seventh Schedule:

  • The Centre was given the broad, mobile, national tax bases (Corporate Tax, Customs, Personal Income Tax) to ensure uniformity.
  • The States were given local, location-specific tax bases (Land revenue, Stamp duties on real estate, State excise on liquor, Electricity duty).

Because the Centre collects far more revenue than it needs for Union subjects while States spend far more on public health, education, and policing, the Constitution created a Tax Devolution Bridge (Article 280 Finance Commission) to transfer revenue back to the States.

Constitutional Taxonomy: Articles 268 to 271

Examiners frequently test the constitutional classification of Union-State tax flows:

Article Constitutional Rule Current Practical Example
Article 265 No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law Executive notifications cannot create taxes without statutory backing
Article 268 Levied by Union, Collected and Retained by States Stamp duties on promissory notes, bills of exchange, and transfer of shares
Article 269 Levied and Collected by Union, but Assigned 100% to States Taxes on consignment of goods in inter-state trade
Article 269A IGST on Inter-State Supply: Levied and Collected by Union, Apportioned between Union and States Integrated GST (IGST) split 50:50 based on destination state
Article 270 The Divisible Pool: Levied and Collected by Union, Distributed between Union and States Income Tax, Corporate Tax, CGST, Customs (Shared via Finance Commission: 41%41%41%)
Article 271 Surcharges and Cesses for Union Purposes Health & Education Cess, Agriculture Infrastructure Cess (100% retained by Centre)
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Agricultural Income Taxation: The Central Government CANNOT tax agricultural income (barred by Entry 82 List I). Only State Governments have the constitutional power under Entry 46 List II (though most states exempt it).
  • Trap 2: Article 246A is NOT in Concurrent List: Article 246A is a standalone sui generis constitutional provision giving simultaneous power. GST is NOT an entry in List III (Concurrent List)!
  • Trap 3: Cesses and Surcharges (Article 271): Cesses and surcharges are EXCLUDED from the Divisible Pool of Article 270, meaning the Union retains 100% of cess collections.
  • Trap 4: Alcoholic Liquor for Human Consumption: States retain exclusive taxation power over alcohol under Entry 51 List II (State Excise) and Entry 54 List II (State VAT); it was strictly excluded from GST.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & UPSC APFC: Articles 268/269/269A/270/271 taxonomy, Article 246A simultaneous power, and 80th Constitutional Amendment (Alternative Devolution Scheme).
  • RPSC RAS & SBI/IBPS PO: 2-mark definitions of Article 265, Article 270 Divisible Pool, and Entry 82 vs Entry 46 agricultural tax division.
