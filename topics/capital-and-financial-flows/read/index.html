Capital Flows Typology: FDI vs FPI, External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) & Hot Money Dynamics

The institutional taxonomy and macroeconomic dynamics of cross-border capital flows in the Balance of Payments Financial Account. Distinguishes Foreign Direct Investment (FDI: long-term, $\\ge 10\\%$ equity, managerial control, technology transfer, illiquid) from Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI: short-term, $< 10\\%$ equity in listed securities, highly liquid, "hot money"). Analyzes External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs), NRI deposit schemes (NRE, NRO, FCNR-B), banking capital, and the financial stability risks associated with sudden capital stops and capital flight.

Intuition & Context

Building a Factory vs Buying Shares on a Smartphone

Consider two foreign investors putting money into India:

  1. Investor A (FDI): Japanese auto giant Suzuki invests ₹10,000 Crore to build a mega car manufacturing plant in Gujarat. Suzuki hires 5,000 workers, trains engineers, and builds physical machinery. Can Suzuki press a button on a smartphone and pull its ₹10,000 Crore out of India tomorrow morning? No. The factory is physically bolted to Indian soil. This is Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): stable, non-debt creating, and long-term.
  2. Investor B (FPI): A hedge fund in New York buys ₹100 Crore worth of shares in Indian IT companies on the National Stock Exchange. Three weeks later, the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates. The hedge fund clicks "Sell" on its terminal, converts Rupees to US Dollars, and leaves India in 30 seconds. This is Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI / "Hot Money"): highly liquid, volatile, and reversible.

Developing nations welcome FPI for stock market liquidity, but rely on FDI for enduring industrial growth.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

NRI Deposit Schemes & External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs)

Beyond equity flows, India's Capital Account contains two critical debt channels:

1. Non-Resident Indian (NRI) Deposit Schemes

  • Non-Resident External (NRE) Account: Rupee-denominated account. Fully repatriable (both principal and interest). Exchange rate risk borne by the NRI depositor.
  • Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) [FCNR-B] Account: Denominated in designated foreign currencies (USD, GBP, EUR, JPY). Fully repatriable. Exchange rate risk borne by the Indian commercial bank.
  • Non-Resident Ordinary (NRO) Account: Rupee-denominated for managing legitimate income earned in India (rent, pension). Restricted repatriability (up to US \USD 1 Millionillion per financial year).

2. External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs)

Indian corporations borrow from foreign lenders (commercial banks, bond markets) to fund capital expenditure due to lower foreign interest rates. However, ECBs create Currency Mismatch Risk: if the Rupee depreciates by 10%, the domestic corporate must earn 10% more Rupees to service its foreign debt.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: The 10% Rule: If an FPI acquires $9.9%$ equity in an Indian firm, it is FPI. If it purchases an additional $0.2%, taking its stake to $10.1%, the ENTIRE holding is reclassified as FDI under Mayaram Committee rules!
  • Trap 2: Are NRI Deposits FDI?: No! NRI deposits are debt liabilities in the Financial Account under Banking Capital. They earn interest and must be repaid.
  • Trap 3: Automatic vs Government Route in FDI: In India, over 90%90%90% of sectors are on the Automatic Route (requiring only post-facto RBI reporting). Sectors like defence beyond certain caps, print media, or investments from countries sharing land borders with India require prior Government Approval.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Mayaram 10% rule, FPI categorization, ECB all-in-cost parameters, and NRE vs FCNR-B exchange risk allocation.
  • IIBF DBF & SBI/IBPS PO: NRI account operational rules (NRE, NRO, FCNR-B) and FDI sectoral caps.

Currency Convertibility: Current vs Capital Account Convertibility & Tarapore Committee Preconditions

The economic theory, legal framework, and sequencing of Currency Convertibility. Distinguishes Current Account Convertibility (the freedom to convert domestic currency into foreign currency for goods, services, and remittances at market exchange rates, achieved by India in August 1994 under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement) from Capital Account Convertibility (CAC: the freedom to convert local financial assets into foreign financial assets and vice versa). Analyzes the S.S. Tarapore Committee reports (1997 & 2006) and the essential macroeconomic preconditions required for full CAC.

Intuition & Context

Can You Convert ₹10 Crore into Dollars and Buy a Villa in Dubai?

Consider two everyday scenarios:

  1. Scenario A (Current Account): You run an electronics shop in Jaipur and want to import ₹50 Lakh worth of computer chips from Taiwan. You walk into your bank with Indian Rupees, and by law, the bank must convert your Rupees into US Dollars at the market exchange rate. The government cannot stop you. India has Full Current Account Convertibility.
  2. Scenario B (Capital Account): You sell your ancestral land in Rajasthan for ₹20 Crore. You want to convert all ₹20 Crore into US Dollars, wire it to Zurich, and buy Swiss sovereign bonds. Can you do this freely? No. Under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), an Indian resident can remit a maximum of US $250,000 per financial year abroad for capital investments. India has Partial / Calibrated Capital Account Convertibility.

Why does the RBI restrict capital transfers? Because during a panic, if all wealthy citizens converted their wealth to dollars simultaneously, the Rupee would collapse and the banking system would disintegrate (as happened in the 1997 East Asian Financial Crisis).

Why India Adopted a "Calibrated" Approach

The 1997 Asian Crisis (Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea) proved that premature full CAC in countries with fragile domestic banks and high short-term debt causes catastrophic economic collapse. India's cautious, calibrated liberalization (permitting non-debt FDI first, then long-term debt, while retaining prudential limits on volatile debt and retail outflows) protected India during the 1997 Asian Crisis and 2008 Global Financial Crisis.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Is the Rupee Fully Convertible on Current Account?: YES. Under Article VIII of the IMF (since August 1994), India cannot restrict payments for imports, services, or travel. The Rupee is 100% convertible on the Current Account.
  • Trap 2: Is the Rupee Fully Convertible on Capital Account?: NO. The Rupee is only partially convertible on the Capital Account. Foreigners can invest easily in equity, but domestic citizens face retail caps (LRS US $250,000), and foreign debt flows face strict RBI caps.
  • Trap 3: FEMA Section 5 vs Section 6: Section 5 of FEMA guarantees freedom for Current Account transactions (unless restricted by Central Govt for security/morals); Section 6 empowers the RBI to regulate, restrict, or prohibit Capital Account transactions.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Tarapore Committee signposts (1997 vs 2006), Article VIII IMF mandate, and FEMA Sections 5 vs 6.
  • IIBF DBF, SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) annual limit (US $250,000) and definitions of current vs capital account convertibility.

⚡ Key Revision & Architecture

Current Account Convertibility: Freedom to convert currency for goods, services, and remittances. Achieved by India in August 1994 (Article VIII IMF). Capital Account Convertibility (CAC): Freedom to convert financial assets across borders. India has Partial CAC (LRS limit USD 250,000/yr for residents). Tarapore Committee preconditions: Fiscal deficit < 3.5%, Inflation 3-5%, Gross NPAs < 5%, adequate forex reserves.
