11:T41a,### Core Electrical Formulas:

| Quantity | Mathematical Formula | SI Unit | Measurement Instrument | Circuit Connection Mode |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Electric Charge ($Q$)** | $Q = n \cdot e = I \cdot t$ | **Coulomb (C)** | Electrometer / Coulombmeter | Integrated charge |
| **Potential Difference ($V$)** | $\mathbf{V = \frac{W}{Q}}$ | **Volt (V)** $= \text{J/C}$ | **Voltmeter** (Infinite/High resistance) | **Connected in PARALLEL** across the component. |
| **Electric Current ($I$)** | $\mathbf{I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{n e}{t}}$| **Ampere (A)** $= \text{C/s}$| **Ammeter** (Zero/Low resistance) | **Connected in SERIES** in the circuit path. |

### The Electron vs Conventional Flow Duality:
- **Conventional Current Direction**: Flows from **Positive ($+$) $\to$ Negative ($-$)** terminal (Established in the 18th century before the discovery of electrons).
- **Actual Electron Drift Flow**: Negatively charged free electrons are repelled by negative terminal and flow from **Negative ($-$) $\to$ Positive ($+$)** terminal.12:T48d,### Ohm's Law Mathematical Formulation:
$$\mathbf{V = I \cdot R \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad I = \frac{V}{R} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad R = \frac{V}{I}}$$

- $V = \text{Potential difference across conductor (Volts, V)}$
- $I = \text{Current passing through conductor (Amperes, A)}$
- $R = \text{Electrical Resistance of conductor (Ohms, } \Omega\text{)}$
- **$1 \text{ Ohm} = 1 \text{ Volt} / 1 \text{ Ampere}$**

### Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Devices Comparison:

| Feature | Ohmic Conductors | Non-Ohmic Conductors / Devices |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **V-I Relationship** | **Strictly Linear ($V \propto I$)**; straight line through origin. | **Non-Linear (Curved)**; resistance varies with voltage/current. |
| **Resistance Value** | Constant $R = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$ (at constant temp). | Dynamic / Variable Resistance $r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$. |
| **Temperature Sensitivity**| Resistance increases linearly with temperature. | Often non-linear (e.g. semiconductor resistance drops with heat). |
| **Master Examples** | **Copper, Silver, Aluminium, Nichrome wire**. | **p-n Junction Diodes, LEDs, Transistors, Filament Bulbs, Vacuum Tubes**. |13:T4d9,### 1. The Wire Stretching Theorem (Constant Volume $V = A \cdot L$)
If a wire of resistance $R$ is stretched to $n$ times its original length ($L' = n L$):
- Because volume is conserved ($A' L' = A L \implies A' = A / n$):
  $$R' = \rho \frac{L'}{A'} = \rho \frac{n L}{A / n} = n^2 \left(\rho \frac{L}{A}\right) = \mathbf{n^2 R}$$
- **If a wire is stretched to DOUBLE its length ($2\times$)**: $R' = 2^2 R = \mathbf{4 R}$ (Resistance quadruples!).
- **If a wire is stretched to TRIPLE its length ($3\times$)**: $R' = 3^2 R = \mathbf{9 R}$.

### 2. Temperature Dependence across Material Classes:
- **Metals (Copper, Aluminium)**: $R_T = R_0 (1 + \alpha T)$. As $T \uparrow$, metal lattice ions vibrate with larger amplitude $\implies$ Electron collision frequency increases $\implies \mathbf{R \uparrow}$.
- **Semiconductors (Silicon, Germanium)**: As $T \uparrow$, covalent bonds break, releasing millions of new free conduction electrons $\implies$ Increased carrier density overwhelms collisions $\implies \mathbf{R \downarrow}$ (Negative temperature coefficient).
- **Heating Alloys (Nichrome = $80\% \text{ Ni} + 20\% \text{ Cr}$)**: High resistivity, very low $\alpha$, does **not oxidize (burn) even at red-hot $900^\circ\text{C}$**.14:T413,### Comparison of Circuit Topologies

| Circuit Parameter | Series Circuit ($R_s$) | Parallel Circuit ($R_p$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Current ($I$)** | **CONSTANT / SAME** through all components ($I_1 = I_2 = I$). | **DIVIDED** across branches: $\mathbf{I = I_1 + I_2 + \dots}$ |
| **Voltage ($V$)** | **DIVIDED** across components: $\mathbf{V = V_1 + V_2 + \dots}$ | **CONSTANT / SAME** across all branches ($V_1 = V_2 = V$). |
| **Equivalent Formula**| $\mathbf{R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots}$ | $\mathbf{\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots}$ |
| **Two Resistor Shortcut**| $R_s = R_1 + R_2$ | $\mathbf{R_p = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}}$ *(Product / Sum)* |
| **$n$ Identical Resistors ($R$)**| $R_s = n \cdot R$ | $R_p = \frac{R}{n}$ |
| **Net Magnitude** | **Larger than the largest resistor** ($R_s > R_{\text{max}}$). | **Smaller than the smallest resistor** ($R_p < R_{\text{min}}$). |
