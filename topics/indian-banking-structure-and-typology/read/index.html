Indian Banking Institutional Architecture & Typology

The institutional classification of the Indian banking system: Second Schedule of RBI Act 1934 (Scheduled vs Non-Scheduled banks), Public Sector Banks, Private Sector Banks (Old vs New generation), and Foreign Banks operating via branches vs Wholly Owned Subsidiaries (WOS).

Indian Banking Institutional Architecture & Typology · Concept 1 of 3

The Commercial Banking Landscape: Scheduled Banks (Second Schedule), PSBs, Private Banks & Foreign Bank Modes

The statutory classification and structural hierarchy of commercial banks in India under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 and Banking Regulation Act, 1949. Covers Scheduled Commercial Banks (Second Schedule inclusion criteria: paid-up capital & reserves >= ₹5 Lakh and non-detrimental affairs), Public Sector Banks (Nationalized Banks and State Bank of India), Private Sector Banks (Old vs New generation post-1993/2001), and Foreign Banks (Branch mode vs Wholly Owned Subsidiary / WOS mode).

Intuition & Context

The Tiered Banking Pyramid: Why Ownership and Status Matter

Not all banks are created equal. When you see a bank branch in India, it belongs to a precise statutory tier.

A Scheduled Bank carries the gold standard of trust: it has statutory borrowing access to the RBI's discount window (Repo/MSF), can clear cheques in the national settlement system, and can accept government deposits.

Furthermore, ownership shapes operational incentives: Public Sector Banks (PSBs) historically prioritized social banking, rural branches, and priority sector credit; Private Banks focused on technological innovation, customer service, and profitability; and Foreign Banks specialized in cross-border trade finance and multinational corporate banking.

Privileges and Obligations of Scheduled Bank Status

  • Privileges: Eligible for refinancing and liquidity support under RBI's Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF - Repo/MSF/Bank Rate), automatic membership in the Clearing House, and eligibility to handle Government business.
  • Obligations: Compulsory maintenance of Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) with the RBI under Section 42(1) and submission of statutory fortnightly returns (Form A).
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Ownership Models: PSBs, Private Generations & Foreign WOS Mode

1. Public Sector Banks (PSBs)

  • Following major consolidation mergers (2019–2020), India has 12 Public Sector Banks (SBI + 11 Nationalized Banks).
  • Majority equity (ge51%ge 51%ge51%) is held by the President of India (Central Government).
  • Primary vehicle for sovereign welfare schemes (PMJDY, PM-SVANidhi, MUDRA).

2. Private Sector Banks: The Two Generations

  • Old Private Sector Banks: Banks that existed prior to 1969 nationalization but were not nationalized because of smaller asset size (e.g., Federal Bank, South Indian Bank, Karnataka Bank).
  • New Private Sector Banks: Licensed post-1991 reforms following the Narasimham Committee I recommendations (1993 guidelines: HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank; 2001 guidelines: Kotak Mahindra Bank, YES Bank; 2014 guidelines: Bandhan Bank, IDFC FIRST Bank).

3. Foreign Banks (Branch vs WOS Model)

  • Under RBI's 2013 roadmap, foreign banks are incentivized to convert into Wholly Owned Subsidiaries (WOS) incorporated in India. WOS entities enjoy near-national branch expansion freedom and ring-fence Indian operations from global parent contagion.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Scheduled Bank Capital Criteria: Section 42(6)(a) of the RBI Act specifies a statutory historical threshold of ₹5 Lakh for Second Schedule inclusion. (Do not confuse this statutory minimum for Second Schedule with modern licensing guidelines requiring ₹500 Crore / ₹1,000 Crore capital for starting a new bank!).
  • Trap 2: SBI vs Nationalized Banks: SBI is governed by the State Bank of India Act, 1955, whereas Nationalized Banks (PNB, BoB, Canara Bank, etc.) are governed by the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Acts, 1970/1980.
  • Trap 3: Foreign Bank PSL Target: Foreign banks with 20 or more branches have the same 40% PSL target as domestic commercial banks.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Second Schedule Section 42 criteria, Foreign Bank WOS model, and PSB merger architecture.
  • SBI/IBPS PO, NABARD & RPSC RAS: List of 12 PSBs, Old vs New private banks, and scheduled bank privileges.

Indian Banking Institutional Architecture & Typology · Concept 2 of 3

Differentiated Banking Architecture: Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), Small Finance Banks (SFBs) & Payments Banks

The economic rationale, regulatory design, and operational mandates of differentiated (niche) banks in India: Regional Rural Banks (RRB Act 1976, 50:15:35 ownership model, 75% PSL mandate), Small Finance Banks (Nachiket Mor Committee 2014, financial inclusion of unserved/underserved, 75% PSL target, 50% loans <= ₹25 Lakh), and Payments Banks (high-volume low-value payments, ₹2 Lakh deposit cap, prohibited from lending/issuing credit cards).

Intuition & Context

Universal vs Differentiated Banking: Tailoring Banks to Specific Economic Needs

For decades, India followed a Universal Banking philosophy: every licensed bank was expected to do everything—accept deposits, fund giant steel factories, open rural branches, issue credit cards, and provide foreign exchange services.

However, universal banks naturally gravitated toward large corporate loans and wealthy urban centers, neglecting small farmers, street vendors, and micro-enterprises. Following the Nachiket Mor Committee Report (2014), the RBI shifted to a Differentiated Banking Architecture—licensing specialized, "niche" banks designed from the ground up to solve specific financial inclusion bottlenecks:

  • Small Finance Banks (SFBs): To provide savings and micro-credit to small business units and unorganized sector workers.
  • Payments Banks: To build high-volume, low-value digital payment pipelines and mobilize remittances without taking credit risk.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Institutional Mechanics of RRBs & SFB Evolution

1. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs - RRB Act, 1976)

  • Created following the Narasimham Working Group (1975) to combine the local familiarity of cooperatives with the modern business acumen of commercial banks.
  • Statutory Equity Tripartite Structure:
    Central Government: 50%∣Sponsor Bank: 35%∣State Government: 15%\text{Central Government: } 50% \quad | \quad \text{Sponsor Bank: } 35% \quad | \quad \text{State Government: } 15%Central Government: 50%∣Sponsor Bank: 35%∣State Government: 15%
  • Consolidation: Amalgamations reduced RRBs from 196 in the 1980s to 43 RRBs operating on a "One State, One RRB" (or contiguous region) model under NABARD supervisory oversight.

2. Small Finance Banks: Transition & "On-Tap" Licensing

  • Promoters: Primarily converted Microfinance Institutions (NBFC-MFIs like Equitas, Ujjivan) and Local Area Banks (Capital Small Finance Bank).
  • On-Tap Licensing (2019): Minimum initial paid-up capital of ₹200 Crore (₹300 Crore for universal transition eligibility after 5 years of successful operations).
  • Branch Requirement: At least 25%25%25% of branches must be in unbanked rural centers.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Can Payments Banks issue Credit Cards?: NO. Payments banks can issue ATM / Debit Cards, but are legally prohibited from issuing Credit Cards (because issuing a credit card is a form of unsecured lending).
  • Trap 2: Payments Bank Deposit Limit: The deposit cap for Payments Banks was increased by RBI in April 2021 from ₹1 Lakh to ₹2 Lakh per individual customer at the end of the day.
  • Trap 3: PSL Target for SFBs vs Universal Banks: Universal Commercial Banks have a 40% PSL target; Small Finance Banks and RRBs have a 75% PSL target.
  • Trap 4: RRB Shareholding Ratio: Central Govt is 50%, Sponsor Bank is 35%, and State Govt is 15%. (Examiners often swap the Sponsor Bank and State Govt shares).

Cross-Exam Focus

  • NABARD Grade A & RBI Grade B: RRB Act 1976 shareholding, Nachiket Mor Committee, SFB vs Payments Bank regulatory differences, and 75% PSL mandates.
  • SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Payments bank deposit limit (₹2 Lakh), debit vs credit card prohibition, and RRB ownership breakdown.

Cooperative Banking Architecture: Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs), The 3-Tier Rural Cooperative Structure & Dual Regulation Reforms

The cooperative credit architecture in India operating on the principle of mutual cooperation and democratic member control ("one member, one vote"). Examines the Urban Cooperative Banking structure (4-tiered regulatory categorization: Tier 1 to Tier 4), the Three-Tier Short-Term Rural Cooperative Credit Structure (Primary Agricultural Credit Societies / PACS at village level, District Central Cooperative Banks / DCCBs at district level, and State Cooperative Banks / StCBs at state apex), and the historical dual regulation conflict resolved by the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020.

Intuition & Context

Democratic Banking: The Cooperative Philosophy vs Joint-Stock Capitalism

In a standard commercial bank (like SBI or HDFC Bank), voting power is dictated by capital ownership: a billionaire holding 1,000,000 shares gets 1,000,000 votes, while a retail shareholder holding 1 share gets 1 vote.

A Cooperative Bank is fundamentally different. It is established by members of a community or occupational group based on mutual self-help. In a cooperative, voting follows the democratic principle of "One Member, One Vote", regardless of how many shares a member owns. Cooperative banks mobilize local savings and reinvest them within the same local community.

The Three-Tier Short-Term Rural Chain

  1. Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS): Village-level grassroots units. Not regulated as banks under BR Act; deal directly with farmers for crop loans (Kisan Credit Cards).
  2. District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs): Operate at district level, federating PACS and providing banking services.
  3. State Cooperative Banks (StCBs): Apex cooperative bank at state level, linking the cooperative system to NABARD and the RBI money market.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Dual Regulation Problem & The Historic 2020 Statutory Fix

The Dual Regulation Conflict (Pre-2020)

Historically, cooperative banks suffered from a disastrous regulatory overlap:

  • Banking Functions (Licensing, CRR, SLR, Prudential Norms): Regulated by RBI under Section 56 of Banking Regulation Act.
  • Incorporation, Management, Elections, Audit, and Liquidation: Controlled by the Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS) under State Cooperative Societies Acts (or Central Registrar for Multi-State Coops).

The Fallout: When cooperative bank managements engaged in fraud or political crony lending (e.g., the ₹6,500 Crore Punjab and Maharashtra Cooperative / PMC Bank scam in 2019), RBI could not remove directors or restructure the board without state RCS approval.

The Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020

Passed in September 2020, this landmark amendment transformed cooperative banking governance:

  1. Powers over Management: RBI can now superscede the Board of Directors of any UCB without consulting state governments.
  2. CEO/MD Appointment: Prior RBI approval is mandatory for appointing or removing MDs and CEOs of cooperative banks.
  3. Capital Raising: UCBs can now issue equity shares, preference shares, and unsecured debentures with RBI approval to boost capital.
  4. Voluntary Amalgamation & Reconstruction (Section 45): RBI can prepare schemes of reconstruction or merger without placing the bank under a moratorium.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Are PACS Banks?: NO. Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) are cooperative credit societies, NOT banks under the Banking Regulation Act (they cannot use the word "Bank" or issue cheques).
  • Trap 2: 2020 BR Act Amendment Scope: The 2020 amendment applies to Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) and Multi-State Cooperative Banks. It does NOT apply to PACS or Land Development Banks.
  • Trap 3: Tiered CRAR for UCBs: Tier 1 UCBs (deposits ≤\le≤ ₹100 Cr) have a 9% CRAR requirement. Tiers 2, 3, and 4 UCBs must maintain a 12% CRAR.
  • Trap 4: PSL Target for UCBs: RBI mandated UCBs to progressively increase their Priority Sector Lending (PSL) target to 75% of ANBC (phased in by March 31, 2026).

🏛️ 5-Minute Architecture & Key Revision

Cooperative Banking Structural Architecture:

  1. Short-Term Rural Pyramid:
    • Apex: State Cooperative Bank (StCB) (State level, connects to RBI/NABARD money market).
    • Intermediate: District Central Cooperative Bank (DCCB) (District level, federates PACS).
    • Grassroots: Primary Agricultural Credit Society (PACS) (Village level, provides crop loans/KCC; non-bank entity).
  2. Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs - 2022 RBI Framework):
    • Tier 1: Deposits ≤\le≤ ₹100 Crore (CRAR 9%).
    • Tier 2: Deposits ₹100 Crore to ₹1,000 Crore (CRAR 12%).
    • Tier 3: Deposits ₹1,000 Crore to ₹10,000 Crore (CRAR 12%).
    • Tier 4: Deposits $>$ ₹10,000 Crore (CRAR 12%, full Basel-like compliance).
  3. Legislative Landscape:
    • Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act 2020 amended Section 56 of BR Act.
    • RBI gained direct powers to supersede boards, appoint CEOs, and approve UCB mergers without moratoria under Section 45.
