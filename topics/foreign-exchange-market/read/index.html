Foreign Exchange Market & Exchange Rate Determination

Foreign Exchange Market & Exchange Rate Determination · Concept 1 of 2

Foreign Exchange Market Mechanics: Demand-Supply Dynamics, Quotation Systems & Currency Appreciation vs Depreciation

The foundational microstructure and macroeconomic price discovery mechanisms of the foreign exchange (forex) market. Analyzes the market institutional hierarchy (Authorised Dealers Category I, II, III under FEMA 1999), spot vs forward transactions, Direct Quotation (Price Currency / Base Currency, e.g. ₹84/USD) vs Indirect Quotation, the downward-sloping Demand Curve for foreign currency (derived from imports and outward capital) vs upward-sloping Supply Curve (derived from exports and inward capital), and the structural distinction between Market-Driven Appreciation/Depreciation and Statutorily Fixed Revaluation/Devaluation.

Intuition & Context

The Onion Market Analogy for US Dollars in Mumbai

Think of the US Dollar as a commodity, just like onions in a wholesale mandi in Nashik:

  • When a bad monsoon destroys onion crops, the supply of onions shrinks, and the price of onions shoots up from ₹30/kg to ₹80/kg.
  • In the Mumbai foreign exchange market, the US Dollar is the commodity and the Indian Rupee is the price.

What makes the price of a Dollar rise from ₹80 to ₹85 (Rupee Depreciation)?

  1. High Demand for Dollars: Global crude oil prices surge, so Indian oil refiners rush to buy billions of US Dollars to pay Saudi Aramco.
  2. Low Supply of Dollars: Foreign investors panic and sell Indian stocks, reducing the inflow of fresh US Dollars into Mumbai.

When demand for dollars exceeds supply, the price of 1 Dollar goes up in Rupees (from ₹80 to ₹85). The Rupee has depreciated, and the Dollar has appreciated.

Exchange Rate Regimes: Fixed, Floating, Crawling Peg & India's Managed Float Architecture

The institutional taxonomy of national exchange rate systems across the macroeconomic spectrum. Compares Hard Pegs (Dollarization, Currency Boards), Soft Pegs (Conventional Fixed Pegs, Target Bands, Crawling Pegs), Free Floating (Pure Market Clearing), and Managed / Dirty Floating regimes. Traces India's historic structural evolution from the post-independence British Pound peg, through the Basket Peg (1975–1991), the dual exchange rate LERMS (1992), to the Unified Market-Determined Exchange Rate System (1993–present) operating under an active RBI volatility-smoothing managed float.

Intuition & Context

The Shock Absorber vs The Anchor: Choosing an Exchange Rate Regime

Imagine driving a car over a rocky mountain road:

  1. A Fixed Exchange Rate is a Rigid Steel Axle: It provides total price certainty to businesses (no currency risk). But when a massive external shock hits (e.g., global oil prices double), the rigid axle cannot bend. The entire shock smashes directly into the domestic economy—causing factory closures, wage cuts, unemployment, and potential currency collapse when the central bank runs out of dollar reserves.
  2. A Free Floating Exchange Rate is a Soft Hydraulic Shock Absorber: When global oil prices surge, the currency instantly depreciates by 10%. This price change immediately makes foreign goods expensive, curbs domestic oil demand, makes domestic exports cheaper, and automatically absorbs the shock without forcing domestic wages down or depleting reserves.

Most nations (including India) choose a Managed Float: let the hydraulic shock absorber work to reflect economic reality, but step on the brakes if the car begins violently spinning out of control.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

India's Historic Evolution to the Market-Determined Managed Float

India's exchange rate architecture evolved through four historic epochs:

  1. Post-Independence (1947–1975) — Sterling / Par Value Peg:
    • Rupee pegged to the British Pound Sterling (and later US Dollar) under the Bretton Woods par value system with rigid foreign exchange controls (FERA 1973).
  2. 1975–1991 — Basket Peg System:
    • Rupee pegged to an undisclosed trade-weighted basket of currencies of India's major trading partners, with daily official rates set by the RBI.
  3. 1992–1993 — Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS):
    • Following the 1991 BoP Crisis, India introduced a Dual Exchange Rate System: Exporters surrendered 40%40%40% of foreign exchange at the official government rate (for essential imports like oil and fertilizer), while the remaining 60%60%60% was converted at market-determined rates.
  4. March 1993 to Present — Unified Market-Determined Exchange Rate (Managed Float):
    • Based on the C. Rangarajan Committee recommendations, the dual rate was abolished. India adopted a unified, market-determined exchange rate where the Rupee is determined by demand and supply in the interbank market, while the RBI intervenes to smooth excessive volatility.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Does the RBI Have a Target Exchange Rate?: NO. The RBI's official stated policy is that it does NOT target any specific level or band for the Rupee (e.g. ₹80 or ₹85). The RBI intervenes strictly to contain excessive high-frequency volatility, maintain orderly market conditions, and prevent speculative bubbles.
  • Trap 2: LERMS Architecture: LERMS (1992) was a 40:60 dual exchange rate system (40% official rate, 60% market rate), which acted as a successful stepping stone to full market unification in March 1993.
  • Trap 3: Currency Board vs Central Bank: A country with a Currency Board (like Hong Kong) cannot act as a Lender of Last Resort by printing unbacked currency; every domestic dollar issued must be 100% backed by physical foreign reserve assets.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: IMF classification of regimes, Rangarajan committee recommendations, LERMS 40:60 architecture, and currency board mechanics.
  • UPSC APFC, IIBF DBF & RPSC RAS: Distinguishing fixed, floating, and managed float regimes, and history of India's exchange rate reforms.
