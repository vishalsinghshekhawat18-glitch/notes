```

    │             /      \ Demand for Foreign Currency (D_USD)
    │            /        \ (Imports, Outward Travel, Loan Repayments)
    └───────────┴──────────┴────────► Quantity of Foreign Currency (USD)
```

### 1. Demand for Foreign Currency ($D_{\\text{USD}}$) - Downward Sloping
- When the price of USD is high (e.g., ₹90/USD), foreign goods are expensive in India, so Indian imports drop $\\implies$ lower demand for USD.
- When the price of USD is low (e.g., ₹75/USD), imports are cheap, so import demand surges $\\implies$ higher demand for USD.

### 2. Supply of Foreign Currency ($S_{\\text{USD}}$) - Upward Sloping
- When the price of USD is high (e.g., ₹90/USD), Indian goods are cheap for foreign buyers, so Indian exports expand $\\implies$ foreign buyers supply more USD to buy Rupees.

### 3. Equilibrium Price Discovery ($R^*$)
At $R^*$, the quantity of foreign currency demanded by Indian importers exactly equals the quantity supplied by Indian exporters and foreign investors.15:T414,How macroeconomic events shift the exchange rate in a market-determined float:

| Macroeconomic Shock | Demand / Supply Curve Shift | Impact on Equilibrium Exchange Rate ($₹/USD$) | Economic Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Global Crude Oil Price Surge** | $D_{\\text{USD}}$ shifts **RIGHT** (higher import bill) | Exchange rate rises (e.g., ₹84 $\\to$ ₹88) | **Rupee Depreciates** (Imported inflation rises) |
| **Global Tech Rally (Services Exports Surge)** | $S_{\\text{USD}}$ shifts **RIGHT** (higher export earnings) | Exchange rate falls (e.g., ₹84 $\\to$ ₹81) | **Rupee Appreciates** (Exports become less competitive) |

1. **A Fixed Exchange Rate is a Rigid Steel Axle**: It provides total price certainty to businesses (no currency risk). But when a massive external shock hits (e.g., global oil prices double), the rigid axle cannot bend. The entire shock smashes directly into the domestic economy—causing factory closures, wage cuts, unemployment, and potential currency collapse when the central bank runs out of dollar reserves.
2. **A Free Floating Exchange Rate is a Soft Hydraulic Shock Absorber**: When global oil prices surge, the currency instantly depreciates by 10%. This price change immediately makes foreign goods expensive, curbs domestic oil demand, makes domestic exports cheaper, and automatically absorbs the shock without forcing domestic wages down or depleting reserves.

| **Free Float** | High | Managed domestically | **Maximum** (100% independent) | Low / Minimal |19:T4cb,India's exchange rate architecture evolved through four historic epochs:

1. **Post-Independence (1947–1975) — Sterling / Par Value Peg**:
   - Rupee pegged to the British Pound Sterling (and later US Dollar) under the Bretton Woods par value system with rigid foreign exchange controls (FERA 1973).
2. **1975–1991 — Basket Peg System**:
   - Rupee pegged to an undisclosed trade-weighted basket of currencies of India's major trading partners, with daily official rates set by the RBI.
3. **1992–1993 — Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS)**:
   - Following the 1991 BoP Crisis, India introduced a **Dual Exchange Rate System**: Exporters surrendered $40\%$ of foreign exchange at the official government rate (for essential imports like oil and fertilizer), while the remaining $60\%$ was converted at market-determined rates.
4. **March 1993 to Present — Unified Market-Determined Exchange Rate (Managed Float)**:
   - Based on the **C. Rangarajan Committee recommendations**, the dual rate was abolished. India adopted a unified, market-determined exchange rate where the Rupee is determined by demand and supply in the interbank market, while the RBI intervenes to smooth excessive volatility.1a:T482,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Does the RBI Have a Target Exchange Rate?**: **NO.** The RBI's official stated policy is that it does **NOT target any specific level or band for the Rupee (e.g. ₹80 or ₹85)**. The RBI intervenes strictly to contain excessive high-frequency volatility, maintain orderly market conditions, and prevent speculative bubbles.
- **Trap 2: LERMS Architecture**: LERMS (1992) was a **40:60 dual exchange rate** system (40% official rate, 60% market rate), which acted as a successful stepping stone to full market unification in March 1993.
- **Trap 3: Currency Board vs Central Bank**: A country with a **Currency Board** (like Hong Kong) cannot act as a Lender of Last Resort by printing unbacked currency; every domestic dollar issued must be 100% backed by physical foreign reserve assets.
