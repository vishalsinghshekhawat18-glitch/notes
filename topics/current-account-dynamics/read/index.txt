### Numerical Example of Current Account Balance Calculation
Suppose India's external accounts in a given year show the following figures:
- Merchandise Exports ($X_g$): **+US \\USD 450 Billionillion**
- Merchandise Imports ($M_g$): **-US \\USD 700 Billionillion**
- $\\implies$ **Merchandise Trade Balance = -US \\USD 250 Billionillion** (Trade Deficit)
- Net Services Exports (Software, BPO, Travel): **+US \\USD 160 Billionillion**
- Net Primary Income (Interest/Dividend payments to foreign investors): **-US \\USD 35 Billionillion**
- Net Secondary Income (Workers' Remittances from diaspora): **+US \\USD 115 Billionillion**

$$\\begin{aligned}
CAB &= \\text{Trade Balance} + \\text{Net Services} + \\text{Net Primary Income} + \\text{Net Secondary Income} \\
&= (-250) + (+160) + (-35) + (+115) \\
&= -250 + 240 \\
&= -10 \\text{ Billion USD} \\quad (\\text{Current Account Deficit of US \\USD 10 Billionillion})
\\end{aligned}$$
*Result*: Despite a massive **US \\USD 250 Billionillion Trade Deficit**, India's actual **Current Account Deficit is only US \\USD 10 Billionillion**!15:T481,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Where Do Remittances Go?**: Remittances sent by NRIs to their families are recorded under **Secondary Income in the Current Account** (they are unrequited transfers, not loans). However, if an NRI deposits money into an **NRE/FCNR bank account** in India to earn interest, it is recorded in the **Financial/Capital Account** under Banking Capital!
- **Trap 2: Software Exports Are Invisibles**: Software exports do not pass through customs ports on physical ships; they are transmitted digitally and recorded under **Services (Invisibles) in the Current Account**.
- **Trap 3: Dividend Payments Abroad**: When an Indian subsidiary of a multinational (e.g., Samsung India) repatriates profits/dividends to its parent in Seoul, it is recorded as a **Debit entry under Primary Income in the Current Account**, NOT in the Capital Account!

### Step 1: Aggregate Demand (GDP by Expenditure)
$$Y = C + I + G + (X - M)$$
- $Y$: Gross National Output / Income
- $C$: Private Household Consumption
- $I$: Gross Domestic Investment (Physical Capex)
- $G$: Government Consumption Expenditure
- $X - M$: Net Exports / Current Account ($CA$)

### Step 2: Disposable Income Allocation
Gross domestic income $Y$ is allocated by households into consumption ($C$), private savings ($S_p$), and taxes paid to government ($T$):
$$Y = C + S_p + T$$

### Step 3: Equating the Two Expressions for $Y$
$$C + I + G + CA = C + S_p + T$$
Subtract $C$ from both sides:
$$I + G + CA = S_p + T$$
Rearranging to isolate $CA$:
$$CA = (S_p - I) + (T - G)$$

$$\\boxed{CA = S_{\\text{national}} - I}$$
Where:
- $S_p - I$: **Private Saving-Investment Gap**
- $T - G$: **Public Sector Budget Balance** (Government Revenue minus Expenditure = negative of Fiscal Deficit)
- $S_{\\text{national}} = S_p + (T - G)$: **Total National Saving**

### Theoretical Implications
1. If a country runs a **Current Account Deficit ($CA < 0$)**, then $I > S_{\\text{national}}$. The country is investing more than it saves.
2. **The Twin Deficits Hypothesis**: If the government runs a larger fiscal deficit (so $T - G$ becomes more negative), and private behavior ($S_p - I$) does not fully offset it, $CA$ must deteriorate (the CAD widens).17:T5c9,```

### India's Sustainable CAD Threshold
The Reserve Bank of India and the Rangarajan Committee benchmark India's **sustainable CAD limit at $2.0\% - 2.5\%$ of GDP**. A CAD within this range can be comfortably financed by stable, non-debt creating foreign capital inflows (FDI) without depleting foreign exchange reserves or elevating sovereign rollover risk.18:T497,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: CAD Means the Economy is Weak**: False! A rapidly growing developing economy with high return on capital *should* run a moderate CAD to import foreign technology and capital goods. The US has run a continuous CAD for 40 years.
- **Trap 2: Eliminating CAD by Banning Imports**: If the government restricts imports without raising domestic savings ($S$) or reducing physical investment ($I$), the underlying $S - I$ gap remains, causing domestic inflation or crowding out private investment.
- **Trap 3: Ricardian Equivalence in Twin Deficits**: Under strict **Ricardian Equivalence**, a tax cut ($T \\downarrow$) causes households to save 100% of the tax cut ($S_p \\uparrow$) in anticipation of future taxes, neutralizing the fiscal deficit impact on CAD ($CA$ unchanged). In reality, Ricardian Equivalence fails, and twin deficits hold.
