Interest Rate Theory & The Fisher Relation

Theoretical foundations of interest rates: nominal vs real interest rates, the Fisher Equation and Fisher Effect, expected vs ex-post real interest rates, negative real interest rate regimes, and macroeconomic consequences for savers, debtors, capital investment, and asset bubbles.

Interest Rate Theory & The Fisher Relation · Concept 1 of 1

Interest Rate Theory: Nominal vs Real Interest Rates, The Fisher Effect, Negative Real Rates & Macroeconomic Transmission

The foundational relationship between interest rates and price expectations: the distinction between nominal interest rate ($i$) and real interest rate ($r$), the Fisher Equation ($1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi^e) \implies r \approx i - \pi^e$), the Fisher Effect (one-for-one adjustment of nominal rates to expected inflation), the economic consequences of negative real interest rates, and transmission to savings, capital accumulation, and exchange rates.

Intuition & Context

The Illusion of Nominal Yield: Why 7% Return Can Be Negative

Imagine you deposit ₹1,00,000 in a bank fixed deposit yielding a 7%7%7% nominal interest rate. At the end of one year, the bank hands you ₹1,07,000. You feel 7%7%7% richer.

However, during that same year, general price inflation was 8%8%8%. A basket of goods that cost ₹1,00,000 at the start of the year now costs ₹1,08,000. Even though your account shows more nominal rupees, you can buy less real goods and services than when you started!

Your real return is negative: 7%−8%=−1%7% - 8% = -1%7%−8%=−1%. The nominal rate measures the growth in rupees; the real rate measures the growth in actual purchasing power.

Core Principle

The Fisher Equation & The Classical Fisher Effect

Formulated by economist Irving Fisher (1930), the relationship connects three variables:

1. The Exact Fisher Equation

(1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)(1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e)(1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)
Expanding:
1+i=1+r+πe+rπe1 + i = 1 + r + \pi^e + r\pi^e1+i=1+r+πe+rπe
Subtracting 1 and dropping the cross-product term rπer\pi^erπe (which is negligibly small for low rates):
i≈r+πe  ⟹  r≈i−πei \approx r + \pi^e \implies r \approx i - \pi^ei≈r+πe⟹r≈i−πe
Where:

  • iii = Nominal interest rate (the stated rate on financial contracts)
  • rrr = Real interest rate (the real growth rate of purchasing power)
  • πe\pi^eπe = Expected inflation rate over the loan period

2. Ex-Ante vs Ex-Post Real Interest Rate

  • Ex-Ante Real Interest Rate (rex-ante=i−πer_{\text{ex-ante}} = i - \pi^erex-ante​=i−πe): The real return expected at the time a loan contract is signed.
  • Ex-Post Real Interest Rate (rex-post=i−πr_{\text{ex-post}} = i - \pirex-post​=i−π): The actual real return realized after the actual inflation rate (π\piπ) is revealed at loan maturity.

3. The Fisher Effect (Long-Run Monetary Neutrality)

According to the Classical Dichotomy, the equilibrium real interest rate (rrr) is determined in the goods market by real forces (savings supply and investment productivity). Therefore, an increase in central bank money growth raises expected inflation πe\pi^eπe, causing a one-for-one upward adjustment in nominal interest rates (iii), leaving long-run equilibrium rrr unaffected.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Macroeconomic Consequences of Negative Real Interest Rates

When central bank policy rates or deposit rates fall below the prevailing inflation rate (i<πi < \pii<π), the real interest rate turns negative (r<0r < 0r<0). This produces significant structural distortions in an emerging economy like India:

Key Channels

  1. Financial Disintermediation: Households withdraw financial savings from banks and purchase physical inflation-hedges (Gold, Real Estate), starving the formal manufacturing sector of long-term investment capital.
  2. Financial Repression: Governments historically maintained artificially low nominal interest rates below inflation to cheapen sovereign debt service at the expense of domestic savers.

  • Trap 2: Ex-Ante vs Ex-Post: Ex-ante uses expected inflation πe\pi^eπe; Ex-post uses actual realized inflation π\piπ.
  • Trap 3: Real Rate and Monetary Policy Stance: A policy repo rate of 6.50%6.50%6.50% when CPI inflation is 4.00%4.00%4.00% implies a positive real policy rate of +2.50%+2.50%+2.50% (restrictive/tight stance). If inflation surges to 7.00%7.00%7.00%, the real policy rate becomes −0.50%-0.50%−0.50% (accommodative/expansionary stance in real terms), even without any change in the nominal rate!

Fisher Interest Rate Architecture:

  1. Exact Equation: (1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)  ⟹  r=1+i1+πe−1(1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e) \implies r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi^e} - 1(1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)⟹r=1+πe1+i​−1.
  2. Linear Approximation: r≈i−πer \approx i - \pi^er≈i−πe.
  3. Ex-Ante vs Ex-Post Distinction:
    • Ex-Ante: rex-ante=i−πer_{\text{ex-ante}} = i - \pi^erex-ante​=i−πe (Forward-looking decision rate).
    • Ex-Post: rex-post=i−πr_{\text{ex-post}} = i - \pirex-post​=i−π (Realized outcome rate).
  4. Fisher Effect Mechanism:
    • Step 1: Central bank accelerates money growth ΔM\Delta MΔM.
    • Step 2: In long run, inflation expectations rise Δπe=ΔM\Delta \pi^e = \Delta MΔπe=ΔM.
    • Step 3: Nominal interest rate rises by identical quantum: Δi=Δπe\Delta i = \Delta \pi^eΔi=Δπe.
    • Step 4: Real interest rate rrr remains constant at goods market equilibrium.
  5. Macroeconomic Regimes:
    • i>πi > \pii>π: Positive real rate (Incentivizes financial savings, controls credit).
    • i<πi < \pii<π: Negative real rate (Financial repression, credit boom, asset bubbles, currency pressure).
