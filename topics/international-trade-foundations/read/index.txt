11:T6e0,Let us examine a 2-country (India and Germany), 2-commodity (Textiles and Machinery) model with labor as the only input:\n\n### Output Produced per 100 Hours of Labor\n| Country | Textiles (Bales) | Machinery (Units) | Domestic Opportunity Cost of 1 Unit of Machinery | Domestic Opportunity Cost of 1 Bale of Textiles |\n| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **India** | 100 bales | 20 units | $\\frac{100}{20} = 5$ bales of textiles | $\\frac{20}{100} = 0.2$ units of machinery |\n| **Germany** | 120 bales | 60 units | $\\frac{120}{60} = 2$ bales of textiles | $\\frac{60}{120} = 0.5$ units of machinery |\n\n### Analytical Takeaways\n1. **Absolute Advantage**: Germany has an absolute advantage in **both** textiles ($120 > 100$) and machinery ($60 > 20$).\n2. **Comparative Advantage**:\n   - In Germany, producing 1 machine costs **2 bales of textiles**.\n   - In India, producing 1 machine costs **5 bales of textiles**.\n   - Therefore, **Germany has a comparative advantage in Machinery** (lower opportunity cost: $2 < 5$).\n   - In India, producing 1 bale of textiles costs **0.2 machines**, whereas in Germany it costs **0.5 machines**.\n   - Therefore, **India has a comparative advantage in Textiles** (lower opportunity cost: $0.2 < 0.5$).\n3. **Mutually Beneficial Terms of Trade (TOT)**:\n   Trade will occur at any exchange ratio between the two domestic opportunity cost ratios:\n   $$2 \\text{ bales of textiles} < 1 \\text{ unit of machinery} < 5 \\text{ bales of textiles}$$\n   If the international price settles at $1 \\text{ machine} = 3.5 \\text{ bales of textiles}$, Germany gains 1.5 bales per machine exported, and India saves 1.5 bales per machine imported. Total world production expands without adding any extra labor inputs!12:T59c,How international trade expands welfare beyond domestic technological frontiers:\n\n```\n                    AUTARKY (NO TRADE) vs TRADE EQUILIBRIUM\n                                       │\n        ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐\n        ▼                                                             ▼\nPRODUCTION POSSIBILITY FRONTIER (PPF)               CONSUMPTION POSSIBILITY FRONTIER (CPF)\n• Bounded by domestic resource constraints          • Established by International Terms of Trade\n• Economy must consume what it produces             • Specialization at Point of Comparative Advantage\n• Welfare strictly inside or on domestic PPF        • Consumption point shifts OUTSIDE domestic PPF\n```\n\n### Steps to Mutual Gains from Trade\n1. **Specialization**: Each country reallocates its domestic factors toward the sector where its marginal rate of transformation is lower than the world price ratio.\n2. **Export & Import**: India produces excess textiles and exports them; Germany produces excess machinery and exports them.\n3. **Consumption Expansion**: Both nations consume at points that lie **strictly outside their domestic PPFs**, achieving higher social indifference curves (higher collective utility) without technological breakthroughs.13:T430,### Critical Examiner Traps\n- **Trap 1: The Pauper Labor Fallacy**: "Free trade with low-wage countries hurts high-wage nations because cheap labor undercuts domestic industry." -> *False*: High wages reflect high labor productivity. What matters is *comparative* cost, not absolute wage differentials.\n- **Trap 2: The Sweatshop Fallacy**: "Trade exploits developing nations because their wages are lower than developed nations." -> *False*: In the absence of trade, developing country workers would face even worse domestic alternatives with lower real purchasing power.\n- **Trap 3: Absolute vs Comparative Advantage**: An absolute productivity advantage is **neither necessary nor sufficient** for exporting a good. Theoretical Progression:\n   - Mercantilism (16th-18th c.): Trade is zero-sum; maximize gold reserves via export surplus.\n   - Absolute Advantage (Adam Smith 1776): Positive-sum trade based on absolute labor productivity differences.\n   - Comparative Advantage (David Ricardo 1817): Positive-sum trade determined by relative domestic opportunity costs.\n   - Opportunity Cost Theory (Gottfried Haberler 1936): Replaced labor theory with PPF Marginal Rate of Transformation ($MRT$).\n2. Equilibrium Condition:\n   - Domestic Opportunity Cost Ratio Country A: $\\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_y}\\right)_A$\n   - Domestic Opportunity Cost Ratio Country B: $\\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_y}\\right)_B$\n   - Mutually Beneficial Terms of Trade ($TOT$): $\\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_y}\\right)_A < \\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_y}\\right)_{\\text{world}} < \\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_y}\\right)_B$\n3. Net Barter (Commodity) Terms of Trade ($N$)\n$$N = \\left(\\frac{P_x}{P_m}\\right) \\times 100$$\n- $P_x$ = Export Price Index, $P_m$ = Import Price Index.\n- *Limitation*: Measures only price ratios, ignoring export volume expansion.\n\n### 2. Income Terms of Trade ($I$ / Capacity to Import)\n$$I = N \\times Q_x = \\left(\\frac{P_x \\times Q_x}{P_m}\\right)$$\n- $Q_x$ = Export Volume Index. Represents the total purchasing power of export earnings over imports. If export prices fall by $10\\%$ but export volume expands by $50\\%$, $N$ deteriorates but $I$ significantly improves!\n\n### 3. Single Factoral Terms of Trade ($S$)\n$$S = N \\times Z_x$$\n- $Z_x$ = Productivity Index in the domestic export sector. Measures the volume of imports a nation can purchase per unit of domestic factor (labor/capital) input.\n\n### 4. Double Factoral Terms of Trade ($D$)\n$$D = N \\times \\left(\\frac{Z_x}{Z_m}\\right)$$\n- $Z_m$ = Productivity Index in foreign export sectors. Compares the productivity efficiency of domestic factors against foreign factors.\n\n### 5. Gross Barter Terms of Trade ($G$)\n$$G = \\left(\\frac{Q_m}{Q_x}\\right) \\times 100$$\n- Measures physical quantities exchanged. An increase in $G$ means more physical imports are received per unit of physical exports.16:T5f1,When countries form a Preferential Trade Agreement (PTA), Free Trade Area (FTA), or Customs Union, two opposing economic forces occur:\n\n```\n                      REGIONAL ECONOMIC INTEGRATION WELFARE\n                                        │\n       ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐\n       ▼                                                                 ▼\nTRADE CREATION (Welfare Gain: +)                                TRADE DIVERSION (Welfare Loss: -)\n• Domestic high-cost, inefficient production                    • Low-cost non-member external producer\n  is replaced by lower-cost partner imports.                      is displaced by higher-cost union partner.\n• Tariff removed among members.                                 • Occurs because partner faces 0% tariff\n• Resource reallocation expands consumer surplus.                 while global lowest-cost producer pays external tariff.\n• Example: India importing cheaper machinery from FTA partner.  • Result: Tariff revenue lost, global efficiency reduced.\n```\n\n### Net Welfare Condition for a Customs Union\n$$\\text{Net Welfare Gain} = \\Delta \\text{Welfare}_{\\text{Trade Creation}} - \\Delta \\text{Welfare}_{\\text{Trade Diversion}}$$\n- A Customs Union is **welfare-enhancing** for its members and the world if Trade Creation exceeds Trade Diversion.17:T44b,### Critical Examiner Traps\n- **Trap 1: Deteriorating Net Barter TOT vs Income TOT**: A fall in export prices (deteriorating Net Barter TOT) does NOT mean a country is worse off if export volumes expand so dramatically that its total capacity to import ($I$) surges.\n- **Trap 2: FTAs are Always Good**: Preferential Free Trade Agreements are **second-best solutions**.
