12:T65a,Economics categorizes the functions of money into three distinct tiers:

1. **Primary Functions**:
   - **Medium of Exchange**: Acts as an intermediary to settle transactions, eliminating barter search costs.
   - **Unit of Account (Measure of Value)**: Provides a common denominator to express relative prices across heterogeneous commodities (e.g., pricing books, haircuts, and steel in ₹).

2. **Secondary Functions**:
   - **Standard of Deferred Payment**: Facilitates credit transactions, contracts, and future loan repayments.
   - **Store of Value**: Enables the transfer of purchasing power across time without rapid physical deterioration (subject to the erosive effect of inflation).

3. **Contingent Functions** (Prof. David Kinley):
   - Basis of credit system, distribution of national income among factor inputs, and equalization of marginal utility/productivity.

### Typology of Money
- **Commodity Money**: Money whose value comes from the commodity of which it is made (e.g., gold coins, salt, cattle) where Commodity Value = Monetary Value.
- **Representative Full-Bodied Money**: Paper tokens backed 100% by an equivalent reserve of gold or silver in state vaults.
- **Fiat Money**: Currency notes and coins without intrinsic commodity value, declared legal tender by sovereign decree.
- **Fiduciary Money**: Financial instruments accepted based on trust (e.g., cheques, drafts, promissory notes); not legal tender.
- **Near Money**: Highly liquid financial assets easily convertible into cash without significant loss of capital value (e.g., Treasury bills, short-term fixed deposits, commercial paper).13:T66a,In Indian monetary law and banking jurisprudence, precise legal distinctions govern monetary acceptance:

```
                    MONETARY INSTRUMENTS
                             │
        ┌────────────────────┴────────────────────┐
        ▼                                         ▼
   LEGAL TENDER                              OPTION MONEY
(Legally enforceable)                  (Voluntary Acceptance)
   • Currency Notes (Unlimited)           • Bank Cheques
   • Coins (Limited under Coinage Act)    • Demand Drafts
   • RBI Digital Rupee (e₹)               • Credit/Debit Cards
```

### Statutory Framework in India
1. **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 (Section 22 & 26)**:
   - **Section 22**: Grants RBI the exclusive right to issue bank notes in India (except ₹1 note and coins, which are minted by the Ministry of Finance, Government of India, though put into circulation solely by RBI).
   - **Section 26(1)**: Every bank note is legal tender at any place in India and is guaranteed by the Central Government.
   - **Section 26(2)**: Grants power to the Central Government on recommendation of the Central Board of RBI to declare any series of banknotes as ceasing to be legal tender (statutory basis of demonetization).
2. **Coinage Act, 2011 (Section 6)**:
   - Coins of denomination not less than ₹1 are legal tender for any sum not exceeding ₹1,000.
   - Fifty paise coins are legal tender for any sum not exceeding ₹10.
   - Coins of denomination lower than 50 paise were officially demonetized on June 30, 2011.14:T4d0,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: ₹1 Note vs RBI Banknotes**: The ₹1 note is signed by the **Finance Secretary** (Ministry of Finance, GoI), whereas all banknotes from ₹2 up to ₹2,000 (when in circulation) carry the signature of the **Governor of the Reserve Bank of India**.
- **Trap 2: Fiat Money vs Legal Tender**: All legal tender is fiat money, but not all fiat money is necessarily legal tender for all transactions (e.g., demonetized currency ceases to be legal tender even if physically issued by the sovereign). Cheques are fiduciary money, NOT legal tender (a merchant can legally refuse a cheque, but cannot refuse legal tender currency within statutory limits).
- **Trap 3: Limited vs Unlimited Legal Tender**: Coins are **limited legal tender** under Section 6 of the Coinage Act, 2011 (up to ₹1,000 for coins $\ge$ ₹1). Only RBI currency notes are **unlimited legal tender**.

### 1. The Traditional 1977 Aggregates
- **Reserve Money ($M_0$ / High-Powered Money / Monetary Base)**:
  $$\text{M}_0 = \text{Currency in Circulation (CIC)} + \text{Bankers' Deposits with RBI} + \text{"Other" Deposits with RBI}$$
  *(Note: Currency in Circulation includes Vault Cash with banks + Currency with the Public)*.
- **$M_1$ (Narrow Money)**:
  $$\text{M}_1 = \text{Currency with the Public (CWP)} + \text{Demand Deposits with Banking System (DD)} + \text{"Other" Deposits with RBI (OD)}$$
  *Highest liquidity; zero investment yield*.
- **$M_2$**:
  $$\text{M}_2 = \text{M}_1 + \text{Post Office Savings Bank Deposits}$$
- **$M_3$ (Broad Money)**:
  $$\text{M}_3 = \text{M}_1 + \text{Time Deposits with the Banking System (TD)}$$
  *Most widely monitored aggregate for macro policy and inflation forecasting*.
- **$M_4$**:
  $$\text{M}_4 = \text{M}_3 + \text{Total Post Office Deposits (excluding National Savings Certificates / NSC)}$$

### Liquidity Order
$$\text{Liquidity: } \text{M}_1 > \text{M}_2 > \text{M}_3 > \text{M}_4$$16:T6d2,In 1998, the RBI Working Group (Chaired by Dr. Y.V. Reddy) restructured aggregates into **New Monetary Aggregates (NM)** and **Liquidity Aggregates (L)**:

```
                     1998 RBI LIQUIDITY AGGREGATES
                                   │

   L1                             L2                             L3
NM3 + All Post Office       L1 + Term Deposits &        L2 + Public Deposits
Deposits (excl. NSC)         Borrowings of FIs (NABARD,   with NBFCs
                             EXIM, SIDBI, NHB) + CDs
```

### New Monetary Aggregates ($NM_1, NM_2, NM_3$)
- **$NM_1$** = Currency with the Public + Demand Deposits with Banking System + "Other" Deposits with RBI.
- **$NM_2$** = $NM_1$ + Short-term Time Deposits of residents (contractual maturity up to 1 year).
- **$NM_3$** = $NM_2$ + Long-term Time Deposits of residents (maturity over 1 year) + Call/Term Funding from financial institutions.

### Fisher's Velocity of Money ($V$)
The velocity of money represents the average frequency with which a unit of money is spent on final goods and services during a given period.
$$M \cdot V = P \cdot Y$$
Where:
- $M$ = Money Supply ($M_3$ or $M_1$)
- $V$ = Income Velocity of Money ($V = \frac{P \cdot Y}{M} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{M}$)
- $P$ = Price Level
- $Y$ = Real Output (Real GDP)

*Impact of Digital Payments (UPI/IMPS)*: Increases the transaction velocity of narrow money, allowing the same monetary base to intermediate a larger volume of transactions.17:T513,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Currency in Circulation vs Currency with the Public**:
  $$\text{Currency in Circulation (CIC)} = \text{Currency with the Public (CWP)} + \text{Vault Cash held by Commercial Banks}$$
  $M_0$ uses **Currency in Circulation**, whereas $M_1$ uses **Currency with the Public** (because vault cash is an asset of commercial banks and already backs demand deposits; adding it to $M_1$ would cause double counting).
- **Trap 2: Post Office Deposits in $M_3$**: $M_3$ does **NOT** include post office savings deposits. Post office savings deposits are included in $M_2$ and $M_4$ (and in $L_1$).
- **Trap 3: Inter-Bank Deposits**: Inter-bank deposits (deposits held by one commercial bank in another commercial bank) are **excluded** from all money supply measures ($M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4$) because they represent internal banking claims, not public purchasing power.
- **Trap 4: NSC in $M_4$**: National Savings Certificates (NSC) are explicitly **excluded** from $M_4$ and $L_1$.
