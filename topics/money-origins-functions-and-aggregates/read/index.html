Money: Origins, Functions, Typology & Aggregates · Concept 1 of 2

Origins, Functions, Evolution and Typology of Money: From Commodity to Fiat and Legal Tender

The economic evolution of money as a solution to barter frictions (double coincidence of wants), spanning commodity money, metallic coinage, fiduciary instruments, and state-backed fiat currency. Covers the primary functions (medium of exchange, unit of account), secondary functions (standard of deferred payment, store of value), contingent functions, and legal distinctions (legal tender, limited vs unlimited legal tender, and fiat money).

Intuition & Context

The Liquidity Bridge: Eliminating the Coincidence Problem

Imagine an economy with no money. If an economics teacher wants a haircut, she must find a barber who specifically desires 45 minutes of microeconomics tuition. If the barber wants shoes instead, no transaction can take place without an elaborate, high-friction multi-party chain. This is the Double Coincidence of Wants problem.

Money solves this informational and transaction friction by acting as a universal liquidity bridge. Instead of finding reciprocal trading partners, every market participant trades goods and services for money, and subsequently trades money for desired goods. Money is whatever is generally accepted in exchange for goods and services or in the settlement of debts.

Core Principle

The Functional Hierarchy & Typology of Money

Economics categorizes the functions of money into three distinct tiers:

  1. Primary Functions:

    • Medium of Exchange: Acts as an intermediary to settle transactions, eliminating barter search costs.
    • Unit of Account (Measure of Value): Provides a common denominator to express relative prices across heterogeneous commodities (e.g., pricing books, haircuts, and steel in ₹).

  2. Secondary Functions:

    • Standard of Deferred Payment: Facilitates credit transactions, contracts, and future loan repayments.
    • Store of Value: Enables the transfer of purchasing power across time without rapid physical deterioration (subject to the erosive effect of inflation).

  3. Contingent Functions (Prof. David Kinley):

    • Basis of credit system, distribution of national income among factor inputs, and equalization of marginal utility/productivity.

Typology of Money

  • Commodity Money: Money whose value comes from the commodity of which it is made (e.g., gold coins, salt, cattle) where Commodity Value = Monetary Value.
  • Representative Full-Bodied Money: Paper tokens backed 100% by an equivalent reserve of gold or silver in state vaults.
  • Fiat Money: Currency notes and coins without intrinsic commodity value, declared legal tender by sovereign decree.
  • Fiduciary Money: Financial instruments accepted based on trust (e.g., cheques, drafts, promissory notes); not legal tender.
  • Near Money: Highly liquid financial assets easily convertible into cash without significant loss of capital value (e.g., Treasury bills, short-term fixed deposits, commercial paper).
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Legal Tender vs Option Money & Statutory Currency Mechanics in India

In Indian monetary law and banking jurisprudence, precise legal distinctions govern monetary acceptance:

                    MONETARY INSTRUMENTS
                             │
        ┌────────────────────┴────────────────────┐
        ▼                                         ▼
   LEGAL TENDER                              OPTION MONEY
(Legally enforceable)                  (Voluntary Acceptance)
   • Currency Notes (Unlimited)           • Bank Cheques
   • Coins (Limited under Coinage Act)    • Demand Drafts
   • RBI Digital Rupee (e₹)               • Credit/Debit Cards

Statutory Framework in India

  1. Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 (Section 22 & 26):
    • Section 22: Grants RBI the exclusive right to issue bank notes in India (except ₹1 note and coins, which are minted by the Ministry of Finance, Government of India, though put into circulation solely by RBI).
    • Section 26(1): Every bank note is legal tender at any place in India and is guaranteed by the Central Government.
    • Section 26(2): Grants power to the Central Government on recommendation of the Central Board of RBI to declare any series of banknotes as ceasing to be legal tender (statutory basis of demonetization).
  2. Coinage Act, 2011 (Section 6):
    • Coins of denomination not less than ₹1 are legal tender for any sum not exceeding ₹1,000.
    • Fifty paise coins are legal tender for any sum not exceeding ₹10.
    • Coins of denomination lower than 50 paise were officially demonetized on June 30, 2011.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Pitfalls, Examiner Traps & PYQ Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: ₹1 Note vs RBI Banknotes: The ₹1 note is signed by the Finance Secretary (Ministry of Finance, GoI), whereas all banknotes from ₹2 up to ₹2,000 (when in circulation) carry the signature of the Governor of the Reserve Bank of India.
  • Trap 2: Fiat Money vs Legal Tender: All legal tender is fiat money, but not all fiat money is necessarily legal tender for all transactions (e.g., demonetized currency ceases to be legal tender even if physically issued by the sovereign). Cheques are fiduciary money, NOT legal tender (a merchant can legally refuse a cheque, but cannot refuse legal tender currency within statutory limits).
  • Trap 3: Limited vs Unlimited Legal Tender: Coins are limited legal tender under Section 6 of the Coinage Act, 2011 (up to ₹1,000 for coins ≥\ge≥ ₹1). Only RBI currency notes are unlimited legal tender.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Focus on definitions of money, functions, demonetization statutory powers (Section 26(2) RBI Act), and fiat vs fiduciary distinctions.
  • IIBF DBF & SBI/IBPS PO: Focus on ₹1 note signatory, Coinage Act limits, legal tender definitions, and negotiable instruments.

Money: Origins, Functions, Typology & Aggregates · Concept 2 of 2

Money Supply Aggregates: Reserve Money (M0), Narrow/Broad Money (M1, M2, M3, M4), Liquidity Aggregates & Velocity of Money

The official measurement of money supply in India based on the RBI Working Group recommendations (1977 Working Group and 1998 Y.V. Reddy Working Group). Covers Reserve Money / High-Powered Money ($M_0$), Narrow Money ($M_1$), Broad Money ($M_3$), post-1998 New Monetary Aggregates ($NM_1, NM_2, NM_3$), Liquidity Aggregates ($L_1, L_2, L_3$), and Fisher’s Velocity of Money ($V = \frac{PY}{M}$).

Intuition & Context

The Liquidity Spectrum: From Cash in Pocket to Long-Term Bank Deposits

Not all money is equally accessible for immediate spending. A ₹500 currency note in your wallet can buy groceries in 5 seconds. A demand deposit in a savings account can be spent via UPI in 10 seconds. However, a 5-year bank fixed deposit (FD) or an NBFC certificate of deposit requires formal liquidation steps.

Central banks measure the total stock of money in an economy along a liquidity spectrum, arranging assets from the most liquid (immediately spendable without conversion cost) to broad monetary stores of purchasing power. The total volume of money directly dictates spending capacity, aggregate demand, and price pressure.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

New Monetary Aggregates (1998 Y.V. Reddy Report) & Velocity of Money

In 1998, the RBI Working Group (Chaired by Dr. Y.V. Reddy) restructured aggregates into New Monetary Aggregates (NM) and Liquidity Aggregates (L):

                                   │

   L1                             L2                             L3
NM3 + All Post Office       L1 + Term Deposits &        L2 + Public Deposits
Deposits (excl. NSC)         Borrowings of FIs (NABARD,   with NBFCs
                             EXIM, SIDBI, NHB) + CDs

New Monetary Aggregates (NM1,NM2,NM3NM_1, NM_2, NM_3NM1​,NM2​,NM3​)

  • NM1NM_1NM1​ = Currency with the Public + Demand Deposits with Banking System + "Other" Deposits with RBI.
  • NM2NM_2NM2​ = NM1NM_1NM1​ + Short-term Time Deposits of residents (contractual maturity up to 1 year).
  • NM3NM_3NM3​ = NM2NM_2NM2​ + Long-term Time Deposits of residents (maturity over 1 year) + Call/Term Funding from financial institutions.

Fisher's Velocity of Money (VVV)

The velocity of money represents the average frequency with which a unit of money is spent on final goods and services during a given period.
M⋅V=P⋅YM \cdot V = P \cdot YM⋅V=P⋅Y
Where:

  • MMM = Money Supply (M3M_3M3​ or M1M_1M1​)
  • VVV = Income Velocity of Money (V=P⋅YM=Nominal GDPMV = \frac{P \cdot Y}{M} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{M}V=MP⋅Y​=MNominal GDP​)
  • PPP = Price Level
  • YYY = Real Output (Real GDP)

Impact of Digital Payments (UPI/IMPS): Increases the transaction velocity of narrow money, allowing the same monetary base to intermediate a larger volume of transactions.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps, Formulas & Multi-Exam Question Patterns

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Currency in Circulation vs Currency with the Public:
    Currency in Circulation (CIC)=Currency with the Public (CWP)+Vault Cash held by Commercial Banks\text{Currency in Circulation (CIC)} = \text{Currency with the Public (CWP)} + \text{Vault Cash held by Commercial Banks}Currency in Circulation (CIC)=Currency with the Public (CWP)+Vault Cash held by Commercial Banks
    M0M_0M0​ uses Currency in Circulation, whereas M1M_1M1​ uses Currency with the Public (because vault cash is an asset of commercial banks and already backs demand deposits; adding it to M1M_1M1​ would cause double counting).
  • Trap 2: Post Office Deposits in M3M_3M3​: M3M_3M3​ does NOT include post office savings deposits. Post office savings deposits are included in M2M_2M2​ and M4M_4M4​ (and in L1L_1L1​).
  • Trap 3: Inter-Bank Deposits: Inter-bank deposits (deposits held by one commercial bank in another commercial bank) are excluded from all money supply measures (M1,M2,M3,M4M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4M1​,M2​,M3​,M4​) because they represent internal banking claims, not public purchasing power.
  • Trap 4: NSC in M4M_4M4​: National Savings Certificates (NSC) are explicitly excluded from M4M_4M4​ and L1L_1L1​.

🏛️ 5-Minute Architecture & Key Revision

Comprehensive Monetary Aggregates Formula Architecture:

  1. Reserve Money (M0) = Currency in Circulation (CWP + Vault Cash) + Bankers' Deposits with RBI + Other Deposits with RBI.
  2. M1 (Narrow Money) = Currency with the Public + Demand Deposits with Banking System + Other Deposits with RBI.
  3. M2 = M1 + Post Office Savings Bank Deposits.
  4. M3 (Broad Money) = M1 + Time Deposits with Banking System.
  5. M4 = M3 + All Post Office Deposits (excluding National Savings Certificates).
  6. 1998 Y.V. Reddy Aggregates:
    • NM1 = Currency with Public + Demand Deposits + Other Deposits with RBI.
    • NM2 = NM1 + Short-Term Time Deposits of Residents (maturity <= 1 year).
    • NM3 = NM2 + Long-Term Time Deposits of Residents (maturity > 1 year) + Call/Term Funding from FIs.
    • L1 = NM3 + All Post Office Deposits (excl. NSC).
    • L2 = L1 + Term Deposits & Borrowings of Financial Institutions (NABARD, EXIM, SIDBI, NHB) + Certificates of Deposit.
    • L3 = L2 + Public Deposits with NBFCs.
  7. Quantity Theory: M * V = P * Y => V = (Nominal GDP) / M.
