14:T567,### Comparison of Wave Types

| Dimension | Transverse Waves | Longitudinal Waves (Sound) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Particle Oscillation** | **Perpendicular ($90^\circ$)** to wave direction. | **Parallel ($0^\circ / 180^\circ$)** to wave direction. |
| **Structural Geometry** | Alternating **Crests (peaks)** and **Troughs (valleys)**. | Alternating **Compressions ($C$)** and **Rarefactions ($R$)**. |
| **Medium Requirement** | Can travel in solids, surface of liquids, and vacuum (for EM light waves). Cannot travel inside fluid bulk. | Requires a **material medium** with elastic properties (solids, liquids, gases). Cannot travel in vacuum. |
| **Density/Pressure** | No volumetric density change inside medium. | Periodic **oscillations in medium density and pressure**. |
| **Master Examples** | Light waves, radio waves, ripples on a pond surface, vibrating violin strings. | Sound waves in air/water, ultrasound, primary seismic P-waves. |

### The Universal Wave Equation:
$$\mathbf{v = f \cdot \lambda = \frac{\lambda}{T}}$$

- $v = \text{Wave propagation speed (m/s)}$
- $f = \nu = \text{Frequency (Hertz, Hz} = \text{oscillations per second, s}^{-1}\text{)}$
- $\lambda = \text{Wavelength (metres, m)} = \text{distance between consecutive crests or compressions}$
- $T = \text{Time Period (seconds, s)} = \text{time taken for one complete oscillation} = \frac{1}{f}$15:T42d,### The Universal Medium Speed Hierarchy:
$$\mathbf{v_{\text{Solids}} > v_{\text{Liquids}} > v_{\text{Gases}}}$$

### Speed of Sound in Common Media (at $20^\circ\text{C} - 25^\circ\text{C}$):

| Medium | Physical State | Speed of Sound ($m/s$) | Physical Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Aluminium / Steel / Iron** | Solid | $\mathbf{\approx 5000 - 6420 \, \text{m/s}}$ | Enormous elastic modulus (Young's modulus $Y$). |
| **Glass (Flint)** | Solid | $\approx 3980 \, \text{m/s}$ | High rigid elasticity. |
| **Sea Water** | Liquid | $\approx 1531 \, \text{m/s}$ | Dissolved salts increase elasticity and density. |
| **Pure Fresh Water** | Liquid | $\approx 1482 \, \text{m/s}$ | Moderate bulk modulus ($K$). |
| **Air ($22^\circ\text{C}$)** | Gas | $\mathbf{\approx 344 \, \text{m/s}}$ | Compressible gas (Air at $0^\circ\text{C} = 331 \, \text{m/s}$). |
| **Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$)** | Gas | $\approx 267 \, \text{m/s}$ | Heavy molecular mass lowers speed. |
| **Vacuum** | Vacuum | $\mathbf{0 \, \text{m/s}}$ | **Absolute Zero — Cannot propagate!** |16:T4b8,### 1. Effect of Temperature ($T$)
Sound speed in a gas is proportional to the square root of absolute temperature:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}} \implies v \propto \sqrt{T}$$
- In air, speed increases by $\approx \mathbf{0.61 \, \text{m/s}}$ for every $1^\circ\text{C}$ temperature rise:
  $$v_T \approx 331 + 0.61 \cdot T \, (\text{where } T \text{ is in } ^\circ\text{C})$$

### 2. Effect of Humidity (Why Sound Travels Faster on Rainy/Humid Days)
- Water vapor ($H_2O$, molar mass $= 18 \, \text{g/mol}$) is lighter than dry air (dominated by $N_2 = 28$ and $O_2 = 32$, average molar mass $\approx 29 \, \text{g/mol}$).
- As air becomes humid, light water vapor molecules displace heavier nitrogen/oxygen molecules $\implies$ **Moist air is LESS dense than dry air ($\rho_{\text{moist}} < \rho_{\text{dry}}$)**.
- Since $v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$, lower density yields **higher sound speed in humid air**.

### 3. The Pressure Independence Principle
- If atmospheric pressure $P$ increases, gas density $\rho$ increases in exact proportion ($P/\rho = \text{constant}$ at constant $T$).
- **Changing atmospheric pressure has ZERO effect on the speed of sound** if temperature remains constant.17:T47a,### The Three Frequency Bands of Sound

SONAR Echo-Ranging Mechanics
A ship's transmitter sends an ultrasonic pulse vertically down into the ocean. The pulse reflects off the seabed or a submarine and returns to the detector in total time $t$:
- Since the pulse travels down distance $d$ and back up distance $d$, total distance $= 2d$:
$$2d = v \cdot t \implies \mathbf{d = \frac{v \cdot t}{2}}$$
*(Where $v$ is the speed of sound in seawater $\approx 1530 \, \text{m/s}$).*

### 2. High-Yield Medical & Industrial Applications:
- **Lithotripsy**: High-energy focused ultrasound pulses create shockwaves that pulverize hard calcium oxalate kidney stones into fine sand, passed harmlessly in urine without surgery.
- **Flaw Detection in Metal Castings**: Ultrasound penetrates solid steel blocks. Any invisible internal crack or air bubble reflects the wave early, alerting quality engineers before installation in airplanes or bridges.
- **Ultrasonic Cleaning**: Spiral electronic components or jewellery immersed in cleaning liquid are subjected to ultrasound. The high frequency creates microscopic cavitation bubbles that scrub away grease and dirt from inaccessible crevices.19:T549,### Comparison: Distinct Echo vs Reverberation

| Feature | Echo | Reverberation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical Definition** | Repetition of original sound heard as a **distinct, separate event**. | Continuous **persistence / prolonged blurring** of sound due to multiple reflections. |
| **Time Delay** | Time gap $\ge \mathbf{0.1 \, \text{seconds}}$ ($1/10 \text{ s}$). | Continuous overlapping reflections occurring within $< 0.1 \, \text{s}$ intervals. |
| **Required Distance** | Reflecting obstacle must be $\ge \mathbf{17.2 \, \text{m}}$ away (at $22^\circ\text{C}$). | Occurs in enclosed halls of any size without soundproofing. |
| **Auditory Effect** | Clean repetition of words. | Muddies speech, making lectures inaudible and hollow. |

### Mathematical Derivation of Minimum Echo Distance ($d_{\text{min}}$):
- Human brain retains sound sensation for $\Delta t = 0.1 \, \text{s}$ (**Persistence of Hearing**).
- Speed of sound in air at $22^\circ\text{C}$: $v \approx 344 \, \text{m/s}$.
- Round-trip distance covered in $0.1 \, \text{s}$:
  $$\text{Total Distance } (2d) = v \times t = 344 \, \text{m/s} \times 0.1 \, \text{s} = 34.4 \, \text{m}$$
- Therefore, minimum single-way distance to wall:
  $$\mathbf{d_{\text{min}} = \frac{34.4}{2} = 17.2 \, \text{metres}} \quad (\text{or } 16.6 \, \text{m} \text{ at }
