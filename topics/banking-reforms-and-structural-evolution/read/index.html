Banking Reforms & Structural Evolution in India · Concept 1 of 2

Historical Evolution & Structural Reforms: Bank Nationalization, Priority Sector Lending (PSL) & Narasimham Committees

The structural transformation of Indian banking from class banking to mass banking and subsequent market-oriented deregulation. Covers Bank Nationalization (14 banks in 1969 with deposits >= ₹50 Cr, 6 banks in 1980 with deposits >= ₹200 Cr), the Priority Sector Lending (PSL) framework (40% ANBC for domestic/foreign banks >= 20 branches, 75% for RRBs/SFBs; Agriculture 18%, Small/Marginal Farmers 10%, Micro-enterprises 7.5%, Weaker Sections 12%), Priority Sector Lending Certificates (PSLCs), RIDF deposits, and the landmark reforms of Narasimham Committee I (1991) and II (1998).

Intuition & Context

From Elite Enclave to Universal Engine: The Two Eras of Indian Banking

In 1968, before nationalization, the Indian banking system was an exclusive club controlled by industrial conglomerates. Out of 14 major private banks, over 80%80%80% of total bank credit was extended to large corporate houses and directors' own firms, while Agriculture received less than 2%2%2% of all bank credit, despite employing 70%70%70% of the population.

Nationalization in 1969 forcefully transformed "Class Banking into Mass Banking", opening tens of thousands of rural branches. However, by 1991, heavy government control, politically directed lending, and exorbitant reserve requirements (SLR reached $38.5%$ and CRR reached 15%15%15%, pre-empting over 53%53%53% of bank deposits) crippled bank profitability. The Narasimham Committee (1991) restored financial health by introducing market discipline, prudential accounting, and private competition.

Core Principle

PSL Eligible Categories (8 Broad Sectors)

  1. Agriculture | 2. Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) | 3. Export Credit | 4. Education | 5. Housing | 6. Social Infrastructure | 7. Renewable Energy | 8. Others (SHGs/JLGs).

PSL Shortfall Mechanics & PSLCs

  • Priority Sector Lending Certificates (PSLCs): A market-driven trading mechanism launched by RBI on the CBS (e-Kuber) platform. Banks with surplus PSL achievement sell PSLCs to deficit banks without transferring the underlying loan risk. (4 Types: PSLC-Agriculture, PSLC-SF/MF, PSLC-Micro, PSLC-General).
  • Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF): If a bank still fails to meet its PSL target, the shortfall must be deposited with NABARD (RIDF) or SIDBI/NHB/MUDRA at punitive below-market interest rates.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Narasimham Committee Blueprint (1991 & 1998)

The twin reports of the M. Narasimham Committee designed the structural blueprint of modern Indian banking:

Narasimham Committee I (1991: Committee on Financial System)

  1. Phased Reduction of Pre-emptions: Recommended slashing statutory pre-emptions (CRR and SLR) to unlock funds for commercial credit.
  2. Deregulation of Lending Rates: Replaced administered interest rates with market-determined pricing.
  3. Prudential Accounting (IRAC Norms): Mandatory classification of assets into Standard, Sub-standard, Doubtful, and Loss assets with mandatory provisioning.
  4. Entry of New Private Banks: Allowed licensing of modern private sector banks (spawning HDFC, ICICI, Axis).
  5. Asset Reconstruction Fund (ARF): Recommended establishing specialized bad-loan recovery funds.

Narasimham Committee II (1998: Committee on Banking Sector Reforms)

  1. Capital Adequacy Enhancement: Raising CRAR to Basel standards (9%9%9%).
  2. NPA Convergence: Shortening the NPA recognition norm to the global standard of 90 days.
  3. Three-Tier Banking Structure: Merging banks into 2–3 international mega-banks, 8–10 national banks, and large numbers of local/regional banks.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Nationalization Deposit Thresholds: 1969 Nationalization (14 banks) = ₹50 Crore deposits; 1980 Nationalization (6 banks) = ₹200 Crore deposits.
  • Trap 2: PSL Agriculture vs SMF Sub-target: Total Agriculture target is 18%; Small and Marginal Farmers (SMF) sub-target is 10% (part of the 18%, not in addition to it).
  • Trap 3: PSL Shortfall Fund: Commercial bank PSL shortfalls are parked with NABARD (RIDF), NOT with the Central Government's consolidated fund.
  • Trap 4: PSLC Risk Transfer: When a bank buys a PSLC, no loan asset is transferred; only the priority sector compliance credit is transferred on paper.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & NABARD Grade A: PSL targets (40% vs 75%), sub-targets (18%, 10%, 7.5%, 12%), PSLC types, RIDF fund allocation, and Narasimham I/II.
  • SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Dates and bank counts for 1969/1980 nationalization, and Narasimham committee recommendations.

Banking Reforms & Structural Evolution in India · Concept 2 of 2

Modern Banking Sector Reforms: Indradhanush Mission, PSB Mega-Mergers, EASE Reforms & FSIB Governance

The contemporary governance, structural consolidation, and digital transformation initiatives in Indian Public Sector Banks (PSBs) from 2015 to the present. Covers Project Indradhanush (7-pronged revitalization plan: Appointments, BBB, Capitalization, De-stressing, Empowerment, Framework of Accountability, Governance), the consolidation mega-mergers (reducing 27 PSBs to 12 strong anchor banks), the EASE Reforms Agenda (Enhanced Access and Service Excellence: EASE 1.0 to EASE 6.0), and the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB, established in 2022 replacing the Banks Board Bureau).

Intuition & Context

Transforming Bureaucracies into Global Banking Champions

For decades, India had 27 separate Public Sector Banks. Many were small, technologically backward, and constantly ran to the taxpayer for capital bailouts whenever bad loans spiked.

To build global-scale banks capable of financing India's multi-trillion-dollar infrastructure needs, the government implemented a twin strategy:

  1. Structural Consolidation: Merging weak, sub-scale banks into large "Anchor Banks" with massive balance sheets, unified IT networks, and lower operating costs.
  2. Governance Modernization: Depoliticizing board appointments through the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB) and enforcing performance benchmarks through the EASE Reforms Agenda.
Core Principle

The Indradhanush Framework & The PSB Mega-Merger Architecture

Modern public sector banking reforms are anchored by two structural pillars:

1. Mission Indradhanush (The 7 Pillars - 2015)

  • A (Appointments): Splitting the post of Chairman and MD/CEO in PSBs; hiring private sector professionals.
  • B (Bank Board Bureau - now FSIB): Autonomous body to select top leadership.
  • C (Capitalization): Recapitalization bonds and equity infusion by the Government.
  • D (De-stressing): Resolving NPAs via asset sales and fast-track restructuring.
  • E (Empowerment): Greater operational autonomy without bureaucratic micromanagement.
  • F (Framework of Accountability): Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) linked to executive incentives.
  • G (Governance Reforms): Conclaves (Gyan Sangam) to drive institutional strategy.

2. The 12 Consolidated Public Sector Banks (Post-2020)

Mechanism & Operational Rule

The EASE Agenda & FSIB Governance Architecture

1. The EASE Reforms Agenda (Enhanced Access and Service Excellence)

Commissioned jointly by the Ministry of Finance and Indian Banks' Association (IBA), EASE is an annual scored ranking driving digital and operational excellence:

  • EASE 1.0 (2018): Clean banking, transparent NPA recognition, and IBC resolution.
  • EASE 2.0 (2019): Digital customer onboarding and retail lending automation.
  • EASE 3.0 (2020): Smart banking, Tech-enabled lending (dial-a-loan, PSBloansin59minutes), and paperless processing.
  • EASE 4.0 (2021): Collaborative banking, digital agricultural credit, and cloud adoption.
  • EASE 5.0 (2022): Digital customer experience and integrated fintech partnerships.
  • EASE 6.0 (2023–24): AI/ML analytics, predictive credit risk underwriting, and climate/ESG finance.

2. Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB - 2022)

  • Replaced the Banks Board Bureau (BBB) after a 2021 Delhi High Court ruling highlighted that BBB was not statutorily empowered to select General Managers and CMDs of public sector insurance companies.
  • Headed by a Chairman appointed by the Central Government, FSIB recommends CMDs, MDs, and Executive Directors for PSBs, NABARD, SIDBI, EXIM Bank, and Public Sector General Insurers.
Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: BBB vs FSIB: The Banks Board Bureau (BBB) no longer exists; it was superseded and reconstituted as the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB) in July 2022.
  • Trap 2: Count of PSBs: India currently has exactly 12 Public Sector Banks (SBI + 11 Nationalized Banks), down from 27 PSBs in 2017.
  • Trap 3: Merger Anchor Banks: Remember the exact 2020 merger pairs:
    • PNB acquired Oriental Bank of Commerce + United Bank of India.
    • Canara Bank acquired Syndicate Bank.
    • Union Bank of India acquired Andhra Bank + Corporation Bank.
    • Indian Bank acquired Allahabad Bank.
    • Bank of Baroda acquired Vijaya Bank + Dena Bank (2019).

Cross-Exam Focus

  • SEBI Grade A & RPSC RAS: PSB consolidation rationale, corporate governance depoliticization, and FSIB selection process.
