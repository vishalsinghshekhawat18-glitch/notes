Microfinance & Grassroots Collectives: SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (SHG-BLP), Joint Liability Groups (JLGs) & MFIs

The economic principles, social collateral dynamics, and institutional delivery models of microfinance in India. Dissects the **Self-Help Group-Bank Linkage Programme (SHG-BLP)** launched by NABARD in 1992 (now the world's largest community-based microfinance movement): voluntary groups of 10–20 rural women pooling small thrift savings, internal lending, and graduating to collateral-free bank credit (with repayment rates consistently > 96%). Contrasts SHGs with **Joint Liability Groups (JLGs)**: informal credit groups of 4–10 small/tenant farmers using mutual cross-guarantees for individual agricultural loans. Analyzes the **RBI Harmonized Regulatory Framework for Microfinance Loans (2022)**: defining microfinance as collateral-free loans to households with annual income up to ₹3.00 Lakh, with a maximum debt service limit of 50% of monthly household income.

Intuition & Context

Social Collateral: How 15 Women Defeated the Moneylender

If a poor landless woman in a village asks a commercial bank for a ₹30,000 loan to buy a sewing machine, the bank rejects her because she has no land title, no gold, and no credit history.\n\nShe is forced to go to the local moneylender, who charges an extortionate interest rate of 60% per year (55\% \text{ per month}5) and traps her in perpetual debt bondage.\n\nHow does microfinance solve this fundamental market failure?\n- 15 women from the village form a Self-Help Group (SHG).\n- For 6 months, every woman saves ₹100 every week into a common box. They lend small sums to each other for emergencies and repay on time.\n- The bank observes this discipline and gives the SHG a collateral-free loan of ₹3 Lakh!\n- Why does the bank feel safe without land collateral?\n Because if one woman skips a payment, the other 14 women in her village know immediately, visit her house, and ensure she resolves it. "Peer monitoring and social solidarity" replace brick-and-mortar collateral!

Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs): Aggregation Economics, Central Sector Scheme & Agrarian Value Chains

The collective business economics, legal structures, and institutional support mechanisms of Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) and Farmer Producer Companies (FPCs). Explores how FPOs solve the twin curses of smallholder agriculture—scale diseconomies in input procurement and weak bargaining power in output marketing—by aggregating individual smallholders into a unified corporate entity under the Companies Act, 2013 (Part IXA) or Cooperative Societies Acts. Dissects the **Central Sector Scheme for Formation and Promotion of 10,000 new FPOs** (launched in 2020 with ₹6,865 Cr budget): implementing agencies (SFAC, NABARD, NCDC), Cluster-Based Business Organisations (CBBOs), Equity Grant Scheme (matching equity up to ₹2,000 per member, maximum ₹15 Lakh per FPO), and the Credit Guarantee Facility (managed by NABSANRAKSHAN up to ₹2 Crore).

Intuition & Context

From 300 Price-Takers to 1 Business Enterprise

Consider a village with 300 tomato farmers. Each farmer grows 5 tons of tomatoes on a 1-acre plot:\n1. Input Disadvantage: When buying fertilizer, each farmer buys 5 bags at retail price (₹1,500/bag). They have zero negotiating power.\n2. Output Tragedy: When harvesting, 300 farmers rush to the local mandi simultaneously with overloaded tempo trucks. Wholesale traders cartelize and offer a distressed price of ₹3 per kg. Because tomatoes rot in 3 days, farmers are forced to accept or dump them on the highway.\n\nNow, imagine these 300 farmers incorporate a Farmer Producer Company (FPC):\n- Bulk Purchasing: The FPO buys 1,500 bags of fertilizer directly from the factory at wholesale distributor rates (₹1,100/bag), saving ₹400 per bag!\n- Direct Marketing: The FPO sets up a cold-storage sorting hub, packages branded tomatoes, and signs direct supply contracts with major supermarket chains and food processors at ₹15 per kg!\n- Profit Sharing: The profit is distributed back to the 300 farmers as dividends proportional to the tomatoes they contributed!\n\nThis is the economic magic of aggregation.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps\n- Trap 1: Minimum Membership: Minimum membership for an FPO under the Central Sector Scheme is 300 in plain areas and 100 in North-Eastern/Hilly/Desert areas (NOT 500 or 1,000!).\n- Trap 2: Maximum Equity Grant Ceiling: The maximum matching equity grant provided by the central government is ₹15.00 Lakh per FPO (subject to max ₹2,000 per individual member).\n- Trap 3: Voting Rights in Producer Companies: In a Producer Company, voting rights are strictly "One Member, One Vote", NOT proportional to share capital ownership (preventing corporate hostile takeovers by wealthy members).\n\n### Cross-Exam Focus\n- NABARD Grade A (ARD & ESI): Central Sector 10,000 FPOs scheme, CBBO role, SFAC, NABSANRAKSHAN credit guarantee (₹2 Cr), Alagh Committee, ODOFP.\n- RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Value chain financing, agricultural market risk mitigation, venture capital assistance via SFAC.\n- SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: FPO loans under Priority Sector Lending, Rajasthan FPO policy, matching equity grant limits.
