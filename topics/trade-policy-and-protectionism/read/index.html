Trade Policy, Tariffs, Quotas & Non-Tariff Barriers · Concept 1 of 2

Commercial Policy Instruments: Tariffs, Quotas, Subsidies & Partial Equilibrium Welfare Analysis

The economic anatomy and partial equilibrium welfare effects of commercial policy barriers. Analyzes specific, ad valorem, and compound tariffs, dissecting the four distinct welfare consequences in a small open economy: Consumer Surplus Loss, Producer Surplus Gain (Protective Effect), Government Tariff Revenue, and Deadweight Losses (Production Distortion Loss and Consumption Distortion Loss). Contrasts tariffs with Import Quotas (exploring Quota Rents and license allocation mechanisms) and Export Subsidies.

Intuition & Context

The Steel Tariff Dilemma: Who Pays for Protecting Domestic Mills?

Suppose imported steel is available in Mumbai at ₹40,000 per tonne. To protect domestic steel mills whose production cost is ₹50,000 per tonne, the government levies an Import Tariff of ₹10,000 per tonne.

What happens across the Indian economy?

  1. Domestic Steel Producers Win: They can now sell steel at ₹50,000 per tonne and expand their output (Producer Surplus increases).
  2. The Government Gains Revenue: It collects ₹10,000 per tonne on every imported tonne (Tariff Revenue).
  3. Domestic Consumers & Auto Manufacturers Lose: Automakers, construction firms, and consumers must pay ₹50,000 instead of ₹40,000 for every tonne of steel (Consumer Surplus drops significantly).

Does the gain to producers + government equal the loss to consumers? No. The consumer loss is strictly larger than the combined gains to producers and the government. The unrecovered gap is the Deadweight Loss to Society.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Tariffs in Large vs Small Countries: In a small country, a tariff always reduces national welfare (deadweight loss b+db + db+d). In a large country (capable of affecting world prices), a tariff can theoretically improve national welfare if the Terms of Trade gain (area eee) exceeds the deadweight loss (the Optimum Tariff argument).
  • Trap 2: Quota Rents Allocation: A quota does NOT yield government revenue unless the government auctions licenses. If licenses are given free to importers (First-Come-First-Served or historical quota), the revenue is captured as private Quota Rent.
  • Trap 3: Export Subsidies Make Goods Cheaper Domestically: False! An export subsidy makes the good more expensive domestically (Pd=Pw+sP_d = P_w + sPd​=Pw​+s) because domestic producers will refuse to sell locally unless local buyers match the subsidized export price.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Geometric decomposition of deadweight loss areas (a,b,c,da, b, c, da,b,c,d), quota rents, and optimum tariff theory.
  • UPSC APFC, IIBF DBF & RPSC RAS: Definitions of specific vs ad valorem tariffs, tariff-quota equivalents, and anti-dumping duties.

Arguments for Protectionism: Infant-Industry Thesis, Strategic Trade Policy & The Effective Rate of Protection (ERP)

The theoretical justifications and quantitative measurement of trade protectionism. Evaluates the Infant-Industry Argument (Friedrich List, Mill-Bastable test for dynamic learning-by-doing), Strategic Trade Policy (Brander-Spencer oligopoly profit-shifting model), and non-economic arguments (national defense, anti-dumping). Formulates the Effective Rate of Protection ($ERP = \\frac{t_f - a \\cdot t_i}{1 - a}$), proving why low tariffs on imported inputs combined with high tariffs on final outputs create massive effective subsidization for domestic value-adding firms.

Intuition & Context

Why a 10% Car Tariff Can Be a 50% Subsidy to Domestic Assembly

Suppose an imported car sells for ₹10,00,000, and the steel/engine parts required to build it cost ₹8,00,000 in international markets. The domestic value added (assembly, labor, design) is ₹2,00,000 (20% of the car's price).

Now consider the government's tariff structure:

  • Tariff on imported cars (tft_ftf​): 10%10%10% (imported car price rises to ₹11,00,000).
  • Tariff on imported parts (tit_iti​): 0%0%0% (parts remain at ₹8,00,000).

What happens to domestic car assemblers?
Domestic assemblers can now charge up to ₹11,00,000 for a car while spending only ₹8,00,000 on parts. Their allowable domestic value added has surged from ₹2,00,000 to ₹3,00,000—an increase of ₹1,00,000 or 50%!

Even though the nominal car tariff is only 10%10%10%, the Effective Rate of Protection (ERP) on domestic value adding labor is a massive 50%! This is why developing nations structure "cascading tariffs" (zero duty on raw materials, high duty on finished goods) to foster domestic industrialization.

Core Principle

Mathematical Derivation of the Effective Rate of Protection (ERP)

The Effective Rate of Protection is defined as the percentage increase in value added per unit of output made possible by the tariff structure compared to free trade:

ERP=V′−VVERP = \frac{V' - V}{V}ERP=VV′−V​

  • VVV = Value added under free trade per unit of output.
  • V′V'V′ = Value added under the tariff structure per unit of output.

The Corden-Balassa Formula

ERP=tf−a⋅ti1−aERP = \frac{t_f - a \cdot t_i}{1 - a}ERP=1−atf​−a⋅ti​​

  • tft_ftf​ = Nominal tariff rate on the final good.
  • tit_iti​ = Nominal tariff rate on imported intermediate inputs.
  • aaa = Ratio of the cost of imported inputs to the price of the final good under free trade (a=Input CostFinal Pricea = \frac{\text{Input Cost}}{\text{Final Price}}a=Final PriceInput Cost​).

Three Critical Tariff Structure Scenarios

  1. Cascading Tariffs (ti<tft_i < t_fti​<tf​)   ⟹  ERP>tf\implies ERP > t_f⟹ERP>tf​: Effective protection on domestic value added exceeds the nominal tariff on the finished good.
  2. Uniform Tariffs (ti=tft_i = t_fti​=tf​)   ⟹  ERP=tf\implies ERP = t_f⟹ERP=tf​: Effective protection equals the nominal tariff.
  3. Negative Effective Protection (ti>tfat_i > \frac{t_f}{a}ti​>atf​​)   ⟹  ERP<0\implies ERP < 0⟹ERP<0: High duties on raw materials with low duties on finished products actually tax and penalize domestic manufacturers relative to free trade!

Why Infant-Industry Protection Often Fails

In practice, infant industries rarely grow up. Political rent-seeking and regulatory capture cause temporary tariffs to become permanent subsidies, breeding uncompetitive domestic monopolies (e.g., India's Pre-1991 License-Permit Raj). Direct production subsidies are economically superior to tariffs because they do not distort consumer consumption choices.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Nominal vs Effective Protection: A 10% tariff on finished electronic goods does NOT mean the domestic assembly plant is protected by 10%. If parts are imported duty-free, the ERP can easily exceed 50% to 100%.
  • Trap 2: Negative ERP: If a country slaps a 30% duty on raw steel but only a 10% duty on finished machinery, domestic machine-makers face a Negative ERP—they are penalized compared to foreign competitors.
  • Trap 3: Bastable vs Mill Test: Mill's test requires the protected industry to eventually stand on its own feet. Bastable's test is stricter: it requires the net present discounted value of future gains to exceed the past deadweight welfare costs incurred during protection.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: ERP calculations (ERP=tf−ati1−aERP = \frac{t_f - a t_i}{1 - a}ERP=1−atf​−ati​​), Mill-Bastable criteria, and Brander-Spencer strategic trade theory.
  • UPSC APFC & RPSC RAS: Conceptual justifications for protectionism, anti-dumping duties, and India's Phased Manufacturing Programme (PMP).
