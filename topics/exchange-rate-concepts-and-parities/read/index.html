Nominal and Real Exchange Rates: NEER vs REER Indices & International Trade Competitiveness

The mathematical formulation and macroeconomic interpretation of external price competitiveness metrics. Distinguishes the bilateral Nominal Exchange Rate (NER) from the Real Exchange Rate ($RER = NER \\times \\frac{P_{\\text{domestic}}}{P_{\\text{foreign}}}$), and aggregates bilateral rates into multilateral trade-weighted indices: the Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER) and the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER). Analyzes the RBI's 40-currency trade-weighted index, proving why a rising REER ($REER > 100$) indicates domestic currency overvaluation and a loss of export price competitiveness.

Intuition & Context

The Illusion of a Falling Rupee: Why Inflation Neutralizes Depreciation

Suppose you export leather shoes from Agra to Berlin. In Year 1, the exchange rate is ₹80 / Euro, and your shoes cost ₹8,000 to manufacture. In Germany, your shoes sell for 100 Euros.

In Year 2, the Rupee depreciates by 10%10%10% to ₹88 / Euro. You celebrate, thinking your shoes will become cheaper in Germany! But during the year, Indian inflation was 10%10%10%, so your leather, labor, and electricity costs shot up from ₹8,000 to ₹8,800.

What is your price in Germany now? ₹8,800₹88=\frac{\text{₹}8,800}{\text{₹}88} =₹88₹8,800​= 100 Euros!

Even though the Nominal Exchange Rate depreciated by 10%, your Real Exchange Rate did not change at all because domestic inflation wiped out the currency gain. Your real export competitiveness in Berlin remained completely identical.

To eliminate bilateral distortions, central banks construct trade-weighted multilateral indices:

  • PPP: Domestic Consumer Price Index (CPI) of India.
  • PiP_iPi​: Consumer Price Index (CPI) of partner country iii.

3. Interpreting REER Benchmark Values (Base = 100)

  • REER = 100: Rupee is trading at fair real macroeconomic parity against trading partners.
  • REER > 100 (e.g., REER = 105): The Rupee is Overvalued by 5% in real terms. Indian goods are relatively more expensive abroad   ⟹  \implies⟹ Loss of export price competitiveness; imports become artificially cheaper.
  • REER < 100 (e.g., REER = 95): The Rupee is Undervalued by 5% in real terms. Indian goods are cheaper in global markets   ⟹  \implies⟹ Gain in export price competitiveness.

Why RBI Uses Consumer Price Index (CPI) for REER

Prior to 2014, the RBI calculated REER using the Wholesale Price Index (WPI). Under Governor Raghuram Rajan and the Urjit Patel Committee, the RBI shifted to CPI-based REER because CPI captures the true retail purchasing power and cost of living that drives domestic labor wages.

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Higher REER is Always Good: "A rising REER means the country is doing better." -> False: A rising REER indicates that the domestic currency is becoming overvalued, making domestic exports less competitive and worsening the trade deficit!
  • Trap 2: NEER Depreciation vs REER Appreciation: A country can experience NEER depreciation (nominal Rupee falling against USD/Euro) and simultaneously experience REER appreciation if domestic inflation exceeds foreign inflation by a margin larger than the nominal depreciation.
  • Trap 3: WPI vs CPI in Modern REER: The RBI officially compiles and reports CPI-based NEER and REER with base year 2015-16 = 100.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Geometric formula of REER, 40-currency basket composition, base year, and overvaluation diagnostics.
  • UPSC APFC, SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Conceptual difference between NEER and REER, and export competitiveness implications.

International Parity Conditions: Purchasing Power Parity (PPP), Balassa-Samuelson Effect & Interest Rate Parity

The theoretical equilibrium conditions governing long-run and short-run international price and asset relationships. Formulates the Law of One Price, Absolute Purchasing Power Parity ($S = \\frac{P}{P^*}$), Relative Purchasing Power Parity ($\\frac{\\Delta S}{S} \\approx \\pi - \\pi^*$), the Balassa-Samuelson Effect (explaining why developing economies exhibit lower price levels in non-tradable sectors, making PPP-adjusted GDP higher than market-exchange-rate GDP), Covered Interest Rate Parity (CIP: $\\frac{F - S}{S} = \\frac{i - i^*}{1 + i^*}$), and Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (UIP).

Intuition & Context

The Haircut Paradox: Why a ₹200 Haircut in Jaipur Costs USD 30 in New York

Why is a haircut in Jaipur priced at ₹200 (about US $2.40), while an identical haircut in Manhattan costs US $30 (about ₹2,500)?

  • Can you box up a Jaipur barber and export his service across the Atlantic to sell in Manhattan for US $30? No. Haircuts, restaurant dining, rent, and domestic transport are Non-Tradable Goods.
  • In Manhattan, high productivity in Wall Street and Silicon Valley drives up wages for everyone, including barbers.
  • In Jaipur, lower average industrial wages keep service prices very cheap.

Because non-tradables are cheap in developing countries, ₹84 in Jaipur buys far more food, haircuts, and domestic living than US $1 buys in New York.

This is why India's GDP at market exchange rates is simtextUSUSD4Trillionrillion\sim \text{US \USD 4 Trillionrillion}simtextUSUSD4Trillionrillion, but when measured in Purchasing Power Parity (PPP), India's GDP is simtextUSUSD14Trillionrillion\sim \text{US \USD 14 Trillionrillion}simtextUSUSD14Trillionrillion (the 3rd largest in the world behind China and the USA)! This insight is the Balassa-Samuelson Effect.

Core Principle

The Parity Theories Mathematical Taxonomy

International macroeconomics rests on three foundational parity conditions:

1. Absolute Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

S=PP∗S = \frac{P}{P^*}S=P∗P​

  • SSS: Spot exchange rate (Domestic currency per foreign currency unit, e.g. ₹/USD).
  • PPP: Domestic price level; P∗P^*P∗: Foreign price level.
  • Core Claim: 1 unit of currency converted at the spot rate buys the identical basket of goods in both countries (The Big Mac Index).

2. Relative Purchasing Power Parity (RPPP)

S1−S0S0≈π−π∗\frac{S_1 - S_0}{S_0} \approx \pi - \pi^*S0​S1​−S0​​≈π−π∗

  • π\piπ: Domestic inflation rate; π∗\pi^*π∗: Foreign inflation rate.
  • Core Claim: The percentage change in the exchange rate equals the inflation rate differential. If India's inflation is 6%6%6% and US inflation is 2%2%2%, the Rupee should depreciate by approximately 4%4%4% per year.

3. Covered Interest Rate Parity (CIP)

F−SS=i−i∗1+i∗≈i−i∗\frac{F - S}{S} = \frac{i - i^}{1 + i^} \approx i - i^*SF−S​=1+i∗i−i∗​≈i−i∗

  • FFF: Forward exchange rate; SSS: Spot exchange rate.
  • iii: Domestic interest rate (e.g. 7% in India); i∗i^*i∗: Foreign interest rate (e.g. 5% in US).
  • Core Claim: The forward currency premium/discount eliminates riskless foreign exchange arbitrage. If India's interest rates are 2% higher than US rates, the US Dollar must trade at a 2% Forward Premium against the Rupee (F>SF > SF>S).

Why PPP Fails in the Short Run

In reality, PPP does not hold in the short run due to: (1) Transport costs and import tariffs, (2) Non-tradable goods (haircuts, real estate), (3) Sticky nominal prices, and (4) Massive speculative cross-border capital flows (>90%> 90%>90% of daily forex turnover is financial investment, not goods trade).

Exam Application & Traps

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Absolute vs Relative PPP: Absolute PPP requires price levels to be identical (S=P/P∗S = P/P^S=P/P∗). Relative PPP requires only that the change in exchange rate matches the inflation differential (%ΔS = π - π). Relative PPP can hold even if Absolute PPP fails due to transport costs.
  • Trap 2: Forward Premium Rule: If domestic interest rate (iii) is HIGHER than foreign interest rate (i∗i^*i∗), the foreign currency must trade at a Forward Premium (F>SF > SF>S), and the domestic currency trades at a forward discount.
  • Trap 3: Market GDP vs PPP GDP Ranking: India is the 5th largest economy by Market Exchange Rate GDP (surpassing UK), but the 3rd largest economy by PPP GDP (surpassing Japan and Germany) due to the Balassa-Samuelson effect on cheap non-tradables.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: CIP forward premium calculation, RPPP inflation differentials, and Balassa-Samuelson mechanism.
  • UPSC APFC & RPSC RAS: Market GDP vs PPP GDP definitions and Law of One Price assumptions.
