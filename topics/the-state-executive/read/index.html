Part VI: State Executive — Governor, CM & Advocate General · Concept 1 of 4

Governor of the State: Constitutional Dual Role, Appointment & Removal

The Governor as the constitutional Head of State and representative of the Centre (Art 153-154), appointment under presidential warrant (Art 155), pleasure tenure (Art 156), and landmark judicial restraints against arbitrary removal (B.P. Singhal 2010).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Governor of the State

Think of Governor of the State as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

The Dual Persona of the State Governor

The Governor of a State (Article 153) occupies a unique dual role in Indian asymmetric federalism:

  1. Constitutional Head of the State Executive (Article 154): Acts on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister.
  2. Representative / Agent of the Union Government: Acts as a constitutional link between the Centre and the State, reporting on the constitutional machinery of the State under Article 356.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Appointment, Qualifications & Pleasure Doctrine

Constitutional Provisions (Articles 153–158):

• Article 153: There shall be a Governor for each State (7th CAA 1956 allows same person for 2+ states).
• Article 155: Appointed by the President of India by warrant under hand and seal (Canadian model of appointed Governor, rejecting the US elected model to prevent dual power centers in the state).
• Article 156 (Tenure): Holds office during the pleasure of the President. Normal term is 5 years, but no security of tenure is guaranteed by the text.
• Qualifications (Article 157): Citizen of India, completed 35 years of age.
• Conventions (Sarkaria & Punchhi Commissions): Should be an outsider to the state; should not be intimately connected with local state politics; Chief Minister of the state should be consulted before appointment.

Landmark Removal Jurisprudence (B.P. Singhal v. UOI, 2010):

• A change in the central government cannot be a valid ground to remove Governors en masse.
• The President need not state reasons in the removal order, but must have valid, compelling, and non-arbitrary reasons.
• If a removed Governor challenges the removal alleging mala fides, the Court can call upon the Union Government to produce the records justifying the removal.

Exam Application & Traps

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:\n\n1. Article & Nomenclature Confusion: Examiners frequently confuse identical sounding powers across Union and State levels (e.g. Art 123 vs Art 213; Art 72 vs Art 161). Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Constitutional vs Statutory Distinction: Do not confuse bodies created by constitutional articles (e.g. UPSC, Finance Commission, ECI) with statutory watchdogs created by ordinary Acts of Parliament (e.g. NGT, CVC, Lokpal, NHRC).\n\n4. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

🏛️ 5-Minute Architecture & Key Revision

The appointment of Governors by the President rather than by state-level elections was a deliberate constitutional choice by the Constituent Assembly to safeguard national unity, prevent state separatist tendencies, and preserve the smooth operation of cabinet governance without conflicting mandates between Governor and Chief Minister.

Governor's Discretionary Powers (Art 163), Bill Reservation (Arts 200–201) & Ordinance (Art 213)

The explicit constitutional and situational discretion of the Governor under Article 163, mandatory floor test jurisprudence (S.R. Bommai), reservation of state bills for Presidential consideration (Arts 200-201), and state ordinance power (Art 213).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Governor's Discretionary Powers (Art 163), Bill Reservation (Arts 200–201) & Ordinance (Art 213)

Think of Governor's Discretionary Powers (Art 163), Bill Reservation (Arts 200–201) & Ordinance (Art 213) as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Explicit Discretion in State Governance vs Presidential Model

A fundamental distinction between the Union and State executive is that the President of India has no express constitutional discretionary powers (Art 74 has no discretionary clause), whereas Article 163(1) explicitly recognizes the Governor’s discretion.

This explicit discretion empowers the Governor to act independently of the State Cabinet when national unity, constitutional machinery, or institutional integrity are at stake.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Constitutional vs Situational Discretion (Article 163)

1. Constitutional Discretion (Explicit in Text):

• Reservation of a Bill for President (Article 200): Governor can reserve any state bill for presidential assent.
• Recommending President’s Rule (Article 356): Sending reports to the President on the breakdown of constitutional machinery.
• Sixth Schedule Tribal Districts: Determining royalties from mineral licenses in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram.
• Special Responsibilities (Articles 371–371J): Maharashtra/Gujarat (development boards), Nagaland (law & order), Manipur (hill areas), etc.

2. Situational Discretion (Emergent Political Scenarios):

• Appointment of Chief Minister: In a hung assembly when no party has a clear majority.
• Dismissal of Council of Ministers: When the ministry loses majority in the Assembly and refuses to resign.
• Dissolution of Legislative Assembly: When the council of ministers loses its majority.

Landmark Judicial Restraints:

• S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): The Supreme Court held that the floor of the Legislative Assembly (Floor Test) is the ONLY legitimate arena to test majority, not the subjective assessment of the Governor in Raj Bhavan.
• Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016): Governor cannot summon, prorogue, or dissolve the Assembly without the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers under Article 174, unless the government’s majority is in active doubt.

DISTINCTION

Bill Assent (Arts 200–201), Ordinance (Art 213) & Pardoning (Art 161)

Article 200 & 201 (State Bill Assent):

• Governor has 4 choices under Art 200: (1) Give assent, (2) Withhold assent, (3) Return for reconsideration (if not Money Bill), (4) Reserve for President.
• Mandatory Reservation: A bill that endangers the constitutional position of the High Court MUST be reserved for the President.
• President’s Action under Art 201: The President may give assent, withhold assent, or return the bill. Crucial Rule: Even if the State Legislature re-passes the bill, the President is NOT BOUND to give assent (unlike the Union Suspensive Veto under Art 111).

Article 213 (State Ordinance Power):

• Promulgated when state legislature is in recess; max life 6 weeks from reassembly.
• Presidential Instruction Needed: If the bill would have required Presidential previous sanction or reservation under Article 200.

Article 161 vs Article 72 (Pardoning Power):

• Death Sentence: The Governor CANNOT pardon a death sentence (only the President can under Art 72). The Governor can only suspend, remit, or commute a death sentence.
• Court Martial: The Governor has no power regarding punishment by Court Martial.

Chief Minister & State Council of Ministers: Constitutional Architecture

The Chief Minister as the real state executive, collective responsibility to the Legislative Assembly (Art 164), 91st Amendment size limits (15% ceiling, minimum 12), and aid & advice jurisprudence (Shamsher Singh, S.R. Bommai).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Chief Minister & State Council of Ministers

Think of Chief Minister & State Council of Ministers as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

The Real Executive Head of State Administration

The Chief Minister is the Head of Government at the State level and the real executive (De Facto), while the Governor is the constitutional Head of State (De Jure). The Chief Minister commands the majority in the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha), leads the Council of Ministers, and acts as the sole constitutional link between the State Cabinet and the Governor (Article 167).

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Constitutional Architecture: Articles 163, 164 & 167

1. Appointment and Size (Article 164):

• Article 164(1): The Chief Minister is appointed by the Governor, and other Ministers are appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister.
• 91st Amendment (2003) — Dual Threshold (Article 164(1A)):

  1. Maximum Ceiling: Total Ministers (incl. CM) shall not exceed 15% of the total strength of the Legislative Assembly.
  2. Minimum Floor: Number of Ministers (incl. CM) shall NOT be less than 12 Ministers.
    (Example: In Rajasthan with 200 MLAs, max ministers = $200 \times 15% = 30$; min ministers = 12).
    • Article 164(4): A minister who is not a member of the State Legislature for 6 consecutive months ceases to be a minister.

2. Collective Responsibility (Article 164(2)):

• The State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible strictly to the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha).
• In bicameral states, ministers can be from either Assembly or Council, but the ministry is responsible ONLY to the Assembly (the Legislative Council cannot pass a No-Confidence Motion).

3. Duties of Chief Minister (Article 167):

• Communicate all decisions of the Council of Ministers to the Governor.
• Furnish information regarding state administration and legislative proposals as the Governor calls for.
• Submit for the consideration of the Council of Ministers any matter on which a decision has been taken by an individual minister without Cabinet consideration.

Advocate General for the State (Article 165) & Rights in Legislature (Art 177)

The Advocate General as the highest law officer in the State (Art 165), qualified as a High Court Judge, holding office during governor’s pleasure, with the constitutional right to participate in State Legislature proceedings without voting (Art 177).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Advocate General for the State (Article 165) & Rights in Legislature (Art 177)

Think of Advocate General for the State (Article 165) & Rights in Legislature (Art 177) as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Highest Legal Officer of the State Government

The Advocate General for the State (Article 165) is the highest law officer in the State executive. Corresponding to the Attorney General of India at the federal level, the Advocate General acts as the chief legal counsel to the State Government, advising on legal matters and representing the State in litigations before the High Court and the Supreme Court.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Appointment, Functions & Legislative Rights (Article 177)

Qualifications & Appointment (Article 165):

• Appointed by the Governor on the advice of the State Cabinet.
• Must be qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court (Citizen of India, held judicial office in India for 10 years or been an advocate in a High Court for 10 years).
• Tenure & Removal: Holds office during the pleasure of the Governor (no fixed term in Constitution). Resigns conventionally when the State Ministry resigns.
• Remuneration: Determined by the Governor (not fixed by Constitution).

Rights in State Legislature (Article 177):

• Has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) and State Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad), and any Assembly committee of which named a member.
• ⚠️ CRUCIAL LIMITATION: Has NO RIGHT TO VOTE.
• Enjoys all privileges and immunities of an MLA (Article 194(4)).
