Part III Overview & Nature of Fundamental Rights

Part III of the Indian Constitution (Articles 12–35), known as the Magna Carta of India, guarantees justifiable basic political and civil rights against State encroachment.

Intuition & Context

Why Fundamental Rights Exist

Fundamental Rights represent the basic civic charter securing individual liberty, equality, and human dignity against arbitrary majoritarian or executive tyranny. They establish a "government of laws and not of men".

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Part III Overview & Nature of Fundamental Rights

Most Fundamental Rights are negative prohibitions telling the State what it CANNOT do (e.g. Art 14: "The State shall not deny...", Art 15: "The State shall not discriminate..."). Conversely, Article 21A imposes a positive duty on the State to provide free and compulsory education.

Analogy: A constitutional shield held by the individual against the sword of sovereign authority.
Core Insight: Rights are inherent limitations on State power, not privileges granted by the government of the day.
Where Analogy Breaks Down: Unlike an absolute shield, Fundamental Rights are not absolute and are subject to constitutional reasonable restrictions.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Negative Restraints vs Positive Mandates

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

CONSTITUTIONAL TEXT

6 Categories of Guaranteed Rights

  1. Right to Equality (Arts 14–18)
  2. Right to Freedom (Arts 19–22)
  3. Right against Exploitation (Arts 23–24)
  4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Arts 25–28)
  5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Arts 29–30)
  6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art 32).

(Note: Right to Property under Arts 19(1)(f) and 31 was deleted by the 44th Amendment 1978 and relocated to Article 300A in Part XII).

Article 12 — Definition of State & Instrumentalities

Article 12 defines the term "State" for Part III enforcement, encompassing Union/State executives and legislatures, local authorities, statutory instrumentalities, and the administrative side of the Judiciary.

Intuition & Context

Purpose of Article 12: Identifying the Duty-Bearer

Because Fundamental Rights are primarily shields against sovereign power, Article 12 specifies precisely which entities are legally bound not to violate them.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Article 12 — Definition of State & Instrumentalities

  1. Entire share capital held by Government
  2. Financial assistance meeting almost entire expenditure
  3. Monopoly status conferred or protected by State
  4. Deep and pervasive State control
  5. Functions of public importance closely related to governmental functions
  6. Transfer of a government department to a corporation.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

The Ajay Hasia 6-Factor Instrumentality Test

  1. Entire share capital held by Government
  2. Financial assistance meeting almost entire expenditure
  3. Monopoly status conferred or protected by State

CASE ANALYSIS

Dual Status of the Judiciary & Autonomous Bodies

Is the Judiciary "State"?
• Administrative Side: YES (rules, staff appointments, registry tenders are subject to writ jurisdiction under Art 32/226).
• Judicial Side: NO (judicial orders of a competent court cannot be challenged via Art 32 writ petition claiming the judge violated Part III; remedy lies in appellate review or curative petition under Art 137/142 — Naresh Mirajkar 1967, Rupa Hurra 2002).

Private Bodies Performing Public Functions:
• Statutory bodies (LIC, ONGC, SAIL, SBI) = State.
• Private autonomous bodies (BCCI, NCERT) = Outside Art 12 (Zee Telefilms 2005).

Article 13 — Judicial Review & Constitutional Doctrines

Article 13 invalidates pre- and post-constitutional laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights and establishes the constitutional foundation for judicial review through the Doctrines of Severability, Eclipse, and Waiver.

Intuition & Context

Article 13 as the Guardian Clause

Article 13 is the constitutional gatekeeper: it gives teeth to Fundamental Rights by ensuring any conflicting legislative or executive measure is null and void.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Article 13 — Judicial Review & Constitutional Doctrines

  1. Severability: Blue pencil test — prune the diseased branch, preserve the tree.
  2. Eclipse: Pre-constitutional law is shadowed, not dead; a constitutional amendment can revive it.
  3. Waiver: Fundamental Rights cannot be surrendered by private consent in India (Basheshar Nath 1959).
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Doctrinal Triptych: Severability, Eclipse, and Waiver

  1. Severability: Blue pencil test — prune the diseased branch, preserve the tree.
  2. Eclipse: Pre-constitutional law is shadowed, not dead; a constitutional amendment can revive it.
  3. Waiver: Fundamental Rights cannot be surrendered by private consent in India (Basheshar Nath 1959).

Article 14 — Right to Equality & Non-Arbitrariness

Article 14 commands that the State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India, prohibiting class legislation while permitting reasonable classification.

Intuition & Context

Core Concept: Equals Treated Equally, Unequals Treated Unequally

Article 14 does not demand universal mechanical uniformity; it forbids discriminatory favouritism while allowing affirmative support for disadvantaged groups.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Article 14 — Right to Equality & Non-Arbitrariness

  1. Traditional Test: Intelligible Differentia + Rational Nexus.
  2. Modern Test: Absence of arbitrariness, proportionality, and adherence to natural justice.
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Dual Evolution: Traditional Classification to Modern Non-Arbitrariness

  1. Traditional Test: Intelligible Differentia + Rational Nexus.
  2. Modern Test: Absence of arbitrariness, proportionality, and adherence to natural justice.

Articles 15 & 16 — Protective Discrimination & Reservation Jurisprudence

Articles 15 and 16 prohibit discrimination on specified grounds while enabling affirmative action, creamy layer exclusions, EWS quotas (103rd Amendment), and reservation in public employment and education.

Intuition & Context

Substantive Equality vs Formal Equality

Articles 15 and 16 operationalize substantive equality by recognizing that treating unequals equally perpetuates inequality, justifying constitutional affirmative action.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Articles 15 & 16 — Protective Discrimination & Reservation Jurisprudence

• Step 1: Champakam Dorairajan (1951) →\rightarrow→ Supreme Court strikes down communal G.O.; Parliament responds with 1st Amendment (1951) inserting Article 15(4).
• Step 2: Indra Sawhney (1992) →\rightarrow→ 9-judge bench upholds 27% OBC quota; caps total reservation at 50%; mandates Creamy Layer exclusion; bars reservation in promotions.
• Step 3: 77th, 81st & 85th Amendments (1995–2001) →\rightarrow→ Inserts Art 16(4A) for SC/ST promotion quotas, Art 16(4B) exempting backlog vacancies from 50% cap, and consequential seniority.
• Step 4: M. Nagaraj (2006) & Jarnail Singh (2018) →\rightarrow→ Upholds promotion quotas subject to quantifiable backwardness data; extends Creamy Layer principle to SC/ST promotions.
• Step 5: 103rd Amendment & Janhit Abhiyan (2022) →\rightarrow→ 10% EWS quota based solely on economic disadvantage upheld (does not breach 50% cap because 50% rule applies to social reservation).
• Step 6: Davinder Singh (2024) →\rightarrow→ 7-judge bench overrules E.V. Chinnaiah (2004), empowering States to sub-classify SCs and STs for preferential intra-quota benefits.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

The 6-Step Chronological Ladder of Indian Reservation Jurisprudence

• Step 1: Champakam Dorairajan (1951) →\rightarrow→ Supreme Court strikes down communal G.O.; Parliament responds with 1st Amendment (1951) inserting Article 15(4).
• Step 2: Indra Sawhney (1992) →\rightarrow→ 9-judge bench upholds 27% OBC quota; caps total reservation at 50%; mandates Creamy Layer exclusion; bars reservation in promotions.
• Step 3: 77th, 81st & 85th Amendments (1995–2001) →\rightarrow→ Inserts Art 16(4A) for SC/ST promotion quotas, Art 16(4B) exempting backlog vacancies from 50% cap, and consequential seniority.
• Step 4: M. Nagaraj (2006) & Jarnail Singh (2018) →\rightarrow→ Upholds promotion quotas subject to quantifiable backwardness data; extends Creamy Layer principle to SC/ST promotions.
• Step 5: 103rd Amendment & Janhit Abhiyan (2022) →\rightarrow→ 10% EWS quota based solely on economic disadvantage upheld (does not breach 50% cap because 50% rule applies to social reservation).
• Step 6: Davinder Singh (2024) →\rightarrow→ 7-judge bench overrules E.V. Chinnaiah (2004), empowering States to sub-classify SCs and STs for preferential intra-quota benefits.

Articles 17 & 18 — Abolition of Untouchability & Titles

Article 17 absolutely abolishes untouchability in any form, enforceable against the State and private individuals, while Article 18 abolishes aristocratic titles to establish civic equality.

Intuition & Context

Eradicating Feudal and Caste Hierarchies

Articles 17 and 18 dismantle inherited feudal privileges and caste hierarchies, establishing that all Indian citizens stand in equal civic brotherhood without hereditary nobility or ritual subjugation.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Articles 17 & 18 — Abolition of Untouchability & Titles

Article 17 is one of the few Fundamental Rights available directly against private individuals (horizontal effect), backed by statutory penalties under the PCR Act 1955 and Atrocities Act 1989. It has NO exceptions and cannot be suspended.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Article 17: Self-Executing Absolute Prohibition

Article 17 is one of the few Fundamental Rights available directly against private individuals (horizontal effect), backed by statutory penalties under the PCR Act 1955 and Atrocities Act 1989. It has NO exceptions and cannot be suspended.

Article 32 guarantees the fundamental right to approach the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Part III rights, empowering the Court to issue 5 prerogative writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto.

Intuition & Context

A Right Without a Remedy is a Meaningless Declaration

Article 32 makes Fundamental Rights real by providing a direct, non-derogable constitutional highway to the highest court of the land.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Article 32 & Writs — Right to Constitutional Remedies

Writ Literal Meaning Against Whom Issued When NOT Issued
Habeas Corpus "To have the body of" Public authorities & Private individuals Lawful detention, contempt of court/legislature, detention outside jurisdiction
Mandamus "We command" Public officials, statutory bodies, lower courts, Government Private individuals, discretionary duties, contractual obligations, President/Governor
Prohibition "To forbid" Judicial and Quasi-judicial authorities only Administrative, legislative, or private bodies
Certiorari "To be certified" Judicial, Quasi-judicial, and Administrative authorities affecting rights Legislative bodies and private individuals
Quo Warranto "By what authority" Public offices of substantive character created by statute/Constitution Ministerial offices or private private offices
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Procedural Mechanics & Statutory Workflow

The procedural execution of this provision follows a strict constitutional workflow:\n1. Initiation & Authority: Action originates strictly under the designated constitutional article or statutory section.\n2. Procedural Checks & Balances: Mandatory consultations, parliamentary majorities, or judicial safeguards must be satisfied prior to implementation.\n3. Legal Consequences & Remedies: Non-compliance renders the act ultra vires, opening avenues for judicial review under Articles 32 and 226.

Exam Application & Traps

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:\n\n1. Article & Nomenclature Confusion: Examiners frequently confuse identical sounding powers across Union and State levels (e.g. Art 123 vs Art 213; Art 72 vs Art 161). Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Constitutional vs Statutory Distinction: Do not confuse bodies created by constitutional articles (e.g. UPSC, Finance Commission, ECI) with statutory watchdogs created by ordinary Acts of Parliament (e.g. NGT, CVC, Lokpal, NHRC).\n\n4. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

Articles 33–35 & Amendability — Limitations & Basic Structure

Articles 33–35 empower Parliament to modify Fundamental Rights for armed forces, indemnify acts during Martial Law, and exclusively legislate for Part III, framed within the historical amendability debate from Shankari Prasad (1951) to Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Minerva Mills (1980).

Intuition & Context

National Security Discipline & Constitutional Balance

Articles 33–35 preserve the operational integrity of security forces and legislative uniformity, while the Basic Structure doctrine prevents legislative destruction of core constitutional freedoms.

Core Principle

Core Constitutional Architecture: Articles 33–35 & Amendability — Limitations & Basic Structure

  1. Shankari Prasad (1951): Article 368 includes power to amend Part III.
  2. Golaknath (1967): 11-judge bench holds Part III is transcendental; Art 368 is only procedure.
  3. 24th Amendment (1971): Parliament asserts power to amend any part of Constitution.
  4. Kesavananda Bharati (1973): 13-judge bench establishes Basic Structure Doctrine; Parliament can amend Part III without destroying basic features.
  5. Minerva Mills (1980): Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are two wheels of a chariot; neither can destroy the other.
