The Union Legislature: Parliament Architecture, Budget & Procedures · Concept 1 of 6

Parliament Architecture: Bicameralism (Art 79), Composition & Sessions

The tripartite structure of Parliament (President, Rajya Sabha, Lok Sabha under Art 79), federal composition of Rajya Sabha (Art 80), democratic representation in Lok Sabha (Art 81), and constitutional rules for sessions, prorogation, and quorum (Arts 83, 85, 100).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Parliament Architecture

Think of Parliament Architecture as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

The Tripartite Bicameral Design of the Union Legislature

Under Article 79, the Parliament of India is not merely the two legislative chambers; it is a tripartite institution comprising:

  1. The President of India
  2. The Council of States (Rajya Sabha)
  3. The House of the People (Lok Sabha)

Although the President does not sit or participate in regular debates, the President is an indispensable component of Parliament because no bill passed by both Houses can become law without Presidential Assent (Article 111), and the President exercises exclusive constitutional prerogatives to summon, prorogue, and dissolve the Lok Sabha (Article 85).

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Nominated Members, Sessions & Quorum Rules

Nominated Members in Rajya Sabha (Article 80(3)):

• The President nominates 12 members having special knowledge or practical experience in:

  1. Literature
  2. Science
  3. Art
  4. Social Service
    • ⚠️ EXAM TRAP: "Sports" is NOT an explicit constitutional category in Art 80(3); Sachin Tendulkar was nominated under "Art/Social Service". Furthermore, "Cooperative Movement" is listed in State Governor nominations under Article 171, but is NOT in Rajya Sabha Art 80(3).

Parliamentary Sessions & Quorum (Articles 85 & 100):

• Sessions: President summons each House. Maximum permissible gap between two sessions shall not exceed 6 months (Parliament must meet at least twice a year; conventionally meets in 3 sessions: Budget, Monsoon, Winter).
• Adjournment vs Prorogation vs Dissolution:

  • Adjournment: Suspends sitting for hours/days/weeks; done by the Presiding Officer (Speaker/Chairman).
  • Adjournment Sine Die: Terminates sitting without naming a day for reassembly; done by the Presiding Officer.
  • Prorogation: Terminates a session of the House; done by the President of India under Article 85(2)(a).
  • Dissolution: Ends the very life of the Lok Sabha; done by the President of India under Article 85(2)(b) (Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved).
    • Quorum (Article 100(3)): Minimum 10% (1/10th) of the total membership of the House required to constitute a meeting (55 members in Lok Sabha, 25 members in Rajya Sabha).
Exam Application & Traps

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:\n\n1. Article & Nomenclature Confusion: Examiners frequently confuse identical sounding powers across Union and State levels (e.g. Art 123 vs Art 213; Art 72 vs Art 161). Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Constitutional vs Statutory Distinction: Do not confuse bodies created by constitutional articles (e.g. UPSC, Finance Commission, ECI) with statutory watchdogs created by ordinary Acts of Parliament (e.g. NGT, CVC, Lokpal, NHRC).\n\n4. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

Presiding Officers: Speaker vs Chairman (Arts 89, 93) & Disqualifications

The constitutional roles and powers of the Speaker of Lok Sabha and Chairman of Rajya Sabha (Arts 89, 93), casting vote (Art 100), Money Bill certification (Art 110(3)), and the dual disqualification frameworks under Article 102 vs the 10th Schedule (Kihoto Hollohan).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Presiding Officers

Think of Presiding Officers as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Custodians of Parliamentary Sovereignty and House Order

The Presiding Officers—the Speaker of the Lok Sabha (Article 93) and the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Article 89)—act as the constitutional guardians of the dignity, order, and procedural sovereignty of their respective chambers.

While the Speaker is elected from amongst the members of the Lok Sabha, the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is the Vice-President of India ex-officio (Article 64) and is therefore NOT a member of the Rajya Sabha.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Dual Disqualification Architecture: Article 102 vs 10th Schedule

1. Constitutional Disqualifications (Article 102(1)):

• Holds any Office of Profit under the Government of India or State Government (unless exempted by Parliament under the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959).
• Declared of unsound mind by a competent court.
• Undischarged insolvent.
• Not a citizen of India, or voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship.
• Disqualified by any law made by Parliament (e.g. Representation of the People Act, 1951 — conviction for ≥2\ge 2≥2 years under Sec 8(3)).
• Decision-Making Authority (Article 103): The President of India decides. Crucial Rule: The President MUST obtain the opinion of the Election Commission of India (ECI) and ACT ACCORDING TO SUCH OPINION (the ECI opinion is strictly binding on the President).

2. Anti-Defection Disqualifications (10th Schedule / 52nd CAA 1985):

• Voluntarily gives up party membership; votes or abstains contrary to party whip without 15-day condonation; independent member joins any political party; nominated member joins a political party after 6 months.
• Decision-Making Authority: The Speaker of Lok Sabha or Chairman of Rajya Sabha.
• Judicial Review (Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu, 1992): The Presiding Officer acts as a Tribunal while deciding anti-defection petitions. The decision is subject to Judicial Review by High Courts and Supreme Court on grounds of mala fides, perversity, or violation of natural justice (though courts will not intervene before the Presiding Officer makes a formal decision).

Legislative Enactments: Classification of Bills (Arts 107–110, 117) & Joint Sittings (Art 108)

The four-tier classification of parliamentary bills (Ordinary, Money, Financial Types I & II), Speaker’s Money Bill certification, Article 108 Joint Sittings architecture with strict exclusions (Money Bills & CAAs), and Article 107 rules on the lapse of bills upon dissolution.

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Legislative Enactments

Think of Legislative Enactments as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

The Four Categories of Parliamentary Legislation

The Constitution establishes four distinct classes of legislative bills, each subjected to different procedural rules, introduction chambers, presidential recommendations, and Rajya Sabha powers:

  1. Ordinary Bills (Articles 107, 108)
  2. Money Bills (Articles 109, 110)
  3. Financial Bills — Category I (Article 117(1))
  4. Financial Bills — Category II (Article 117(3))
    (Constitutional Amendment Bills under Article 368 constitute an independent fifth sovereign category).
Mechanism & Operational Rule

Joint Sitting Architecture (Article 108) & Dissolution Lapse Rules (Article 107)

Joint Sitting (Article 108):

• Trigger: If after a bill is passed by one House and sent to the other: (a) rejected by the other House, (b) Houses disagree on amendments, or (c) more than 6 months elapse without being passed.
• Summoned by: The President of India by notification.
• Presided by: Speaker of Lok Sabha →\rightarrow→ in absence, Deputy Speaker →\rightarrow→ in absence, Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha (⚠️ Chairman of Rajya Sabha NEVER presides).
• Majority Required: Simple Majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting (Lok Sabha’s numerical superiority of 543 vs 245 usually guarantees passage).
• Strict Bars: NEVER held for Money Bills (Art 109) or Constitutional Amendment Bills (Art 368).

Rules on Lapse of Bills upon Lok Sabha Dissolution (Article 107):

• BILLS THAT LAPSE:

  1. A Bill pending in the Lok Sabha (whether originated in LS or transmitted from RS).
  2. A Bill passed by the Lok Sabha but pending in the Rajya Sabha.
    • BILLS THAT DO NOT LAPSE:
  3. A Bill originated in the Rajya Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha (not yet passed by Lok Sabha).
  4. A Bill passed by BOTH Houses and waiting for the Assent of the President.
  5. A Bill passed by BOTH Houses and returned by the President for reconsideration.
  6. A Bill where the President has already notified intention to summon a Joint Sitting before dissolution.

Parliamentary Financial Control: Budget Procedure & Cut Motions (Arts 112–116)

The six-stage procedure of the Union Budget (Annual Financial Statement under Art 112), distinction between Charged and Voted expenditure, Demands for Grants (Art 113), the three Cut Motions (Policy, Economy, Token), the Guillotine, and Appropriation vs Finance Bills.

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Parliamentary Financial Control

Think of Parliamentary Financial Control as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

The Constitutional Power of the Purse (Articles 265 & 266)

Parliamentary democracy is founded on the principle that the Executive cannot levy taxes or spend public revenue without explicit statutory authorization from Parliament:

  1. Article 265: "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."
  2. Article 266(3): "No moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India shall be appropriated except in accordance with law and for the purposes and in the manner provided in this Constitution."

The word "Budget" does not appear anywhere in the Constitution; Article 112 designates it as the Annual Financial Statement (AFS).

Mechanism & Operational Rule

The 6 Stages of the Budget in Parliament

  1. Stage 1: Presentation of Budget (Article 112): Presented by the Finance Minister in the Lok Sabha on 1st February (since 2017 reform; Railway Budget merged with Union Budget). No discussion on day of presentation.
  2. Stage 2: General Discussion: Broad fiscal and macroeconomic principles discussed for 3–4 days in both Houses. No voting; no cut motions allowed.
  3. Stage 3: Scrutiny by Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs): Parliament adjourns for 3–4 weeks. The 24 DRSCs scrutinize detailed Demands for Grants of all ministries and submit reports to both Houses.
  4. Stage 4: Voting on Demands for Grants (Article 113): EXCLUSIVE TO LOK SABHA (Rajya Sabha has no voting power). Cut Motions are moved. On the final allotted day, the Speaker applies the "Guillotine" (putting all remaining undiscussed demands to vote together).
  5. Stage 5: Passage of Appropriation Bill (Article 114): Authorizes the legal withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India for both Voted grants and Charged expenditure.
  6. Stage 6: Passage of Finance Bill: Enacts the government’s taxation proposals to legalise revenue collection (must be passed within 75 days under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931).

DISTINCTION

Charged vs Voted Expenditure & The 3 Cut Motions

Charged vs Voted Expenditure (Article 112(3)):

• Charged Expenditure (Non-Votable): Discussed by both Houses, but NOT voted on. Includes:

  • Emoluments of President, Vice-President, Speaker/Deputy Speaker, Chairman/Deputy Chairman.
  • Salaries, allowances, and pensions of Supreme Court Judges.
  • Salaries, allowances, and pensions of the CAG and UPSC Members.
  • ⚠️ CRITICAL EXAM TRAP: High Court Judges’ Salaries are charged on the State Consolidated Fund, but their Pensions are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India!
    • Voted Expenditure: Voted upon as Demands for Grants exclusively in the Lok Sabha.

The 3 Cut Motions in Lok Sabha:

  1. Policy Disapproval Cut: "That the amount of the demand be reduced to ₹1." (Represents complete disapproval of underlying policy; member must advocate an alternative policy).
  2. Economy Cut: "That the amount of the demand be reduced by a specified amount." (Proposes economy in stated expenditure).
  3. Token Cut: "That the amount of the demand be reduced by ₹100." (Ventilates a specific grievance within government responsibility).
    • ⚠️ CONSTITUTIONAL IMPLICATION: If ANY Cut Motion is passed in Lok Sabha, it amounts to a Vote of No-Confidence, and the Council of Ministers MUST resign!

Parliamentary Standing Committees: PAC (1921), Estimates (1950), COPU (1964) & DRSCs

The three premier Financial Standing Committees (Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee with 30 LS-only members, Committee on Public Undertakings), the 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), and the constitutional ban on ministerial membership.

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Parliamentary Standing Committees

Think of Parliamentary Standing Committees as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Committees as the "Eyes and Ears" of Parliament

Because Parliament is too large a body and lacks the specialized time to examine complex legislative bills, fiscal expenditures, and executive administration in detail, it functions through a network of Parliamentary Committees appointed or elected under Article 118(1).

A committee is a Parliamentary Committee only if: (1) Appointed/elected by the House or nominated by the Presiding Officer; (2) Works under direction of the Presiding Officer; (3) Presents report to the House/Presiding Officer; (4) Has a secretariat provided by Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha.

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Departmentally Related Standing Committees (24 DRSCs)

• Structure: Initiated in 1993, there are currently 24 DRSCs covering all Union ministries (16 work under Lok Sabha Speaker, 8 work under Rajya Sabha Chairman).
• Composition: Each DRSC consists of 31 members (21 from Lok Sabha nominated by Speaker + 10 from Rajya Sabha nominated by Chairman).
• Term: 1 year. Ministers cannot be nominated.
• Core Functions:

  1. Scrutinize the Demands for Grants of the concerned ministries during the budget recess.
  2. Examine legislative bills referred by the Presiding Officer.
  3. Consider annual reports of ministries.
    • Advisory Nature: Reports of DRSCs are recommendatory in nature and not legally binding on the Government, but carry immense moral and parliamentary weight.

• ⚠️ CRUCIAL UNIVERSAL RULE: A Minister CANNOT be elected as a member of the PAC, Estimates Committee, or COPU. If a member becomes a Minister, they cease to be a committee member.

Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices

Constitutional privileges and immunities of Parliament (Article 105), individual vs collective privileges, parliamentary devices (Question Hour, Zero Hour, Calling Attention), and major motions (No-Confidence, Adjournment, Censure).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices

Think of Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Protecting Parliamentary Autonomy and Executive Accountability

To perform their constitutional duties without fear of executive coercion, judicial interference, or external intimidation, the Constitution grants special Powers, Privileges, and Immunities to Parliament, its committees, and its members under Article 105 (and to State Legislatures under Article 194).

Mechanism & Operational Rule

Individual vs Collective Privileges (Article 105)

1. Individual Privileges of Members:

• Freedom of Speech in Parliament (Art 105(1)): Complete immunity from civil/criminal liability in any court for anything said or any vote cast in Parliament.
• Immunity from Civil Arrest: Cannot be arrested in civil cases during the session of Parliament and 40 days before and 40 days after the session.

  • ⚠️ EXAM TRAP: This immunity applies ONLY to civil cases. It does NOT extend to criminal offences or preventive detention!
    • Exemption from Jury/Witness Service: Cannot be summoned as a witness in court during session without permission of the House.

2. Collective Privileges of the House:

• Right to publish debates and reports, and the right to prohibit others from publishing unauthorized reports.
• Right to exclude strangers and hold secret (in-camera) sittings.
• Right to regulate internal procedure and adjudicate upon parliamentary matters without court interference (Article 122).
• Power to Punish for Contempt: The House has the penal power to reprimand, admonish, or imprison members or outsiders for Breach of Privilege (Searchlight Case 1959, Raja Ram Pal 2007).

Exam Application & Traps

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:\n\n1. Article & Nomenclature Confusion: Examiners frequently confuse identical sounding powers across Union and State levels (e.g. Art 123 vs Art 213; Art 72 vs Art 161). Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Constitutional vs Statutory Distinction: Do not confuse bodies created by constitutional articles (e.g. UPSC, Finance Commission, ECI) with statutory watchdogs created by ordinary Acts of Parliament (e.g. NGT, CVC, Lokpal, NHRC).\n\n4. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

DISTINCTION

Procedural Devices: Question Hour, Zero Hour & Major Motions

Parliamentary Hours:

• Question Hour (1st hour of sitting, 11 AM – 12 PM):

  1. Starred Questions: Require an oral answer; supplementary questions CAN be asked.
  2. Unstarred Questions: Require a written answer; supplementary questions CANNOT be asked.
  3. Short Notice Questions: Asked on matters of urgent public importance with less than 10 days notice; answered orally.
    • Zero Hour (Starts at 12 PM immediately after Question Hour):
  • An informal Indian parliamentary innovation (introduced in 1962); not mentioned in Rules of Procedure. Members raise urgent matters without prior notice.

Major Parliamentary Motions:

Motion Key Mechanism & Voting Constitutional Consequence
No-Confidence Motion (Rule 198) Moved in Lok Sabha ONLY; requires support of 50 MPs; no reasons needed. If passed, the entire Council of Ministers MUST resign (Art 75(3)).
Censure Motion Moved in Lok Sabha ONLY; must state specific reasons/policies against a minister or COM. If passed, COM does not need to resign, but must regain house confidence.
Adjournment Motion Requires support of 50 MPs in Lok Sabha to draw attention to a definite urgent matter of public importance. Involves an element of censure; interrupts regular business for 2.5+ hours.
Calling Attention Motion Indian parliamentary innovation (1954); calls minister’s attention to urgent matter. Authoritative statement by Minister; does NOT involve censure.
