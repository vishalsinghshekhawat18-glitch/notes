RBI Monetary Policy Operating Framework & Instruments · Concept 2 of 3

The Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) Corridor: Policy Repo Rate, Standing Deposit Facility (SDF), Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) & Bank Rate

The modern price-based operating framework of RBI monetary policy: the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) corridor. Covers the Policy Repo Rate (the single center policy rate), the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF, introduced in April 2022 under Section 17(1A) as the uncollateralized floor rate), the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF, emergency penalty ceiling rate dipping into SLR), the LAF corridor width (50 bps: Repo +/- 25 bps), and the legacy Bank Rate (Section 49 penal anchor).

Intuition & Context

The Interest Rate Corridor: Boxing in the Overnight Inter-Bank Rate

How does the central bank ensure that the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans (the Weighted Average Call Rate / WACR) stays close to the official Repo Rate?

It constructs a Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) Corridor—a financial hallway bounded by an emergency ceiling and a risk-free floor:

  • If a bank has desperate cash shortages, it never pays more than the Ceiling (MSF Rate) in the market, because it can always borrow from the RBI at MSF.
  • If a bank has massive cash surpluses, it never lends for less than the Floor (SDF Rate) in the market, because it can always park risk-free funds at the RBI at SDF.

Thus, the market overnight inter-bank interest rate is trapped inside the corridor, closely tracking the Policy Repo Rate in the center.

Core Principle

The LAF Corridor Architecture (Post-April 2022 Framework)

Following the landmark monetary policy reform on April 8, 2022, the RBI restructured the LAF corridor to its modern, symmetric format:

                      THE LAF INTEREST RATE CORRIDOR
                                    │
  ▲  MSF Rate = Bank Rate (Repo Rate + 25 bps) ─── CEILING (Emergency Overnight Borrowing)
  │                                                 (Collateral: G-Secs dipped from SLR)
  │
  │  POLICY REPO RATE (Benchmark Policy Anchor) ── CENTER (Target for WACR)
  │                                                 (Collateral: Unencumbered G-Secs)
  │
  ▼  SDF Rate (Repo Rate - 25 bps) ────────────── FLOOR (Uncollateralized Absorption)
                                                    (No G-Sec collateral required)

The Core Instruments

  1. Policy Repo Rate (Center):
    • The rate at which RBI injects liquidity against unencumbered government securities (Repo = Repurchase Agreement).
  2. Standing Deposit Facility / SDF (Floor = Repo - 25 bps):
    • Operationalized on April 8, 2022 under Section 17(1A) of the RBI Act.
    • Absorbs surplus liquidity from commercial banks without RBI providing any G-Sec collateral.
    • Eliminated the collateral constraint that previously limited RBI's liquidity absorption capacity.
  3. Marginal Standing Facility / MSF (Ceiling = Repo + 25 bps):
    • Introduced in 2011.
    • Emergency overnight borrowing window for scheduled commercial banks against dipping into their SLR portfolio (up to 2%2%2% of NDTL) at a penalty rate.
  4. Bank Rate (Section 49, RBI Act 1934):
    • The rate at which RBI buys or rediscounts bills of exchange or other commercial papers.
    • Today, it is strictly aligned with the MSF rate and serves as the reference rate for determining penal interest on CRR/SLR shortfalls.

Impact on Market Rates

  • When liquidity is deficit, banks borrow at Repo/MSF   ⟹  \implies⟹ WACR moves toward the ceiling (MSF).
  • When liquidity is surplus, banks park funds at SDF   ⟹  \implies⟹ WACR moves toward the floor (SDF).
  • The ultimate goal of RBI liquidity management is to align WACR with the Policy Repo Rate.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Exam Traps, Structural Shifts & PYQ Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Collateral in SDF vs Reverse Repo: Reverse Repo requires RBI to transfer government securities as collateral to the depositing bank. SDF is uncollateralized (RBI gives NO securities). This was the primary reason SDF was introduced under Section 17(1A) of the RBI Act.
  • Trap 2: Dipping into SLR: Under standard Repo, banks cannot use SLR-locked securities as collateral (must use unencumbered excess G-Secs). Under MSF, banks are legally permitted to dip into their SLR quota (up to a specified percentage, typically 2%2%2% of NDTL).
  • Trap 3: Bank Rate vs Repo Rate: The Bank Rate requires no collateral (rediscounting), but is currently aligned to the MSF rate and used for statutory penalty computations. The Repo Rate is collateralized and set by the MPC.
  • Trap 4: Corridor Width: The standard symmetric LAF corridor width is 50 basis points (SDF=Repo−25 bps\text{SDF} = \text{Repo} - 25\text{ bps}SDF=Repo−25 bps; MSF=Repo+25 bps\text{MSF} = \text{Repo} + 25\text{ bps}MSF=Repo+25 bps).
