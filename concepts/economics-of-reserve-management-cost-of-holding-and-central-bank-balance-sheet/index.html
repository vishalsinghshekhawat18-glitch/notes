Economics of Reserve Management: Central Bank Balance Sheet vs Government Revenue & The Excess Reserves Debate

The economic theory, accounting mechanics, and institutional governance of foreign exchange reserve management. Destroys the common public fallacy that foreign exchange reserves represent "unspent government revenue" or fiscal surplus. Explains how foreign reserves are central bank assets offset by domestic monetary liabilities on the RBI balance sheet. Analyzes the financial trade-off: Safety and Liquidity vs Return, the Opportunity Cost / Negative Carry of holding low-yield foreign sovereign assets, and the "Excess Reserves" debate in India (the Bimal Jalan Economic Capital Framework [ECF] Committee 2019 and the Malegam Committee).

Intuition & Context

The Fallacy of Spending the Reserves: Why We Cannot Build Highways with Forex

Politicians and columnists frequently propose: "India has US \USD 650 Billionillion in foreign exchange reserves sitting idle! Why doesn't the government take US \USD 100 Billionillion to build bullet trains, solar plants, and hospitals?"

Here is why this proposal is economically catastrophic:

  1. The Reserves Do Not Belong to the Ministry of Finance: The RBI did not find US \USD 650 Billionillion on the street. The RBI bought every single dollar from exporters and foreign investors by printing Indian Rupees. On the RBI balance sheet, the US \USD 650 Billionillion of foreign assets is 100% matched by ₹55 Lakh Crore of domestic currency liabilities (the money in your wallet and bank deposits).
  2. What Happens if the Government "Takes" the Dollars?:
    • If the government forces the RBI to give it US \USD 100 Billionillion for free, the RBI's assets drop while liabilities remain, turning the central bank's net worth negative and destroying international confidence in the Indian Rupee.
    • If the government converts those dollars to Rupees to pay Indian contractors, it would inject ₹8.4 Lakh Crore of unsterilized cash into the economy, triggering runaway hyperinflation.

Foreign exchange reserves are a liquidity buffer against international crises, not a fiscal sovereign wealth fund.

  • Domestic Liability Cost: Domestic sterilization bonds and domestic capital yield sim7.0%\sim 7.0%sim7.0%.

Core Safeguard of the Jalan Framework

The Jalan Committee established the inviolable statutory principle that unrealized revaluation gains (CGRA) must never be transferred as dividends to the government. Only audited, realized net operating profits after maintaining the 5.5%–6.5% CRB buffer can be paid as sovereign dividends.

Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: CGRA is Not Distributable: The Currency and Gold Revaluation Account (CGRA) contains trillions of rupees of gains resulting from Rupee depreciation. These are unrealized paper gains. By law and Jalan Committee rules, they CANNOT be transferred as dividend to the Ministry of Finance.
  • Trap 2: Sovereign Wealth Fund (SWF) vs Central Bank Reserves: Countries with massive current account surpluses from oil exports (e.g. Abu Dhabi ADIA, Norway GPFG) transfer true fiscal surpluses into Sovereign Wealth Funds to invest in global stocks and real estate. India has a Current Account Deficit; our reserves are central bank liability-backed liquidity buffers, NOT an SWF.
  • Trap 3: Triad of Reserve Objectives: The universal order of priority is: 1. Safety (Capital Preservation) to\toto 2. Liquidity (Immediate access) to\toto 3. Return (Yield). Maximizing yield is strictly secondary.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Bimal Jalan ECF formula (5.5%–6.5% CRB), CGRA non-distributability, negative carry of reserves, and central bank balance sheet identity.
  • UPSC APFC & RPSC RAS: Conceptual reasons why forex reserves cannot be spent on domestic infrastructure.
