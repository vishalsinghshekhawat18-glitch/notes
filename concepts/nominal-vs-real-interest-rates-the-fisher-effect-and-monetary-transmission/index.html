Interest Rate Theory: Nominal vs Real Interest Rates, The Fisher Effect, Negative Real Rates & Macroeconomic Transmission

The foundational relationship between interest rates and price expectations: the distinction between nominal interest rate ($i$) and real interest rate ($r$), the Fisher Equation ($1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi^e) \implies r \approx i - \pi^e$), the Fisher Effect (one-for-one adjustment of nominal rates to expected inflation), the economic consequences of negative real interest rates, and transmission to savings, capital accumulation, and exchange rates.

Intuition & Context

The Illusion of Nominal Yield: Why 7% Return Can Be Negative

Imagine you deposit ₹1,00,000 in a bank fixed deposit yielding a 7%7%7% nominal interest rate. At the end of one year, the bank hands you ₹1,07,000. You feel 7%7%7% richer.

However, during that same year, general price inflation was 8%8%8%. A basket of goods that cost ₹1,00,000 at the start of the year now costs ₹1,08,000. Even though your account shows more nominal rupees, you can buy less real goods and services than when you started!

Your real return is negative: 7%−8%=−1%7% - 8% = -1%7%−8%=−1%. The nominal rate measures the growth in rupees; the real rate measures the growth in actual purchasing power.

Core Principle

3. The Fisher Effect (Long-Run Monetary Neutrality)

According to the Classical Dichotomy, the equilibrium real interest rate (rrr) is determined in the goods market by real forces (savings supply and investment productivity). Therefore, an increase in central bank money growth raises expected inflation πe\pi^eπe, causing a one-for-one upward adjustment in nominal interest rates (iii), leaving long-run equilibrium rrr unaffected.

How It Works / Mechanism

Key Channels

  1. Financial Disintermediation: Households withdraw financial savings from banks and purchase physical inflation-hedges (Gold, Real Estate), starving the formal manufacturing sector of long-term investment capital.
  2. Financial Repression: Governments historically maintained artificially low nominal interest rates below inflation to cheapen sovereign debt service at the expense of domestic savers.
