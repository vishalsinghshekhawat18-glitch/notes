Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices

Constitutional privileges and immunities of Parliament (Article 105), individual vs collective privileges, parliamentary devices (Question Hour, Zero Hour, Calling Attention), and major motions (No-Confidence, Adjournment, Censure).

Intuition & Context

Intuitive Mental Model: Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices

Think of Parliamentary Privileges (Art 105), Motions & Procedural Devices as a fundamental structural safeguard in a constitutional democracy. Without this explicit mechanism, the balance between state power and individual freedom would collapse into arbitrary exercise of authority. In everyday governance, this provision ensures that public functionaries operate under defined legal boundaries, guaranteeing predictability, accountability, and the rule of law across the republic.

Core Principle

Protecting Parliamentary Autonomy and Executive Accountability

To perform their constitutional duties without fear of executive coercion, judicial interference, or external intimidation, the Constitution grants special Powers, Privileges, and Immunities to Parliament, its committees, and its members under Article 105 (and to State Legislatures under Article 194).

How It Works / Mechanism

Individual vs Collective Privileges (Article 105)

1. Individual Privileges of Members:

• Freedom of Speech in Parliament (Art 105(1)): Complete immunity from civil/criminal liability in any court for anything said or any vote cast in Parliament.
• Immunity from Civil Arrest: Cannot be arrested in civil cases during the session of Parliament and 40 days before and 40 days after the session.

  • ⚠️ EXAM TRAP: This immunity applies ONLY to civil cases. It does NOT extend to criminal offences or preventive detention!
    • Exemption from Jury/Witness Service: Cannot be summoned as a witness in court during session without permission of the House.

2. Collective Privileges of the House:

• Right to publish debates and reports, and the right to prohibit others from publishing unauthorized reports.
• Right to exclude strangers and hold secret (in-camera) sittings.
• Right to regulate internal procedure and adjudicate upon parliamentary matters without court interference (Article 122).
• Power to Punish for Contempt: The House has the penal power to reprimand, admonish, or imprison members or outsiders for Breach of Privilege (Searchlight Case 1959, Raja Ram Pal 2007).

Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:\n\n1. Article & Nomenclature Confusion: Examiners frequently confuse identical sounding powers across Union and State levels (e.g. Art 123 vs Art 213; Art 72 vs Art 161). Verify whether the authority belongs to the President or Governor.\n\n2. Absolute vs Qualified Language Trap: Beware of statements containing absolute words like "solely", "without exception", or "unconditional". Most constitutional powers are subject to ministerial aid and advice (Art 74/163) or judicial review (Basic Structure doctrine).\n\n3. Constitutional vs Statutory Distinction: Do not confuse bodies created by constitutional articles (e.g. UPSC, Finance Commission, ECI) with statutory watchdogs created by ordinary Acts of Parliament (e.g. NGT, CVC, Lokpal, NHRC).\n\n4. Appointment vs Removal Asymmetry: Remember high-yield asymmetries—such as State PSC members being appointed by the Governor but removable only by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry under Article 317.

DISTINCTION

Procedural Devices: Question Hour, Zero Hour & Major Motions

Parliamentary Hours:

• Question Hour (1st hour of sitting, 11 AM – 12 PM):

  1. Starred Questions: Require an oral answer; supplementary questions CAN be asked.
  2. Unstarred Questions: Require a written answer; supplementary questions CANNOT be asked.
  3. Short Notice Questions: Asked on matters of urgent public importance with less than 10 days notice; answered orally.
    • Zero Hour (Starts at 12 PM immediately after Question Hour):
  • An informal Indian parliamentary innovation (introduced in 1962); not mentioned in Rules of Procedure. Members raise urgent matters without prior notice.

Major Parliamentary Motions:

Motion Key Mechanism & Voting Constitutional Consequence
No-Confidence Motion (Rule 198) Moved in Lok Sabha ONLY; requires support of 50 MPs; no reasons needed. If passed, the entire Council of Ministers MUST resign (Art 75(3)).
Censure Motion Moved in Lok Sabha ONLY; must state specific reasons/policies against a minister or COM. If passed, COM does not need to resign, but must regain house confidence.
Adjournment Motion Requires support of 50 MPs in Lok Sabha to draw attention to a definite urgent matter of public importance. Involves an element of censure; interrupts regular business for 2.5+ hours.
Calling Attention Motion Indian parliamentary innovation (1954); calls minister’s attention to urgent matter. Authoritative statement by Minister; does NOT involve censure.
