Public Debt Architecture: Internal Debt, External Debt, Public Account Liabilities & Maturity Profile

The statutory, institutional, and maturity architecture of sovereign public debt in India. Analyzes the total liabilities of the Central Government under Article 292 of the Constitution, comprising: (1) Internal Debt (dated Government Securities / G-Secs, Treasury Bills, Special Securities issued to PSBs and oil bonds), (2) External Debt (multilateral and bilateral concessional borrowings from World Bank, ADB, JICA denominated in foreign currencies), and (3) Public Account Liabilities (National Small Savings Fund / NSSF, State Provident Funds, and Reserve funds). Examines the maturity profile, currency risk insulation (external debt is $< 5\%$ of total public debt), and the weighted average cost of sovereign borrowing.

Intuition & Context

Who Does the Government Owe Money To?

When you hear that India's national debt is over ₹170 Lakh Crore, do foreign countries own India?

Not at all!

Unlike countries like Argentina, Sri Lanka, or Pakistan that borrowed heavily in US Dollars from foreign commercial hedge funds (and defaulted when their currency crashed), India owes over 95%95%95% of its public debt to its own citizens and domestic institutions in Indian Rupees (INR)!

Who are these domestic lenders? Indian commercial banks (investing your deposits in SLR G-Secs), Life Insurance Corporation (LIC), EPFO pension funds, and post office small savings depositors. When the Indian government repays debt, money simply transfers from one Indian pocket to another Indian pocket!

How It Works / Mechanism

Maturity Profile & Sovereign Risk Insulation

Why is India's public debt remarkably resilient compared to emerging market peers?

  1. Zero Sovereign External Commercial Borrowing: The Government of India does NOT issue foreign currency sovereign bonds (like Brady bonds or Eurobonds) in international markets. It borrows abroad only from multilateral development bodies (World Bank, ADB) at concessional, ultra-long-term interest rates.
  2. Elongated Maturity (Low Rollover Risk): The weighted average maturity of Indian G-Secs is ~11.5 to 12.5 years (with 40-year and 50-year bonds actively issued). The government only has to refinance a small fraction of debt in any given year.
  3. Fixed Coupon Structure (Zero Floating Rate Shock): Over 90%90%90% of G-Secs carry fixed interest rates, protecting the budget from sudden surges in global interest rates.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Exam Traps & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: External Debt Proportion: External sovereign debt is LESS THAN 5% of total central government liabilities. Many candidates confuse total external debt (including private corporate ECBs) with Central Government sovereign external debt.
  • Trap 2: Constitutional Borrowing Powers:
    • Central Government borrows under Article 292 (can borrow domestically and externally).
    • State Governments borrow under Article 293 (States are strictly PROHIBITED from borrowing abroad directly; all foreign loans must be channeled through the Centre!).
  • Trap 3: Weighted Average Maturity: Weighted average maturity of central debt is elongated (> 11 years), not short-term.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Public debt composition, maturity profiles, Public Debt Management Office (PDMO) functions, and G-Sec yields.
  • SBI/IBPS PO & RPSC RAS: Internal vs External debt shares, Article 292/293 provisions, and 2-mark definitions of Public Debt.
