Foreign Exchange Market Mechanics: Demand-Supply Dynamics, Quotation Systems & Currency Appreciation vs Depreciation

The foundational microstructure and macroeconomic price discovery mechanisms of the foreign exchange (forex) market. Analyzes the market institutional hierarchy (Authorised Dealers Category I, II, III under FEMA 1999), spot vs forward transactions, Direct Quotation (Price Currency / Base Currency, e.g. ₹84/USD) vs Indirect Quotation, the downward-sloping Demand Curve for foreign currency (derived from imports and outward capital) vs upward-sloping Supply Curve (derived from exports and inward capital), and the structural distinction between Market-Driven Appreciation/Depreciation and Statutorily Fixed Revaluation/Devaluation.

Intuition & Context

The Onion Market Analogy for US Dollars in Mumbai

Think of the US Dollar as a commodity, just like onions in a wholesale mandi in Nashik:

  • When a bad monsoon destroys onion crops, the supply of onions shrinks, and the price of onions shoots up from ₹30/kg to ₹80/kg.
  • In the Mumbai foreign exchange market, the US Dollar is the commodity and the Indian Rupee is the price.

What makes the price of a Dollar rise from ₹80 to ₹85 (Rupee Depreciation)?

  1. High Demand for Dollars: Global crude oil prices surge, so Indian oil refiners rush to buy billions of US Dollars to pay Saudi Aramco.
  2. Low Supply of Dollars: Foreign investors panic and sell Indian stocks, reducing the inflow of fresh US Dollars into Mumbai.

When demand for dollars exceeds supply, the price of 1 Dollar goes up in Rupees (from ₹80 to ₹85). The Rupee has depreciated, and the Dollar has appreciated.

1. Demand for Foreign Currency (DtextUSDD_{\text{USD}}DtextUSD​) - Downward Sloping

  • When the price of USD is high (e.g., ₹90/USD), foreign goods are expensive in India, so Indian imports drop   ⟹  \implies⟹ lower demand for USD.
  • When the price of USD is low (e.g., ₹75/USD), imports are cheap, so import demand surges   ⟹  \implies⟹ higher demand for USD.

2. Supply of Foreign Currency (StextUSDS_{\text{USD}}StextUSD​) - Upward Sloping

  • When the price of USD is high (e.g., ₹90/USD), Indian goods are cheap for foreign buyers, so Indian exports expand   ⟹  \implies⟹ foreign buyers supply more USD to buy Rupees.

3. Equilibrium Price Discovery (R∗R^*R∗)

At R∗R^*R∗, the quantity of foreign currency demanded by Indian importers exactly equals the quantity supplied by Indian exporters and foreign investors.

Exam Traps & Comparative Terminology Matrix

Critical Examiner Traps: The Four-Way Confusion Matrix

Examiners constantly test the difference between market-driven and administrative currency movements:

Concept Market Regime Mechanism Direction of Domestic Currency Value
Appreciation Floating / Flexible Market forces of demand and supply INCREASES (₹84 to\toto ₹80 / USD)
Depreciation Floating / Flexible Market forces of demand and supply DECREASES (₹84 to\toto ₹88 / USD)
Revaluation Fixed / Pegged Official statutory decree by Central Bank/Govt INCREASES (Peg adjusted upward)
Devaluation Fixed / Pegged Official statutory decree by Central Bank/Govt DECREASES (Peg adjusted downward)
  • Trap 1: Direct Quotation Numbering: Under direct quote (₹/USD), when the number goes UP (from ₹83 to ₹86), the Rupee is getting WEAKER (Depreciation), NOT stronger!
  • Trap 2: Spot vs Forward vs Swap: Spot settlement occurs in T+2T+2T+2 business days. Cash/Same-day is T+0T+0T+0, Tom is T+1T+1T+1. A Forward contract is settled beyond 2 business days at an agreed rate.
