12:T630,Macroeconomics identifies three primary engines of inflation:

### 1. Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too Much Money Chasing Too Few Goods")
- Occurs when **Aggregate Demand ($AD$)** expands beyond the economy's potential productive capacity at full employment ($Y^*$).
- **Triggers**: Fiscal deficit expansion, loose monetary policy (excessive money growth), surging consumer credit, or export booms.
- **Graphically**: The $AD$ curve shifts rightward along an upward-sloping or vertical Aggregate Supply ($AS$) curve, pulling price level from $P_1$ to $P_2$.

### 2. Cost-Push Inflation (Supply-Shock / Cost-Inflation)
- Occurs when the aggregate costs of production increase, shifting the **Short-Run Aggregate Supply ($SRAS$)** curve leftward.
- **Triggers**: Imported commodity shocks (e.g., global Brent crude surges), supply chain blockages, minimum wage hikes exceeding productivity growth, indirect tax hikes (GST), or agricultural crop failure.
- **Wage-Price Spiral**: Workers demand higher nominal wages to offset cost-of-living increases $\implies$ firms raise output prices to protect profit margins $\implies$ triggers a self-reinforcing upward spiral.

### 3. Structural Inflation (Myrdal / Prebisch Developing Economy Model)
- Prevalent in developing economies like India due to structural supply bottlenecks (e.g., poor cold storage, inadequate transport infrastructure, agricultural marketing rigidities, and balance-of-payments import dependencies).
- Structural inflation cannot be permanently extinguished solely by monetary contraction without severely damaging output.13:T70b,Economics classifies inflation along two distinct analytical dimensions:

```
                             PRICE LEVEL DYNAMICS
                                       │
         ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                           ▼
   SPEED / INTENSITY                                         DIRECTION / PHENOMENON
• Creeping (< 3% p.a.)                                      • Inflation (Price level rising)
• Walking (3% to 10% p.a.)                                  • Disinflation (Inflation rate falling, e.g. 7% -> 4%)
• Running (10% to 20% p.a.)                                 • Deflation (Price level falling, negative inflation)
• Galloping (20% to 1000% p.a.)                             • Reflation (Deliberate stimulus to recover from deflation)
• Hyperinflation (> 50% per month / Weimar/Zimbabwe)        • Stagflation (Stagnant Growth + High Inflation + High Unemployment)
```

### Detailed Trajectory Definitions
1. **Disinflation vs Deflation**:
   - **Disinflation**: Inflation rate drops from $+8\%$ to $+3\%$. Prices are **still increasing**, but at a slower speed. Real purchasing power still erodes, but more slowly.
   - **Deflation**: Inflation rate becomes negative (e.g., $-2\%$). Prices are **falling**. Leads to a dangerous deflationary spiral as consumers postpone consumption expecting lower future prices.
2. **Stagflation**:
   - The breakdown of the classical Phillips Curve trade-off. Output contracts ($GDP \downarrow$), unemployment rises ($U \uparrow$), while inflation remains high ($P \uparrow$). Exemplified by the 1970s OPEC oil crises.14:T41f,### Critical Examiner Traps
- **Trap 1: Disinflation vs Deflation**: In disinflation, prices **DO NOT fall**; the *rate of inflation* falls. In deflation, the absolute price level falls.
- **Trap 2: Core vs Headline Inflation**: Headline inflation includes volatile components (Food and Fuel). **Core Inflation** strips out volatile Food and Fuel components (\text{Core} = \text{Headline} - [\text{Food} + \text{Fuel}]). In India, RBI targets **Headline CPI-Combined**, but monitors Core CPI for underlying demand trends.
- **Trap 3: Stagflation Policy Dilemma**: Standard monetary policy faces a policy paralysis during stagflation: raising interest rates to fight inflation worsens unemployment, while cutting interest rates to boost output accelerates inflation.
