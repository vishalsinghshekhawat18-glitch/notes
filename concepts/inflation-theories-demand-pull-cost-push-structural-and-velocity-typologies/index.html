Inflation Theories and Typologies: Demand-Pull, Cost-Push, Structural Inflation, Disinflation, Deflation & Stagflation

The core theoretical foundations of price level inflation: definitions of persistent price rises, the causal dichotomy between Demand-Pull ($AD > AS$) and Cost-Push ($AS$ contraction), the structuralist inflation model for developing economies, speed-based classifications (creeping to hyperinflation), and distinct price path phenomena (disinflation, deflation, reflation, and stagflation).

Intuition & Context

The Price Thermometer: Measuring Purchasing Power Erosion (Professor Ryan)

Inflation is the persistent, ongoing decrease in the purchasing power of money. If a typical basket of consumer goods costs ₹1,000 today and ₹1,060 next year, the purchasing power of ₹1,000 has eroded by 6%6%6%.

In "Inflation" (YouTube: SRmOwc37uIg), Professor Ryan formalizes the essential distinction between Relative Price Changes and General Inflation:

  • Relative Price Change: If unseasonal rain ruins the onion crop, onion prices surge while other prices remain stable or fall. This is a change in the relative price of onions driven by microeconomic supply and demand, NOT macroeconomic inflation.
  • General Inflation: An economy-wide, ongoing increase in the overall price level (PL↑PL \uparrowPL↑) across virtually all goods and services. Individual prices may fluctuate, but the aggregate price index moves upward over time.

Crucially, inflation is not a one-time increase in the price of tomatoes or smartphones; it is a broad-based, sustained increase in the general price index across the economy over time.

Core Principle

Causal Theories: Demand-Pull, Cost-Push & Structural Inflation

Macroeconomics identifies three primary engines of inflation:

1. Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too Much Money Chasing Too Few Goods")

  • Occurs when Aggregate Demand (ADADAD) expands beyond the economy's potential productive capacity at full employment (Y∗Y^*Y∗).
  • Triggers: Fiscal deficit expansion, loose monetary policy (excessive money growth), surging consumer credit, or export booms.
  • Graphically: The ADADAD curve shifts rightward along an upward-sloping or vertical Aggregate Supply (ASASAS) curve, pulling price level from P1P_1P1​ to P2P_2P2​.

2. Cost-Push Inflation (Supply-Shock / Cost-Inflation)

  • Occurs when the aggregate costs of production increase, shifting the Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRASSRASSRAS) curve leftward.
  • Triggers: Imported commodity shocks (e.g., global Brent crude surges), supply chain blockages, minimum wage hikes exceeding productivity growth, indirect tax hikes (GST), or agricultural crop failure.
  • Wage-Price Spiral: Workers demand higher nominal wages to offset cost-of-living increases   ⟹  \implies⟹ firms raise output prices to protect profit margins   ⟹  \implies⟹ triggers a self-reinforcing upward spiral.

3. Structural Inflation (Myrdal / Prebisch Developing Economy Model)

  • Prevalent in developing economies like India due to structural supply bottlenecks (e.g., poor cold storage, inadequate transport infrastructure, agricultural marketing rigidities, and balance-of-payments import dependencies).
  • Structural inflation cannot be permanently extinguished solely by monetary contraction without severely damaging output.
How It Works / Mechanism

The Velocity & Trajectory Typology of Price Dynamics

Economics classifies inflation along two distinct analytical dimensions:

                             PRICE LEVEL DYNAMICS
                                       │
         ┌─────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                           ▼
   SPEED / INTENSITY                                         DIRECTION / PHENOMENON
• Creeping (< 3% p.a.)                                      • Inflation (Price level rising)
• Walking (3% to 10% p.a.)                                  • Disinflation (Inflation rate falling, e.g. 7% -> 4%)
• Running (10% to 20% p.a.)                                 • Deflation (Price level falling, negative inflation)
• Galloping (20% to 1000% p.a.)                             • Reflation (Deliberate stimulus to recover from deflation)
• Hyperinflation (> 50% per month / Weimar/Zimbabwe)        • Stagflation (Stagnant Growth + High Inflation + High Unemployment)

Detailed Trajectory Definitions

  1. Disinflation vs Deflation:
    • Disinflation: Inflation rate drops from +8%+8%+8% to +3%+3%+3%. Prices are still increasing, but at a slower speed. Real purchasing power still erodes, but more slowly.
    • Deflation: Inflation rate becomes negative (e.g., −2%-2%−2%). Prices are falling. Leads to a dangerous deflationary spiral as consumers postpone consumption expecting lower future prices.
  2. Stagflation:
    • The breakdown of the classical Phillips Curve trade-off. Output contracts (GDP↓GDP \downarrowGDP↓), unemployment rises (U↑U \uparrowU↑), while inflation remains high (P↑P \uparrowP↑). Exemplified by the 1970s OPEC oil crises.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Exam Traps, Distinctions & Multi-Exam Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Disinflation vs Deflation: In disinflation, prices DO NOT fall; the rate of inflation falls. In deflation, the absolute price level falls.
  • Trap 2: Core vs Headline Inflation: Headline inflation includes volatile components (Food and Fuel). Core Inflation strips out volatile Food and Fuel components (\text{Core} = \text{Headline} - [\text{Food} + \text{Fuel}]). In India, RBI targets Headline CPI-Combined, but monitors Core CPI for underlying demand trends.
  • Trap 3: Stagflation Policy Dilemma: Standard monetary policy faces a policy paralysis during stagflation: raising interest rates to fight inflation worsens unemployment, while cutting interest rates to boost output accelerates inflation.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Focus on demand-pull vs cost-push supply shocks, wage-price mechanisms, and core vs headline inflation.
  • RPSC RAS, SBI/IBPS PO & UPSC APFC: 2-mark definitions of Stagflation, Disinflation, Reflation, and Creeping/Hyperinflation.
