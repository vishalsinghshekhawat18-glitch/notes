The Mauryan Empire: Foundation, Statecraft, Chandragupta & Bindusara · Concept 1 of 3

Sources for Mauryan History: Kautilya’s Arthashastra, Megasthenes’ Indica, Mudrarakshasa & Epigraphy

Critical historiographical analysis of primary sources for reconstructing the Mauryan Empire: Kautilya’s Sanskrit treatise Arthashastra, Megasthenes’ Hellenistic account Indica (and fragments in Strabo/Arrian/Diodorus), Vishakhadatta’s play Mudrarakshasa, Buddhist chronicles, and epigraphy.

Intuition & Context

Reconstructing the First Pan-Indian Empire: Cross-Examining Primary Sources

How do historians reconstruct an empire that existed over 2,200 years ago? We do so by cross-examining three distinct lenses:

  1. Indigenous Normative Theory (Kautilya's Arthashastra): The insider manual of pragmatic, cold-blooded realpolitik and administrative engineering.
  2. Foreign Observational Account (Megasthenes' Indica): The Greek traveler's eyewitness report, rich in institutional detail but prone to Hellenistic cultural filtering (such as projecting Greek social categories onto Indian reality).
  3. Lithic Royal Self-Testimony (Ashokan Epigraphy): Direct, unedited imperial proclamations carved into indestructible stone pillars and rocks across the subcontinent.

Most authentic, contemporaneous, primary epigraphic source; free from later editorial tampering.

Megasthenes’ Seven Classes of Indian Society

  1. Philosophers (Sophistai): Brahmanas and Shramana ascetics; exempt from taxes; performed public sacrifices.
  2. Farmers (Georgoi): Largest single segment of the population; dedicated purely to cultivating the land; protected even during wars.
  3. Herdsmen & Hunters (Poimenes / Boukoloi): Nomadic pastoralists living in tents and forests, paying tribute in livestock.
  4. Artisans & Traders (Technitai): Metalworkers, carpenters, armor-makers, and merchants.
  5. Soldiers / Military (Polemistai): Second largest class; professional standing army funded entirely from the royal treasury.
  6. Overseers / Inspectors (Episkopoi): Spies and intelligence officers reporting directly to the King and magistrates (corresponds to Kautilya's Gudhapurushas).
  7. Councillors & Assessors (Symbouloi): Elite ministers, treasurers, and judges deliberating on state policy.
How It Works / Mechanism

Historiographical Reconciliation: Slavery in Indica vs Arthashastra

The Great Historiographical Paradox: Was there Slavery in Mauryan India?

  • Megasthenes' Observation: 'All Indians are free, and not even a slave exists among them.'
  • Kautilya's Legal Reality (Arthashastra Book III, Chapter 13): Kautilya dedicates an entire exhaustive legal code to slaves (Dasas and Karmakaras), detailing rules for their humane treatment, property rights, and specific procedures for earning emancipation (Nishkraya). Kautilya explicitly prohibits mortgaging an Aryaprana (free-born Aryan) into slavery.
  • Historical Reconciliation: Megasthenes was accustomed to the brutal chattel slavery of ancient Greece and Rome, where slaves had zero human rights, were worked to death in silver mines, and could be killed at will. In India, domestic Dasas enjoyed customary legal protections, could own personal property, inherit from masters, and buy their freedom. Consequently, to a Greek observer, Indian slavery did not resemble Hellenistic slavery at all.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Examiner Traps, Exception Clauses & High-Yield Pitfalls

Critical Traps & Multi-Statement Pitfalls for Aspirants:

  1. Indica Preservation Trap: Megasthenes' Indica does NOT survive in a complete original manuscript. It is reconstructed entirely from citations in later Greek and Roman authors (Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus, Pliny).

  2. 7 Castes vs 4 Varnas: Megasthenes described SEVEN occupational classes, NOT the traditional four Varnas. Do NOT confuse his 7 classes with the Chaturvarna system.

  3. Mudrarakshasa Authorship & Period: Mudrarakshasa was authored by Vishakhadatta in the Gupta period (c. 5th century CE), NOT during the Mauryan period, though it depicts Mauryan events.

  4. Discovery of Arthashastra: Kautilya's Arthashastra was discovered on palm-leaf manuscripts by R. Shamasastry in 1905 at the Mysore Oriental Library and published in 1909.
