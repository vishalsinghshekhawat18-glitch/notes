Water of Crystallisation, Plaster of Paris (POP) vs Gypsum & Salt Hydrates

The fixed stoichiometry of water molecules locked inside crystal lattices (water of crystallisation), the reversible dehydration of Gypsum at 373 K (100°C) into Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O), and surgical bone casting.

Intuition & Context

Why Blue Crystals Turn Ghost-White When Heated (Where Did the Blue Go?)

If you gently heat beautiful deep-blue crystals of Copper Sulphate (CuSO4⋅5H2OCuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2OCuSO4​⋅5H2​O) in a dry test tube, droplets of liquid water condense at the top of the tube, and the crystal turns into a ghostly chalky-white powder (Anhydrous CuSO4CuSO_4CuSO4​). Did you destroy the chemical? Put just two drops of water back onto the white powder: it instantly snaps back into brilliant electric blue! The blue color was created by 5 water molecules locked in the crystal lattice (Water of Crystallisation).

How It Works / Mechanism

Why POP Formula is Written with Half a Molecule of Water (½ H₂O)

The Fraction Explained:

  • You cannot physically cut a water molecule in half!
  • In the crystal lattice of Plaster of Paris, TWO formula units of Calcium Sulphate (CaSO4CaSO_4CaSO4​) share ONE single water molecule (H2OH_2OH2​O) between them:
    (CaSO4)2⋅H2O≡CaSO4⋅12H2O\mathbf{(CaSO_4)_2 \cdot H_2O \equiv CaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2O}(CaSO4​)2​⋅H2​O≡CaSO4​⋅21​H2​O
  • To express the formula per single CaSO4CaSO_4CaSO4​ unit, it is conventionally written as CaSO4⋅12H2OCaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2OCaSO4​⋅21​H2​O.

The 373 K (100°C) Temperature Guardrail:

  • Heating Gypsum at exactly $373 , \text{K}$ (100∘C100^\circ\text{C}100∘C) removes $1.5$ water molecules, yielding active POP.
  • If heated above $373 , \text{K}$ (>100∘C>100^\circ\text{C}>100∘C): All $2$ water molecules are driven off, forming Dead Burnt Plaster (CaSO4CaSO_4CaSO4​), which completely loses its setting ability!
