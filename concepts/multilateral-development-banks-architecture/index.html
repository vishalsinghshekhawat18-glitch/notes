Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs): Bretton Woods, Regional Banks & South-South Institutions

A comparative analysis of Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) and international financial institutions: the Bretton Woods twins (World Bank Group and IMF), regional banks (ADB, EIB, IDB, AfDB), and modern South-South institutions (AIIB and NDB). Analyzes founding charters, headquarters, capital subscriptions, voting share structures, and India’s institutional relationship.

Core Principle

Evolution of Global Development Finance

Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) are supranational financial institutions established by sovereign states to fund infrastructure, social programs, and macroeconomic stabilization.

The global architecture has evolved across three waves:

  1. Post-WWII Bretton Woods (1944): World Bank Group & IMF (US/European dominance).
  2. Post-Colonial Regional Banks (1960s): ADB, IDB, AfDB (Regional focus, dominant OECD anchors).
  3. 21st-Century South-South Institutions (2015–2016): AIIB & NDB (Emerging market leadership, alternative voting formulas).

Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

High-Yield MDB Exam Distinctions

  1. India and ICSID: India is a founding/active member of IBRD, IDA, IFC, and MIGA, but has consistently refused to sign the ICSID Convention. Rationale: ICSID arbitral awards cannot be reviewed by domestic courts, infringing on sovereign jurisdiction.
  2. AIIB Voting Structure: China holds over 25% voting power in AIIB, giving it effective veto power over major constitutional amendments (which require a 75% supermajority). India is the second-largest shareholder (7.6%) and highest project borrower.
  3. NDB Equality Principle: Unlike the World Bank or ADB where voting power strictly reflects capital contributed, NDB was designed so that no single founding member holds veto power or permanent dominant shareholding.
