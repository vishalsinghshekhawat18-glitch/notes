Inflation Expectations, The Phillips Curve (Short-Run vs Long-Run) & Economic Costs of Anticipated vs Unanticipated Inflation

The role of forward-looking inflation expectations in macroeconomic wage-setting and price determination: adaptive vs rational expectations, the Short-Run Phillips Curve (trade-off between inflation and unemployment) vs the Friedman-Phelps Long-Run Vertical Phillips Curve at Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), and the economic welfare costs of inflation (shoe-leather costs, menu costs, tax distortions, arbitrary wealth redistribution).

Intuition & Context

The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: Why Inflation Expectations Matter

Inflation is unique because what people expect to happen tomorrow directly drives what actually happens today.

If factory workers expect inflation to be 8%8%8% next year, they demand an 8%8%8% wage increase today. To pay those higher wages without losing profit, factory owners immediately raise output prices by 8%8%8%. The expectation of 8%8%8% inflation created 8%8%8% inflation! This is why central bankers are obsessed with anchoring inflation expectations—if the public trusts the central bank's inflation target, expectations remain steady and inflation remains contained.

Core Principle

The Phillips Curve: Short-Run Trade-off vs Long-Run Vertical Reality

The evolution of the Phillips Curve represents a cornerstone of modern monetary macroeconomics:

1. Short-Run Phillips Curve (A.W. Phillips, 1958)

  • Originally demonstrated an empirical trade-off: Higher Inflation   ⟹  \implies⟹ Lower Unemployment.
  • Mechanism: When aggregate demand expands, firms hire more workers (lowering unemployment) while bidding up wages and prices (higher inflation).

πt=πte−β(ut−un)+v\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta(u_t - u_n) + vπt​=πte​−β(ut​−un​)+v
Where πt\pi_tπt​ is inflation, πte\pi_t^eπte​ is expected inflation, utu_tut​ is unemployment, unu_nun​ is the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), and vvv is a supply shock.

2. Long-Run Vertical Phillips Curve (Friedman & Phelps, 1968)

  • Natural Rate Hypothesis: In the long run, workers and firms adjust their expectations completely ( πte=πt ,\pi_t^e = \pi_t,πte​=πt​).
  • When expectations catch up, real wages adjust back to equilibrium, and unemployment returns to the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU) regardless of inflation rate.
  • Conclusion: Central banks cannot permanently buy lower unemployment by tolerating higher inflation. Attempting to do so only generates higher inflation expectations with no output gain.

Economic Costs of Inflation & Distributional Transfers

Macroeconomics separates the welfare costs of inflation into anticipated and unanticipated components:

                         COSTS OF INFLATION
                                 │
        ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                 ▼
   ANTICIPATED INFLATION                             UNANTICIPATED INFLATION
• Shoe-Leather Costs (wasted trips to bank)         • Arbitrary wealth transfer from
• Menu Costs (cost of updating price lists)           creditors to debtors
• Tax Bracket Creep (fiscal drag)                   • Real wage erosion for fixed earners
• Relative Price Distortions                        • Uncertainty suppressing capex

1. Unanticipated Inflation: The Creditor-Debtor Transfer

  • A borrower takes a loan at a nominal interest rate of 10%10%10%, expecting inflation to be 4%4%4% (expected real interest rate = 6%6%6%).
  • If inflation unexpectedly surges to 12%12%12%, the actual ex-post real interest rate becomes: 10%−12%=−2%10% - 12% = -2%10%−12%=−2%.
  • Winner: The Debtor (repays the loan with heavily degraded rupees).
  • Loser: The Creditor / Saver (receives purchasing power worth less than the original principal).

2. Anticipated Inflation: Friction Costs

  • Shoe-Leather Costs: High inflation makes holding non-interest-bearing cash costly, forcing consumers to keep funds in interest accounts and make frequent bank/ATM trips.
  • Menu Costs: Resources wasted constantly repricing catalogs, changing POS software, and reprinting menus.
  • Bracket Creep: Progressive tax systems tax nominal income gains, pushing individuals into higher tax brackets without any real increase in purchasing power.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

Exam Traps, Survey Architecture & PYQ Crosswalks

Critical Examiner Traps

  • Trap 1: Who Gains from Unexpected Inflation?: Borrowers / Debtors gain, while Lenders / Creditors / Savers lose. Fixed pension earners lose; equity investors and real estate owners are partially hedged.
  • Trap 2: Shape of Phillips Curve in Long Run: The Short-Run Phillips Curve is downward sloping, but the Long-Run Phillips Curve is strictly vertical at NAIRU.
  • Trap 3: RBI Inflation Expectations Surveys: RBI conducts two distinct surveys:
    1. IESH (Inflation Expectations Survey of Households): Captures qualitative and quantitative 3-month and 1-year ahead household inflation perceptions.
    2. SPF (Survey of Professional Forecasters): Captures institutional and academic economic forecasts.

Cross-Exam Focus

  • RBI Grade B & SEBI Grade A: Mathematical equations of expectations-augmented Phillips curve, NAIRU, and debtor-creditor transfers.
  • RPSC RAS, APFC & SBI/IBPS PO: 2-mark definitions of NAIRU, Phillips Curve, menu costs, and winners/losers of inflation.
