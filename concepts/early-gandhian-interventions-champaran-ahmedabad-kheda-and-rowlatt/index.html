The Gandhian Mass Movements (1917–1942) · Concept 1 of 4

The Advent of Mahatma Gandhi: South African Laboratory, Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918), Kheda (1918) & Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)

The entry and crystallization of Gandhian mass satyagraha technique in India: Return from South Africa (Jan 9, 1915 - Pravasi Bharatiya Divas), Gopal Krishna Gokhale as political guru, Sabarmati Ashram; Champaran Satyagraha (1917, Bihar, first Civil Disobedience, Rajkumar Shukla, Tinkathia 3/20th system of indigo cultivation); Ahmedabad Mill Strike (March 1918, first Hunger Strike, 35% wage bonus demand for plague allowance); Kheda Satyagraha (1918, Gujarat, first Non-Cooperation, crop failure remission, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel); Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act 1919, "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"); Rowlatt Satyagraha; and the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919, General Dyer, Hunter Commission, Rabindranath Tagore renouncing Knighthood).

Intuition & Context

The South African Crucible & The Awakening of a Moral Titan

How did an introspective, London-trained barrister transform into the indisputable mass leader of the largest anti-colonial struggle in human history?

Mahatma Gandhi did not arrive in India in 1915 as an untested political philosopher. He arrived having completed a twenty-year laboratory experiment in South Africa (1893–1914):

  • In South Africa, confronting severe racial discrimination against Indian indentured labourers and merchants (poll tax, Asiatic Registration Act, invalidation of non-Christian marriages), Gandhi forged the radical technique of Satyagraha (Satya = Truth, Agraha = Firm adherence / Soul-force).
  • Influenced by Leo Tolstoy (The Kingdom of God is Within You), John Ruskin (Unto This Last), and Henry David Thoreau (Civil Disobedience), Gandhi rejected both the "polite mendicancy" of the Indian Moderates and the "individual terror" of the Revolutionaries.
  • On the advice of his political guru, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Gandhi spent his first two years in India (1915–1916) travelling in third-class train compartments across the subcontinent with his "ears open and mouth shut", absorbing the lived agony of ordinary peasants. When he finally struck, he intervened not on lofty constitutional abstractions, but on immediate, localized, visceral economic oppressions.

Champaran Satyagraha (1917) Champaran, Bihar: European planters forced ryots to grow indigo on 320th\frac{3}{20}^{\text{th}}203​th of their land (Tinkathia System). Planters demanded exorbitant illegal cash compensations (Tawan and Sharbeshi) to release peasants when synthetic German dyes ruined indigo profits. Invited by peasant Rajkumar Shukla. First Civil Disobedience (Savinay Avagya) in India:
Gandhi defied the British District Magistrate's order to leave Champaran under Section 144, stating he was obeying the "higher law of our being, the voice of conscience".		 Rajendra Prasad, J.B. Kripalani, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parikh, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Anugrah Narayan Sinha. Government appointed the Champaran Agrarian Committee with Gandhi as a member.
• Tinkathia system abolished.
• Planters forced to refund 25% of the illegally extorted money.
Ahmedabad Mill Strike (March 1918) Ahmedabad, Gujarat: Mill owners sought to withdraw the Plague Bonus (up to 75% granted during the 1917 plague epidemic) when the epidemic ended, while workers faced crushing wartime inflation and demanded a 50% wage increase. Mill owners offered only 20%. First Hunger Strike (Bhookh Hartal) in India:
Gandhi intervened on behalf of mill workers (led by Anasuya Sarabhai, sister of mill owners' association president Ambalal Sarabhai) and fasted to sustain workers' moral discipline.		 Anasuya Sarabhai, Shankarlal Banker, Vallabhbhai Patel. After a 3-day fast, mill owners agreed to submit to a tribunal of arbitration.
• Workers were awarded the full 35% wage increase recommended by Gandhi.
Kheda Satyagraha (March–June 1918) Kheda District, Gujarat: Severe drought caused total crop failure. Under the colonial Revenue Code, if the yield was less than one-fourth (25%), farmers were entitled to total remission of land revenue. British officials refused remission and threatened seizure of land and cattle. First Non-Cooperation (Asahyog) in India:
Gandhi advised cultivators to withhold revenue payments peacefully, face property confiscation without resistance, and boycott government auctions.		 Vallabhbhai Patel (abandoned his lucrative legal practice to join Gandhi), Indulal Yagnik, Narhari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya ("Dungli Chor"). Government issued secret orders directing officials to collect revenue only from those peasants who could afford to pay, suspending coercive attachments.
Rowlatt Satyagraha (April 1919) Pan-India: British government passed the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act 1919 (Rowlatt Act) based on Sir Sidney Rowlatt's Sedition Committee. Allowed indefinite detention without trial for up to 2 years, secret in camera trials without right to appeal ("No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"). First Pan-Indian Mass Political Satyagraha:
Gandhi organized the Satyagraha Sabha (Feb 1919) and called for a nationwide Hartal (general strike), fasting, and prayer on April 6, 1919.		 Pan-Indian mobilization; Jinnah, Malaviya, and Mazhar-ul-Haq resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest. Sparked massive nationwide demonstrations; led to widespread British repression and the catastrophic Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

2. The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919)

  • Immediate Context: Arrest of popular nationalist leaders Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal in Amritsar under the Rowlatt Act on April 10, 1919, leading to local riots.
  • The Atrocity (April 13, 1919 - Baisakhi Day):
    • A peaceful gathering of 20,000 men, women, and children gathered in the walled enclosure of Jallianwala Bagh to protest the arrests and celebrate Baisakhi.
    • Acting Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer marched in with Gurkha and Baluchi riflemen, sealed the only narrow exit, and without giving any warning, ordered his troops to fire 1,650 rounds into the trapped crowd until ammunition was exhausted.
    • Officially 379 dead; independent estimates by the Congress inquiry committee established over 1,000 dead.
  • National Repercussions:
    • Rabindranath Tagore renounced his British Knighthood ("The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in the incongruous context of humiliation...").
    • Gandhi renounced his Kaiser-i-Hind gold medal and called off the Rowlatt Satyagraha on April 18, 1919, describing launching mass civil disobedience with an untrained populace as a "Himalayan Blunder".
    • Hunter Commission (Disorders Inquiry Committee): British official inquiry; Dyer was censured and removed from command but exonerated of murder and hailed as the "Savior of the Punjab" by the British House of Lords and British public (who presented him a purse of £26,000 and a jewelled sword).
    • Michael O'Dwyer: The Lieutenant Governor of Punjab who endorsed Dyer's firing was later assassinated on March 13, 1940 at Caxton Hall, London by revolutionary Udham Singh (Shaheed Ram Mohammad Singh Azad).
How It Works / Mechanism

The Gandhian Triad: Satya, Ahimsa and the Moral Subversion of Colonialism

The Mechanics of Mass Conversion via Suffering

Gandhian Satyagraha operated on a psychological mechanism that paralyzed the British colonial apparatus:

  1. The Tactical Disarmament of Imperial Coercion:
    Colonial Hegemony=Physical Brutality×Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")\text{Colonial Hegemony} = \text{Physical Brutality} \times \text{Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")}Colonial Hegemony=Physical Brutality×Moral Justification ("Civilizing Mission")

    • When an empire uses batons and bullets against armed rebels, it easily justifies itself as the protector of public order.
    • But when British police beat unarmed, non-violent, praying men and women who refuse to retaliate or flee, the moral justification for empire shatters in the eyes of world opinion and the British electorate itself.

  2. The Technique of the 25% Refund at Champaran:

    • Why did Gandhi agree to a mere 25% refund of the illegally extorted money from the Champaran planters, when the ryots demanded 100%?
    • Gandhi explained: The monetary amount was secondary; what mattered was that the European planters—who had behaved as hereditary feudal lords above the law—were forced to surrender their prestige, status, and legal invulnerability. Within a decade, all European planters packed up and abandoned Champaran forever.

Spatial & Strategic Dynamics:

  1. Micro-Regional Laboratory to Pan-Indian Stage: Gandhi tested non-violent techniques first on isolated rural peasants (Champaran), urban mill workers (Ahmedabad), and peasant proprietors (Kheda) before launching a pan-Indian movement.
  2. The Canal Colony & Railway Strike: The anti-Rowlatt movement paralyzed major transport nodes in Punjab and Bombay, demonstrating for the first time that urban workers and railway men could enforce total political shutdowns.
  3. The Amritsar Shockwave: Dyer's butchery at Jallianwala Bagh on Baisakhi day shattered forever the moral legitimacy of British rule, converting loyalist moderates (Tagore, Nehru) into resolute anti-colonialists.
Exam Trap & High-Yield Analysis

High-Frequency Traps, Chronological Sequences & Examiner Pitfalls

⚠️ Top 5 High-Frequency Traps for UPSC & State PCS

  1. Chronology of 1918 (The Invariable Prelims Trap):

    • Trap: Thinking Kheda happened before Ahmedabad.
    • Fact: Ahmedabad Mill Strike occurred in March 1918, whereas Kheda Satyagraha occurred from March to June 1918. Remember the sequence: CAKE (Champaran 1917 →\to→ Ahmedabad 1918 →\to→ Kheda 1918).

  2. The "Firsts" in India Classification:

    • First Civil Disobedience (Savinay Avagya): Champaran (1917)
    • First Hunger Strike (Bhookh Hartal): Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918)
    • First Non-Cooperation (Asahyog): Kheda Satyagraha (1918)
    • First Pan-Indian Mass Satyagraha: Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)

  3. Dyer vs O'Dwyer Confusion:

    • Reginald Dyer: Acting Brigadier-General who physically ordered the firing at Jallianwala Bagh on April 13, 1919.
    • Michael O'Dwyer: Lieutenant Governor of Punjab who approved the action and imposed martial law; he was shot dead in London in 1940 by Udham Singh.

  4. Hunter Commission Distinction:

    • Trap: Confusing the 1882 Hunter Commission with the 1919 Hunter Committee.
    • Fact: The 1882 Hunter Commission (chaired by William Wilson Hunter) investigated Indian Education. The 1919 Hunter Committee (Disorders Inquiry Committee, chaired by Lord William Hunter) investigated the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

  5. Title Renunciations:

    • Rabindranath Tagore: Renounced his Knighthood.
    • Mahatma Gandhi: Renounced his Kaiser-i-Hind medal (awarded for ambulance services in the Boer War).
