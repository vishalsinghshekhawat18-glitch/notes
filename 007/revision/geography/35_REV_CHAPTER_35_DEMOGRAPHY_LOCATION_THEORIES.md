### Matrix C: Urban Size-Distribution Laws

| Empirical Law | Scholar & Year | Mathematical Formulation | Axiom & Urban Dynamic | Real-World Diagnostic Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Law of the Primate City** | Mark Jefferson (1939) | $P_1 > 2 \times P_2$ | The largest city is **at least twice as large** as the second-ranking city and disproportionately dominant in economic, political, and cultural influence. | Signifies a centralized, unbalanced, or post-colonial urban system (e.g., Bangkok in Thailand, Paris in France, Lima in Peru). |
| **Rank-Size Rule** | George Kingsley Zipf (1949) | $P_n = \frac{P_1}{n}$ | The population of the $n$-th ranked city is inversely proportional to its rank (Rank 2 is half of Rank 1, Rank 3 is one-third, Rank 4 is one-fourth). | Signifies a mature, integrated, polycentric, and economically diversified urban system (e.g., United States, Germany). |

### Matrix D: Walter Christaller's Central Place Theory (1933)

| Principle | $K$-Multiplier | Geometric Node Location | Calculation Formula | Functional Primary Objective | Settlement Progression |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Marketing Principle** | **$K = 3$** | Lower-order centers at the **6 vertices** of the hexagonal market area. | $1 + 6 \times \left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 3$ | Minimizes consumer travel distance; optimizes market coverage without overlaps. | $1, 3, 9, 27, 81\dots$ ($3^n$) |
| **Transport Principle** | **$K = 4$** | Lower-order centers at the **midpoints of the 6 sides (edges)**. | $1 + 6 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 4$ | Maximizes alignment along straight arterial transit routes; minimizes construction costs. | $1, 4, 16, 64, 256\dots$ ($4^n$) |
| **Administrative Principle** | **$K = 7$** | Lower-order centers **entirely inside** the hexagonal territory. | $1 + 6 \times (1) = 7$ | Undivided sovereign control; jurisdictional integrity for governance, policing, and tax collection. | $1, 7, 49, 343, 2401\dots$ ($7^n$) |

* **Range of a Good**: The maximum physical distance consumers are willing to travel to obtain a good/service.
* **Threshold Population**: The minimum customer population required to make the provision of a good/service commercially viable.
* **Hexagonal Tessellation**: Arises because circular market areas either leave unserved triangles (tangent) or create duplicate wasteful competition (overlapping).

### Matrix E: Alfred Weber's Industrial Location Theory (1909)

| Material Category | Material Index ($MI$) | Mathematical Ratio | Optimal Industrial Plant Location | Real-World Industrial Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Weight-Losing (Gross) Material** | **$MI > 1.0$** | $\frac{\text{Weight of Raw Material}}{\text{Weight of Finished Product}} > 1$ | **Raw Material Source** (To minimize freight on bulk waste) | Iron & Steel, Copper smelting, Sugar mills, Pulp & Paper |
| **Weight-Gaining Material** | **$MI < 1.0$** | $\frac{\text{Weight of Raw Material}}{\text{Weight of Finished Product}} < 1$ | **Market Location** (Finished product heavier due to ubiquitous inputs) | Breweries, Soft drink bottling, Commercial baking |
| **Pure Material** | **$MI = 1.0$** | $\frac{\text{Weight of Raw Material}}{\text{Weight of Finished Product}} = 1$ | **Anywhere along the transit line** (Footloose tendency) | Cotton textile spinning, Precision electronics, Synthetic textiles |

* **Isotim**: A contour line connecting points of equal transport cost for an *individual* commodity (either raw material or finished product).
* **Isodapane**: A contour line connecting points of equal *total* transport costs (sum of raw material freight + finished product freight).
* **Critical Isodapane**: The specific isodapane where labor cost savings at an alternative site exactly balance the increased transport penalty.

## 3. Instant Killer Traps Checklist

- [ ] **Trap 1: Demographic Transition Stage 2 Driver**: The population explosion in Stage 2 is caused **exclusively by a precipitous DROP in the Death Rate (CDR)**, NOT by an increase in the Birth Rate (CBR). CBR stays constant or elevated while CDR plummets due to sanitation, clean water, and medicine.
- [ ] **Trap 2: Jefferson Primate City vs. Zipf Rank-Size**: If the second-largest city is half the size of the first ($P_2 = P_1 / 2$), it follows **Zipf's Rank-Size Rule**, NOT Jefferson's Primacy. Jefferson's **Primate City** requires the largest city to be disproportionately large ($P_1 > 2 \times P_2$), often 3x to 10x larger, completely violating the Rank-Size harmonic progression.
- [ ] **Trap 3: Christaller $K$-Values Vertex vs. Edge Allocations**:
  - In **$K = 3$ (Marketing)**: Centers are at the **vertices (corners)**, shared by 3 hexagons: $1 + 6 \times (1/3) = 3$.
  - In **$K = 4$ (Transport)**: Centers are at the **edges (midpoints of sides)**, shared by 2 hexagons: $1 + 6 \times (1/2) = 4$.
  - In **$K = 7$ (Administrative)**: Centers are **entirely within the hexagon**, undivided: $1 + 6 \times (1) = 7$.
- [ ] **Trap 4: Pure Materials vs. Weight-Losing Materials in Weber's Theory**: Cotton does NOT lose weight during spinning; it is a **Pure Raw Material** ($MI = 1.0$). Therefore, cotton textile mills are historically footloose or market-oriented. In contrast, Sugarcane is a **Weight-Losing (Gross) Material** ($MI \gg 1.0$, sucrose content is only 9–11%), strictly requiring mills to locate directly at sugarcane fields.
- [ ] **Trap 5: Isotim vs. Isodapane Definition**:
  - **Isotim**: Line of equal transport cost for **one individual commodity**.
  - **Isodapane**: Line of equal **total combined transport costs** (raw materials plus finished goods). The *Critical Isodapane* is where labor savings offset total excess freight.
