# ECONOMIC REFORMS & ISSUES FACING THE INDIAN ECONOMY

The transformation of the Indian economy from a closed, state-directed structure into a dynamic, market-driven emerging market was catalysed by the historic 1991 structural reforms. However, despite rapid aggregate GDP growth, the economy contends with persistent structural challenges including multidimensional poverty, jobless growth, wealth inequality, distress migration, and post-pandemic economic adjustments. This chapter integrates Economic Reforms (Unit 07) and Issues Facing the Indian Economy (Unit 11).

---

## § 7.1 Unit 07: Economic Reforms

> **Curriculum Alignment — Official IIBF Paper 1 Benchmark (Unit 07)**  
> **Core Proposition:** The July 1991 Structural Adjustment Programme dismantled the Industrial Licensing ('Licence-Permit Raj') regime, opened the public sector to private competition, transitioned the rupee to market-determined exchange rates, and initiated continuous waves of financial, fiscal, and digital reforms.

### 1. The Pre-1991 Crisis Context & Triggers

• **Structural Flaws:** State-directed planning prioritized heavy public sector undertakings (PSUs), protected domestic industry behind prohibitive tariffs (> 300%), and enforced rigid administrative controls via the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951, and Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (MRTP) Act, 1969.  
• **The 1991 Balance of Payments (BoP) Shock:**
  - *Immediate Triggers:* The 1990–91 Gulf War spiked crude oil import bills and severed worker remittances from West Asia. Commercial external debt service obligations mounted alongside twin fiscal and current account deficits.
  - *Critical Liquidity Insolvency:* India's foreign exchange reserves plummeted to approximately **$1.2 Billion in June 1991**—barely sufficient to finance three weeks of essential imports.
  - *Emergency Measures:* The Government of India and the Reserve Bank of India pledged **67 tonnes of physical gold reserves** (airlifted to the Bank of England and Union Bank of Switzerland) to secure emergency sovereign bilateral loans, prior to securing an IMF structural adjustment facility.

### 2. The Structural Reform Triad: Liberalization, Privatization, Globalization (LPG)

| Reform Dimension | Core Policy Action Initiated | Structural Impact on the Economy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Liberalization** | • Abolished Industrial Licensing for all except a tiny strategic list (defense, explosives, hazardous chemicals).<br>• Dismantled the MRTP Act investment limits, replacing it later with the **Competition Act, 2002**.<br>• Phased de-reservation of items reserved exclusively for Small Scale Industries (SSI). | Ended bureaucratic production caps; fostered market-driven entry, scale, and technological modernization. |
| **Privatization** | • Reduced the list of industries reserved exclusively for the public sector from 17 to only 2 (Atomic Energy and Railway Operations).<br>• Commenced PSU disinvestment and strategic divestments (e.g., VSNL, Maruti, IPCL).<br>• Opened banking, insurance, telecom, and aviation to private commercial enterprise. | Infused private capital, corporate governance, and operational efficiency into capital-intensive sectors. |
| **Globalization** | • **Two-step Rupee Devaluation** in July 1991 (~18–19%).<br>• Transitioned through the **Liberalized Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS, 1992)** to a unified market exchange rate (1993).<br>• Accepted **IMF Article VIII obligations (August 1994)** for full Current Account Convertibility.<br>• Liberalized FDI caps across majority sectors under the Automatic Approval route. | Integrated domestic production into global value chains; boosted IT/ITeS services exports and foreign capital reserves. |

### 3. Chronology of Landmark Reform Committees

1. **Narasimham Committee I (1991) & II (1998):** Financial system restructuring; reduction of statutory reserves (CRR/SLR), 90-day NPA classification, 8% Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR), deregulation of interest rates, and banking consolidation.
2. **Raja Chelliah Committee on Tax Reforms (1991):** Simplification of direct taxes, lowering corporate and personal income-tax brackets, rationalization of indirect excise duties into VAT.
3. **Khan Committee (1998):** Harmonising the role of Commercial Banks and Development Financial Institutions; established the blueprint for **Universal Banking**.
4. **Vijay Kelkar Committee (2002):** Blueprint for the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, and the Goods and Services Tax (GST) framework.
5. **Urjit Patel Committee (2014):** Transitioned RBI monetary policy from multiple indicators to **Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT)** anchored by CPI, leading to the statutory creation of the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) in 2016.

### 4. Next-Generation Structural Economic Reforms (2014–Present)

• **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016:** Shifted corporate restructuring from 'debtor-in-possession' to 'creditor-in-control', mandating a 180/330-day resolution timeline under NCLT.  
• **Goods and Services Tax (GST), 2017:** Subsumed 17 central and state indirect levies into a unified destination-based tax under the cooperative federalism of the GST Council.  
• **Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI):** The JAM Trinity (Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile) and UPI rails transformed welfare transfers via Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT), eliminating leakages.  
• **Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Schemes:** Performance-linked manufacturing subsidies across 14 strategic sectors (semiconductors, electronics, solar, pharmaceuticals) to foster domestic scale.

## § 7.2 Unit 11: Issues Facing the Indian Economy

> **Curriculum Alignment — Official IIBF Paper 1 Benchmark (Unit 11)**  
> **Core Proposition:** Sustained high aggregate GDP growth coexists with deep structural headwinds: rural-urban consumption divides, multidimensional poverty, informal and jobless growth, wealth concentration, distress migration, and the economic scarring of external shocks.

### 1. Poverty Estimation Methodologies in India

| Committee / Body | Year | Poverty Line Metric & Calorie / Consumption Criterion | Headcount Ratio (HCR) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dandekar and Rath** | 1971 | Minimum daily intake of **2,250 calories per capita** in both rural and urban areas. | ~40% (Rural), ~50% (Urban) |
| **Y.K. Alagh Task Force** | 1979 | Nutrition anchor: **2,400 calories/day** for rural areas; **2,100 calories/day** for urban areas. | Official Planning Commission standard. |
| **D.T. Lakdawala Committee** | 1993 | Retained Alagh calorie norms; introduced **state-specific poverty lines** updated using CPI-AL (rural) and CPI-IW (urban). | ~36% poverty headcount. |
| **Suresh Tendulkar Committee** | 2009 | Shifted from calorie intake to **Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure (MPCE)** on health, education, and nutrition. Poverty lines (2011–12): **₹816/month (₹27.2/day) rural**; **₹1,000/month (₹33.3/day) urban**. | **21.9%** (National HCR for 2011–12: 25.7% rural, 13.7% urban). |
| **C. Rangarajan Committee** | 2014 | Independent nutritional and non-food basket. Poverty lines (2011–12): **₹972/month (₹32/day) rural**; **₹1,407/month (₹47/day) urban**. | **29.5%** (National HCR for 2011–12). |

### 2. NITI Aayog National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

• **Methodology:** Adapted from the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and UNDP **Alkire-Foster method**.  
• **Structure:** Assesses poverty across **3 equally weighted dimensions** (Health, Education, Standard of Living) spanning **12 indicators**:
  1. *Health (1/3 Weight):* Nutrition, Child & Adolescent Mortality, Maternal Health.
  2. *Education (1/3 Weight):* Years of Schooling, School Attendance.
  3. *Standard of Living (1/3 Weight):* Cooking Fuel, Sanitation, Drinking Water, Electricity, Housing, Assets, Bank Account.
• **Multidimensional Headcount Reduction:** As per NITI Aayog's MPI benchmarks, multidimensional poverty in India declined steeply from **24.85% in 2015–16** to **14.96% in 2019–21** and further to approximately **11.28% in 2022–23**, representing the exit of over **135+ million individuals** from multidimensional deprivation.

### 3. Jobless Growth & Informal Employment Dynamics

• **The Jobless Growth Anomaly:** A phenomenon where an economy registers rapid aggregate GDP growth (6–8% annually), but employment elasticity remains extremely low (< 0.1), failing to create proportionate formal employment for new labor market entrants.  
• **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Core Metrics:**
  - *Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR):* Percentage of persons in the labor force (working or seeking work) in the population. Driven upward in recent years by rising rural female LFPR.
  - *Worker Population Ratio (WPR):* Percentage of employed persons in the total population.
  - *Unemployment Rate (UR):* Percentage of persons unemployed among the labor force.
• **High Informality:** Nearly **88% to 90%** of total employment remains in the informal/unorganized sector, characterized by absence of written contracts, social security, and paid leave.

### 4. Economic Inequalities & The Gini Coefficient

• **Lorenz Curve:** A graphical representation of the cumulative distribution of national income or wealth against the cumulative percentage of population.  
• **Gini Coefficient:** The mathematical ratio of the area between the line of absolute equality and the Lorenz curve to the total area below the line of equality:
  $$G = \frac{A}{A + B}$$
  - A Gini coefficient of **0 indicates absolute equality** (every person has identical income).
  - A Gini coefficient of **1 indicates absolute inequality** (one individual captures all income).
• **Inequality Realities in India:** Income and wealth concentration indices indicate that the top 1% of the population holds over 40% of national wealth, while the bottom 50% accounts for barely 3% of aggregate wealth.

### 5. Distress Migration & Resource Degradation

• **Distress Migration:** Driven by agrarian unviability, seasonal crop failure, and rural debt, millions of circular migrant workers move to Tier-I urban centres, resulting in the proliferation of informal slums and overburdening municipal utilities.  
• **Environmental and Resource Degradation:**
  - *Groundwater Stress:* India extracts more groundwater than China and the USA combined (~25% of global extraction), driven by electricity subsidies for tube wells in the Green Revolution belt.
  - *Composite Water Management Index (CWMI):* NITI Aayog warned of severe water stress across 21 major cities.

### 6. Pandemic Economic Shock & Recovery Trajectory

• **COVID-19 Disruption:** Stringent national lockdowns in FY 2020–21 led to a historic GDP contraction of -5.8%. Unorganized workers, MSMEs, and contact-intensive services suffered severe cash flow crunches.  
• **The K-Shaped Recovery:**
  - Upper Arm of the 'K': Formal listed corporations, IT/tech firms, and capital-intensive industries experienced record profits and rapid digital scaling.
  - Lower Arm of the 'K': Informal labor, rural micro-enterprises, and unorganized service workers experienced wage stagnation and depleted household savings.  
• **Policy Buffer Mechanisms:**
  - *PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY):* Free foodgrain entitlement to over 80 Crore citizens, acting as a crucial social floor.
  - *Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme (ECLGS):* 100% credit guarantee from the government for bank lending to stressed MSMEs, disbursing over ₹3.7 Lakh Crore.

### Examiner Trap Vault: Unit 07 & Unit 11 High-Yield Distractors

1. **Trap — Tendulkar vs Rangarajan Poverty Headcount:** The Tendulkar Committee estimated the 2011–12 poverty headcount at **21.9%**, whereas the Rangarajan Committee estimated it at **29.5%**. Rangarajan's poverty line is higher (₹32 rural / ₹47 urban per day) than Tendulkar's (₹27.2 rural / ₹33.3 urban per day).
2. **Trap — NITI Aayog MPI Indicators:** The National Multidimensional Poverty Index uses **12 indicators** across 3 dimensions (adding Maternal Health and Bank Account to the 10 global MPI indicators).
3. **Trap — 1991 Forex Reserve Dip:** At the peak of the BoP crisis in June 1991, India's foreign exchange reserves were approximately **$1.2 Billion** (barely 3 weeks of imports), not zero.
4. **Trap — Gini Coefficient Extremes:** 0 represents **perfect equality**; 1 represents **perfect inequality**. A higher Gini coefficient denotes **greater inequality**.

Statement II: The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1991 abolished industrial licensing for all domestic industries without any exceptions.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?  
A. Statement I only  
B. Statement II only  
C. Both Statement I and Statement II  
D. Neither Statement I nor Statement II  

**Q4. The Gini Coefficient of an economy is mathematically derived from which of the following graphical representations?**

---

### Answer Key & Explanations

• **Q1 — Answer: B.** Tendulkar discarded the fixed calorie intake anchor and developed consumption expenditure baskets that accounted for private spending on education, health, and transport.  
• **Q2 — Answer: D.** NITI Aayog adapted the 10 global indicators by adding Maternal Health (under Health) and Bank Account (under Standard of Living), making a total of 12 indicators.  
• **Q3 — Answer: A.** Statement I is correct (Current Account Convertibility was adopted under Article VIII in August 1994). Statement II is incorrect because industrial licensing was retained for a small list of strategic/hazardous industries (defense, hazardous chemicals, industrial explosives, cigars).  
• **Q4 — Answer: B.** The Gini coefficient is calculated as the ratio of the area between the line of absolute equality and the Lorenz curve to the total area under the equality line.
