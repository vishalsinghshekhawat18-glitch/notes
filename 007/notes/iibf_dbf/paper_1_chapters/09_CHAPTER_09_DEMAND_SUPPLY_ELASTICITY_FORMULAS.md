# LAW OF DEMAND, SUPPLY & ELASTICITY FORMULAS

The mechanics of price determination in market economies depend on the interaction between market demand and supply curves. In banking and treasury management, elasticity principles govern the interest-rate sensitivity of deposits, credit product pricing, and corporate borrower revenue forecasts.

## § 9.1 Unit 13: Supply and Demand

> **Curriculum Alignment — Official IIBF Paper 1 Benchmark (Unit 13)**  
> **Core Proposition:** The Law of Demand posits an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, driven by income and substitution effects. Price, income, and cross-price elasticities measure the degree of quantity responsiveness to exogenous shocks, quantified via point, arc, and total expenditure formulas.

### 1. The Laws of Demand and Supply

• **Law of Demand:** Ceteris paribus (all other factors held constant), as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, yielding a downward-sloping demand curve:
  $$\frac{\Delta Q_d}{\Delta P} < 0$$
  - *Substitution Effect:* As a good becomes cheaper relative to other goods, consumers substitute toward it.
  - *Income Effect:* A fall in price increases the consumer's real purchasing power, enabling higher consumption of normal goods.
• **Exceptions to the Law of Demand (Upward-Sloping Demand Curves):**
  - **Giffen Goods:** Highly inferior basic staples (e.g. coarse grain/potatoes) lacking close substitutes. The negative income effect is so potent that it overpowers the substitution effect; a price rise forces poor households to consume *more* of the staple.
  - **Veblen Goods (Conspicuous Consumption):** Luxury prestige items (designer jewelry, exotic sports cars) whose demand rises with price because higher prices signal wealth and social prestige.
• **Law of Supply:** Ceteris paribus, as the price of a commodity increases, producers expand quantity supplied, generating an upward-sloping supply curve:
  $$\frac{\Delta Q_s}{\Delta P} > 0$$
• **Movement Along vs. Shift in Curves:**
  - *Movement Along Curve:* Caused **solely by changes in the commodity's own price** (Expansion/Contraction of demand).
  - *Shift of Curve:* Caused by changes in **external non-price variables** such as consumer income, tastes, input prices, or government taxes (Increase/Decrease in demand).

### 2. Price Elasticity of Demand ($e_p$) & Core Mathematical Formulas

Price elasticity of demand measures the percentage responsiveness of quantity demanded to a percentage change in own price:

$$e_p = - \left( \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta P} \right) = - \left( \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q} \right)$$

#### A. Point Price Elasticity & Geometric Linear Method
For infinitesimal price changes along a continuous demand curve:

$$e_p = - \frac{dQ}{dP} \times \frac{P}{Q}$$

On a straight-line linear downward-sloping demand curve, elasticity at any given point is given by:

$$e_p = \frac{\text{Length of Lower Segment}}{\text{Length of Upper Segment}}$$

• At the vertical intercept ($P$-axis): Elasticity is **Infinite ($e_p = \infty$)**.  
• Above the midpoint: **Relatively Elastic ($e_p > 1$)**.  
• At the exact midpoint: **Unitary Elastic ($e_p = 1$)**.  
• Below the midpoint: **Relatively Inelastic ($e_p < 1$)**.  
• At the horizontal intercept ($Q$-axis): Elasticity is **Zero ($e_p = 0$)**.

#### B. Arc Elasticity of Demand (Midpoint Formula)
When evaluating discrete, large price movements between two distinct points $(P_1, Q_1)$ and $(P_2, Q_2)$:

$$e_{\text{arc}} = - \frac{\frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_1 + Q_2)/2}}{\frac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_1 + P_2)/2}} = - \left( \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{P_2 - P_1} \right) \times \left( \frac{P_1 + P_2}{Q_1 + Q_2} \right)$$

#### C. The Five Degrees of Price Elasticity

| Elasticity Value | Terminology | Description | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$e_p = 0$** | **Perfecty Inelastic** | Quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes (Vertical curve). | Life-saving insulin, salt. |
| **$0 < e_p < 1$** | **Relatively Inelastic** | Percentage change in quantity is smaller than percentage change in price (Steep curve). | Essential medicines, electricity, basic food staples. |
| **$e_p = 1$** | **Unitary Elastic** | Percentage change in quantity equals percentage change in price (Rectangular Hyperbola). | Standard consumer items. |
| **$e_p > 1$** | **Relatively Elastic** | Percentage change in quantity is greater than percentage change in price (Flatter curve). | Consumer electronics, airline leisure travel, luxury apparel. |
| **$e_p = \infty$** | **Perfecty Elastic** | At a specific price, demand is infinite; any price hike reduces demand to zero (Horizontal curve). | Goods under Perfect Competition. |

#### D. Total Outlay / Expenditure Method (Alfred Marshall)

| Direction of Price Change | Behavior of Total Expenditure ($P \times Q$) | Elasticity Implication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Price Falls ($\downarrow$) or Rises ($\uparrow$) | Total Expenditure moves in **OPPOSITE** direction | **Elastic Demand ($e_p > 1$)** |
| Price Falls ($\downarrow$) or Rises ($\uparrow$) | Total Expenditure remains **UNCHANGED** | **Unitary Elastic Demand ($e_p = 1$)** |
| Price Falls ($\downarrow$) or Rises ($\uparrow$) | Total Expenditure moves in **SAME** direction | **Inelastic Demand ($e_p < 1$)** |

### 3. Cross-Price Elasticity ($e_{xy}$) & Income Elasticity ($e_y$)

#### A. Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand ($e_{xy}$)
Measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded of good $X$ to changes in the price of good $Y$:

$$e_{xy} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_x}{\% \Delta P_y} = \frac{\Delta Q_x}{\Delta P_y} \times \frac{P_y}{Q_x}$$

• **Substitute Goods ($e_{xy} > 0$):** Positive value. When price of tea rises, demand for coffee increases.  
• **Complementary Goods ($e_{xy} < 0$):** Negative value. When price of petrol rises, demand for cars decreases.  
• **Unrelated Goods ($e_{xy} = 0$):** Zero value. Price of shoes has no effect on demand for apples.

#### B. Income Elasticity of Demand ($e_y$)
Measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in consumer income ($Y$):

$$e_y = \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta Y} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta Y} \times \frac{Y}{Q}$$

• **Normal Goods ($e_y > 0$):** Demand increases as consumer income rises.
  - *Necessities ($0 < e_y < 1$):* Quantity increases by a smaller percentage than income (Engel's Law: budget share on food declines as income rises).
  - *Luxuries / Superior Goods ($e_y > 1$):* Quantity increases by a larger percentage than income.  
• **Inferior Goods ($e_y < 0$):** Demand decreases as consumer income rises (consumers shift away from low-quality substitutes).

### Examiner Trap Vault: Unit 13 High-Yield Distractors

1. **Trap — Rectangular Hyperbola Elasticity:** A demand curve shaped as a **rectangular hyperbola** exhibits a price elasticity of demand **strictly equal to 1 (Unitary Elastic) at every single point** along the curve, and total consumer expenditure remains identical regardless of price changes.
2. **Trap — Cross Elasticity Sign:** Substitutes have **POSITIVE** cross elasticity; Complementary goods have **NEGATIVE** cross elasticity.
3. **Trap — Giffen vs Inferior Goods:** All Giffen goods are inferior goods, but **NOT all inferior goods are Giffen goods**. An inferior good is only a Giffen good if its negative income effect is larger than its substitution effect, producing an upward-sloping demand curve.

### Unit 13 Practice Questions (IIBF DB&F Pattern)

**Q1. If the price of a banking financial service drops by 10% and the quantity demanded expands by 15%, the price elasticity of demand ($e_p$) for this service is:**  
A. 0.67 (Inelastic)  
B. 1.00 (Unitary Elastic)  
C. 1.50 (Elastic)  
D. -0.67 (Inelastic)  

**Q2. When the price of good Y increases from ₹20 to ₹25, the quantity demanded of good X increases from 100 units to 120 units. What is the cross-price elasticity of demand between X and Y, and what is their relationship?**  
A. +0.80; Substitute goods  
B. -0.80; Complementary goods  
C. +1.25; Independent goods  
D. -1.25; Inferior goods  

**Q3. According to Alfred Marshall's Total Outlay Method, if a commercial bank reduces the interest rate on home loans and its total interest revenue collected decreases, the demand for home loans is:**  
A. Price elastic ($e_p > 1$)  
B. Price inelastic ($e_p < 1$)  
C. Unitary elastic ($e_p = 1$)  
D. Perfectly elastic ($e_p = \infty$)  

---

### Answer Key & Explanations

• **Q1 — Answer: C.** $$e_p = \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{15\%}{10\%} = 1.50$$. Since $e_p > 1$, demand is relatively elastic.  
• **Q2 — Answer: A.** Percentage change in price of Y: $\frac{25 - 20}{20} \times 100 = 25\%$. Percentage change in quantity of X: $\frac{120 - 100}{100} \times 100 = 20\%$. Cross elasticity $e_{xy} = \frac{20\%}{25\%} = +0.80$. A positive cross-elasticity indicates that X and Y are **substitute goods**.  
• **Q3 — Answer: B.** Under the Total Outlay method, if price (interest rate) falls and total expenditure (interest revenue) also falls in the same direction, the demand is price inelastic ($e_p < 1$).
