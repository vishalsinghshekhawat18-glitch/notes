# BANKER-CUSTOMER RELATIONSHIP, RIGHTS & STATUTORY DUTIES

The banker-customer relationship forms the legal cornerstone of commercial banking operations under English Common Law, the Indian Contract Act 1872, and the Banking Regulation Act 1949. Depending upon the transaction, the relationship shifts dynamically from debtor-creditor in deposit accounts to bailor-bailee in safe custody, trustee-beneficiary in escrow, and principal-agent in collection mandates.

## § 1.1 Comprehensive Operational & Legal Analysis

Banker-Customer Relationship, Rights & Duties

> **Key Concept — Pivotal Concept: Multi-Faceted Legal Relationships in Banking**
> The relationship between a banker and a customer is fundamentally contractual and depends upon the exact nature of the transaction being executed.

## 2. Core Rights of a Banker

Under Indian law and banking conventions, bankers enjoy specific statutory rights to protect their advances and manage operational risks:

• **1. Right of General Lien (Section 171, Indian Contract Act 1872):** A general lien allows a banker to retain any goods, securities, or commercial paper delivered in the ordinary course of business as security for a general balance of account. Unlike a particular lien (which attaches only to the specific goods repaired/handled), a general lien applies across all outstanding obligations unless there is an express contract to the contrary.
• **Exclusions from General Lien:** Safe custody articles, securities left by mistake, money deposited for a specific unfulfilled purpose, and shares deposited for sale through broker.
• **2. Right of Set-Off:** The legal right to merge/combine two accounts of the same customer (one in debit and one in credit) to determine the net liability. Conditions: Debts must be mutual, due and payable immediately, ascertained in sum, and in the *same right and same capacity*.
• **3. Right of Appropriation (Clayton's Rule):** Governed by Sections 59, 60, and 61 of the Indian Contract Act. Under Clayton's Case (1816), in running cash credit/overdraft accounts with a deceased or retired partner/guarantor, payments into the account extinguish the earliest debits first. To preserve guarantor liability, the bank must immediately break the old account and open a new separate account.
• **4. Right to Charge Interest & Incidental Charges:** Permitted under express/implied contract and RBI Prudential Norms.

## 3. Banker's Duty of Secrecy & Statutory Exceptions

Section 13 of the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970/1980 mandates strict confidentiality of customer account affairs. Established under Tournier v. National Provincial and Union Bank of England (1924), exceptions exist only under 4 specific heads:

• **1. Disclosure under Compulsion of Law:** Orders under Section 226(3) Income Tax Act, Section 91 CrPC, Section 43C PMLA, Banker's Books Evidence Act 1891, and Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA).
• **2. Disclosure in Public Interest:** Prevention of national security threats, terror financing, or major public frauds.
• **3. Disclosure in the Bank's Own Interest:** Filing a legal recovery suit against a defaulting borrower, enforcing SARFAESI, or claiming insurance.
• **4. Disclosure with Customer's Express or Implied Consent:** Providing trade opinions upon written authorization, credit bureau sharing (CIBIL/Experian) under Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act 2005.

> **Exam Anchor & Trap:**
> Top Exam Traps on Banker-Customer Relationship:
1. **Locker Relationship vs Safe Custody:** Hiring a locker creates a **Lessor-Lessee** relationship, NOT Bailor-Bailee. The bank does not take possession or knowledge of the locker contents. Safe custody, conversely, is **Bailee-Bailor**.
2. **Right of Set-off Capacity Trap:** A bank CANNOT set off a customer's individual credit balance against a partnership debit balance without specific authorization, nor set off trust accounts against personal liabilities.
3. **Clayton's Rule Operation:** If a partner in a firm dies and the bank continues the running overdraft account without breaking it, subsequent deposits by surviving partners automatically reduce the deceased partner's liability, releasing his estate.

### Practice Questions & Solved Numerical Drills (IIBF Pattern)

**Q1:** When a customer deposits cash into an operative current account, what is the precise legal relationship established between the bank and the customer?
• (A) Trustee (Bank) and Beneficiary (Customer)
• (B) Debtor (Bank) and Creditor (Customer)
• (C) Bailee (Bank) and Bailor (Customer)
• (D) Agent (Bank) and Principal (Customer)

**Q2:** Under Section 171 of the Indian Contract Act 1872, a banker possesses a general lien over customer securities. Over which of the following does Banker’s General Lien NOT apply?
• (A) Bills handed over for collection
• (B) Securities deposited for safe custody
• (C) Matured fixed deposits held in the bank’s custody
• (D) Cheques deposited for clearance

**Q3:** Under the landmark Tournier v. National Provincial Bank ruling and statutory norms, in which situation is a banker legally justified in disclosing customer account confidential details?
• (A) Oral request by a local police sub-inspector without warrant
• (B) Demand by customer’s spouse for household expense verification
• (C) Compulsion of law under Section 133 of the Income Tax Act 1961
• (D) Commercial inquiry by a competing private finance company

#### Solutions & Detailed Explanations

1. **(B) Debtor (Bank) and Creditor (Customer)** — Deposit money becomes the property of the bank, and the bank is indebted to the customer with an obligation to repay on demand (Foley v. Hill).

2. **(B) Securities deposited for safe custody** — General Lien applies only to goods and securities coming into the bank's hands in the ordinary course of business as a banker. Safe custody is a bailment for a specific purpose, superseding general lien.

3. **(C) Compulsion of law under Section 133 of the Income Tax Act 1961** — Banker's duty of secrecy is subject to four statutory exceptions: (1) Under compulsion of law, (2) Duty to the public, (3) Interests of the bank, and (4) Express/implied consent of the customer.

<summary>Distinguish between Banker's General Lien and Particular Lien, and identify when Banker's Lien operates as an "Implied Pledge".</summary>
A Particular Lien (Section 170 Contract Act) gives the right to retain goods only for the specific claim/service rendered on those goods. A Banker's General Lien (Section 171) entitles the banker to retain any security handed over to them in the ordinary course of banking for any general balance of account. By judicial precedent (Brandao v. Barnett), a banker's general lien operates as an "implied pledge", granting the banker the right to sell the retained securities after giving reasonable statutory notice to the borrower.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the Clayton's Case Rule (1816) and how does it govern appropriation of payments in running overdraft accounts?</summary>
Clayton's Case (Devaynes v. Noble) establishes the rule of chronological appropriation in a running broken account: in the absence of specific appropriation by the debtor or creditor, the earliest credit item satisfies the earliest debit item. In an unbroken running overdraft, deposits by an ongoing firm extinguish the old guaranteed debt first, potentially discharging existing guarantors unless the account is broken/frozen upon notice of death/retirement.
</details>
