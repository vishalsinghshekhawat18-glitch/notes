## 4.1 The Epistemic Evolution of Money

Before money existed, economic trade relied on the **Barter System** (direct exchange of goods for goods). Barter collapses at scale due to the intractable friction of **Double Coincidence of Wants** (Person A must want what Person B has, and Person B must want what Person A has simultaneously).

### The Four Universal Functions of Money (Walker & Crowther Formulation):

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Category of Function            │ Structural Economic Role                                    │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Primary Functions            │ • Medium of Exchange: Eliminates double coincidence of wants│
│                                 │ • Unit of Account / Measure of Value: Common denominator    │
│                                 │   pricing all goods (e.g. Rupee, Dollar).                   │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Secondary Functions          │ • Standard of Deferred Payments: Facilitates credit, loans, │
│                                 │   and future contracts.                                     │
│                                 │ • Store of Value: Allows purchasing power to be transferred │
│                                 │   from present to future without physical decay.            │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Contingent Functions         │ • Distribution of National Income among factor shares.      │
│                                 │ • Basis of the credit system (Fractional Reserve Banking).  │
│                                 │ • Equalization of Marginal Utilities across consumer baskets│
└─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 4.2 Typology & Legal Architecture of Money

Money has evolved through five historical stages:
$$\text{Commodity Money (Salt, Cattle)} \longrightarrow \text{Metallic Money (Gold/Silver)} \longrightarrow \text{Paper Money} \longrightarrow \text{Credit/Bank Money} \longrightarrow \text{Digital/CBDC}$$

### 1. Fiat Money vs. Fiduciary Money
* **Fiat Money**: Currency issued by the sovereign decree/command of the State. It has **no intrinsic value** (the paper of a ₹500 note is worth pennies). Its value derives entirely from the sovereign guarantee printed on its face:
  > *"I promise to pay the bearer the sum of Five Hundred Rupees"* — Governor, Reserve Bank of India.
* **Fiduciary Money**: Money accepted as a medium of exchange based on mutual **trust** between the payer and payee, not by legal mandate (e.g. bank cheques, drafts, bills of exchange). A merchant can legally refuse a cheque.

---

### 2. Legal Tender: Limited vs. Unlimited Legal Tender

**Legal Tender** is fiat money that cannot be legally refused by any citizen of the country for the discharge of a commercial or financial debt.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┐
│ Feature                         │ Unlimited Legal Tender          │ Limited Legal Tender            │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ Governing Statute               │ Section 26(1) of RBI Act, 1934  │ Coinage Act, 2011 (Section 6)   │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ Denominations                   │ All RBI Banknotes (₹2, ₹5, ₹10, │ All minted coins (50 paise,     │
│                                 │ ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500).    │ ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20).          │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ Legal Payment Limit             │ **Unlimited Amount**: Can be    │ **Restricted Limits**:          │
│                                 │ used to settle any legal debt to│ • Coins ≥ ₹1: Legal tender  │
│                                 │ any monetary value.             │   up to **₹1,000 only**.        │
│                                 │                                 │ • 50 paise coins: Legal tender  │
│                                 │                                 │   up to **₹10 only**.           │
│                                 │                                 │ *A creditor can legally refuse  │
│                                 │                                 │ coins exceeding these limits.*  │
└─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **The Auto-Rickshaw Legal Nuance**:
> Is an auto-rickshaw driver compelled by law to accept a ₹500 note for a ride?
> * If you are standing on the pavement negotiating a ride, **NO**. He is under no obligation to enter a contract with you.
> * Once the journey is completed, a legal **debt has been created**. At that point, he **CANNOT refuse** legal tender banknotes to discharge that debt.

### The Minimum Reserve System (Section 33 of RBI Act, 1934)
Prior to 1956, RBI issued currency backed by a proportional reserve system. In 1956, India adopted the **Minimum Reserve System**:
* RBI’s Issue Department must maintain total backing assets of at least **₹200 Crore** at all times.
* Of this ₹200 Crore:
  * At least **₹115 Crore must be held in Gold Bullion and Gold Coins**.
  * The remaining **₹85 Crore** can be held in **Foreign Securities / Sovereign Government Securities**.
* Beyond this statutory ₹200 Crore floor, RBI can issue currency as required by the economy, backed by Government Securities and Foreign Exchange Assets.

## 4.4 The Stock of Money Supply: Defining "The Public"

$$\text{Currency in Circulation} = \text{Currency with the Public} + \text{Cash in Hand with Commercial Banks}$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE FUNDAMENTAL RULE OF MONEY SUPPLY**:
> Money Supply measures the stock of money held exclusively by the **USERS of money (The Public: Households, Businesses, Non-Bank Entities)**.
> It **STRICTLY EXCLUDES** all cash and deposits held by the **CREATORS / SUPPLIERS of money**:
> 1. Cash held in the vaults of Commercial Banks (**Cash in hand / Vault Cash**).
> 2. Cash and balances held by the **Central Government** and **State Governments**.
> 3. Cash held inside the **Reserve Bank of India**.
> 4. **Inter-bank deposits** (deposits of one commercial bank with another bank).
> *Why?* Counting vault cash or inter-bank deposits would cause severe double-counting, because these reserves serve as the backing for secondary bank deposits created for the public.

## 4.5 The Classical Monetary Aggregates ($M_0, M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4$)

RBI publishes monetary data under four classical measures of money supply, plus High-Powered Base Money:

```
                            THE LIQUIDITY SPECTRUM
   ◄────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►
   HIGH LIQUIDITY / NARROW MONEY                   LOW LIQUIDITY / BROAD MONEY
              M1                 M2                 M3                 M4
```

### 1. High-Powered Money / Reserve Money ($M_0$ / Base Money / Central Bank Money)
The monetary base created directly by the Central Bank:
$$\mathbf{M_0} = \text{Currency in Circulation} + \text{Bankers' Deposits with RBI} + \text{'Other' Deposits with RBI}$$
* **Bankers' Deposits with RBI**: Required reserves (Cash Reserve Ratio - CRR) and excess voluntary operational reserves maintained by commercial banks with the RBI.
* **'Other' Deposits with RBI**: Quasi-government balances, foreign central bank accounts, and international financial institutions (IMF/World Bank) held at RBI. (Excludes Central/State Government deposits).

---

### 2. The Four Money Supply Measures ($M_1$ to $M_4$):

$$\mathbf{M_1 \text{ (Narrow Money)}} = \text{Currency with the Public} + \text{Demand Deposits with Banking System} + \text{'Other' Deposits with RBI}$$
* **Demand Deposits**: Current Accounts and Savings Account deposits (CASA) that can be withdrawn or transferred on demand via cheques/UPI/ATMs without penalty.
* **$M_1$ is the most liquid** measure of money supply.

$$\mathbf{M_2} = M_1 + \text{Savings Deposits with Post Office Savings Banks}$$
* Post office savings accounts are liquid, but post offices are not part of the commercial cheque-clearing network (though modernization via India Post Payments Bank has bridged this gap).

$$\mathbf{M_3 \text{ (Broad Money)}} = M_1 + \text{Time (Term / Fixed) Deposits with the Banking System}$$
* **Time Deposits**: Fixed Deposits (FDs) and Recurring Deposits (RDs) with contracted maturity periods. Cannot be withdrawn on demand via cheque without penalty.
* **$M_3$ is the most comprehensive and universally monitored measure of money supply in India.** When economists refer to "Money Supply growth in India", they mean $M_3$.

$$\mathbf{M_4} = M_3 + \text{Total Deposits with Post Office Savings Banks (Excluding National Savings Certificates - NSC)}$$
* **$M_4$ is the least liquid** monetary aggregate. National Savings Certificates are excluded because they are locked long-term fiscal savings instruments.

---

### Matrix: Classical Money Supply Aggregates

| Aggregate | Nomenclature | Formula / Components | Liquidity Rank | Primary Operational Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$M_0$** | Reserve / Base Money | $\text{Currency in Circ} + \text{Bankers' Balances with RBI} + \text{'Other' Deposits}$ | High (Base) | The monetary foundation supporting all credit |
| **$M_1$** | Narrow Money | $\text{Currency with Public} + \text{Demand Deposits (CASA)} + \text{'Other' Deposits}$ | **Rank 1 (Most Liquid)**| Immediate transactional purchasing power |
| **$M_2$** | Narrow Money | $M_1 + \text{Post Office Savings Bank Deposits}$ | Rank 2 | Captures non-bank retail savings |
| **$M_3$** | **Broad Money** | $M_1 + \text{Time (Term) Deposits with Commercial Banks}$ | Rank 3 | **Official Headline Measure of Money Supply** |
| **$M_4$** | Broad Money | $M_3 + \text{Total Post Office Deposits (excl. NSC)}$ | **Rank 4 (Least Liquid)**| Complete national deposit umbrella |

## 4.7 The Money Multiplier & Fractional Reserve Banking

How does the Reserve Bank’s injection of ₹100 of Reserve Money turn into ₹500 or ₹600 of broad money in the real economy? Through **Fractional Reserve Banking** and the **Money Multiplier**.

### The Mechanism of Multiple Deposit Creation
Commercial banks do not keep 100% of customer deposits idle in vaults. They keep a legally mandated fraction (**Legal Reserve Ratio = $\text{CRR} + \text{SLR}$**) and lend out the remainder.

```
Individual A deposits ₹1,000 in Bank 1 (Assume CRR + SLR = 20%)
       │
       ▼
Bank 1 keeps ₹200 in reserve; lends ₹800 to Borrower B
       │
       ▼
Borrower B pays ₹800 to Merchant C, who deposits ₹800 in Bank 2
       │
       ▼
Bank 2 keeps ₹160 in reserve; lends ₹640 to Borrower D
       │
       ▼
[Process repeats across the banking system until initial ₹1,000 creates ₹5,000 of total deposits]
```

### The Mathematical Formulation:

$$\mathbf{\text{Money Multiplier } (m)} = \frac{\text{Broad Money } (M_3)}{\text{Reserve Money } (M_0)}$$

In macroeconomic theory, the multiplier is derived from the behavioral ratios of the public and banks:
$$m = \frac{1 + c}{c + r}$$
* **$c = \frac{C}{D}$ (Currency-Deposit Ratio)**: The proportion of money the public prefers to hold as physical cash relative to bank deposits. Driven by financial literacy, banking access, and digital payments (UPI).
* **$r = \frac{R}{D}$ (Reserve-Deposit Ratio)**: The proportion of deposits banks hold as reserves ($r = \text{CRR} + \text{Excess Reserves}$).

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Behavioral Shock                        │ Impact on Money Multiplier ($m$) & Money    │
│                                         │ Supply ($M_3$)                              │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Surge in UPI & Digital Banking       │ Public holds less cash $\implies c \downarrow$.   │
│    (Decreases Currency-Deposit Ratio)   │ Multiplier increases ($m \uparrow$). Money  │
│                                         │ creation capacity of banks expands.         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Panic Bank Runs / Cash Hoarding      │ Public withdraws cash $\implies c \uparrow$.       │
│    (Increases Currency-Deposit Ratio)   │ Multiplier collapses ($m \downarrow$). Total│
│                                         │ money supply contracts sharply.             │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. RBI Hikes Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)   │ Mandatory reserve ratio rises $\implies r \uparrow$.│
│    (Increases Reserve-Deposit Ratio)    │ Multiplier decreases ($m \downarrow$). Bank │
│                                         │ lending capacity shrinks (Monetary squeeze).│
└─────────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 4.8 Velocity of Money & The Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher Equation)

Money creates transactions only when it moves from hand to hand. 

### Velocity of Circulation ($V$)
The average number of times a single unit of currency changes hands to finance transactions in a given year:
$$V = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Money Supply}} = \frac{P \times Y}{M}$$

### The Fisher Equation of Exchange (Irving Fisher, 1911):
$$\mathbf{M \times V = P \times Y}$$
* **$M$**: Nominal Money Supply ($M_3$).
* **$V$**: Velocity of Circulation of Money.
* **$P$**: General Price Level (Inflation/Deflator).
* **$Y$**: Real Output (Real GDP).
* **$P \times Y$**: Nominal GDP.

> **Classical Monetarist Proposition (Milton Friedman)**:
> In the short-to-medium run, the velocity of money ($V$) is stable (determined by payment habits and institutions), and real output ($Y$) is anchored at full employment potential. Therefore, any artificial expansion in money supply ($M$) translates directly into an equivalent percentage rise in the price level ($P$):
> > *"Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."*

## 4.10 Examiner Traps & Warning Vault

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 1: The Commercial Bank Vault Cash Trap**
> *Exam Trap*: "Cash held in the cash registers and vaults of State Bank of India is counted as part of India's Money Supply ($M_1$ or $M_3$)."
> *Correction*: **ABSOLUTELY FALSE.** Bank vault cash is held by the CREATORS/SUPPLIERS of money. Money supply measures only money held by the PUBLIC (users of money). Including vault cash would double-count money, because bank deposits already represent claims against those reserves.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 2: Cheques and Demand Drafts as Legal Tender**
> *Exam Trap*: "Cheques and Demand Drafts are unlimited legal tender in India."
> *Correction*: **FALSE.** Cheques, demand drafts, and credit cards are **Fiduciary Money**, NOT Legal Tender. A payee can legally refuse to accept a cheque or credit card. Only currency notes and coins are Legal Tender.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 3: Inter-Bank Deposits in $M_1$ / $M_3$**
> *Exam Trap*: "Deposits placed by Punjab National Bank into HDFC Bank's money market account are included in $M_3$."
> *Correction*: **FALSE.** Inter-bank deposits are strictly **excluded** from all money supply measures. Only deposits of the **non-bank public** held with banks are included.

## 4.11 The 60-Second Memory Skeleton (Rapid Recall)

* **Functions**: Primary (Medium of exchange, Unit of account), Secondary (Deferred payment, Store of value).
* **Fiat vs Fiduciary**: Fiat = sovereign decree / no intrinsic value; Fiduciary = based on trust (cheques → NOT legal tender).
* **Legal Tender**:
  * Banknotes = **Unlimited** legal tender (RBI Act §26).
  * Coins = **Limited** legal tender (Coinage Act 2011: ≥ ₹1 coins up to ₹1,000; 50p coins up to ₹10).
* **Issuance Architecture**:
  * ₹1 note + coins = Minted by **Govt of India** (Finance Secretary signs ₹1 note; Govt liability).
  * ₹2 to ₹500 notes = Issued by **RBI** (RBI Governor signs; RBI liability; RBI Act §22).
  * Backing = **Minimum Reserve System** (₹200 Cr minimum: ≥ ₹115 Cr gold, remainder foreign securities; RBI Act §33).
* **Money Supply Rule**: Money of the **PUBLIC only**! Excludes RBI cash, Govt cash, Bank vault cash, and Inter-bank deposits.
* **Aggregates**:
  * $\mathbf{M_0} = \text{Currency in Circulation} + \text{Bankers' Deposits with RBI} + \text{'Other' Deposits with RBI}$
  * $\mathbf{M_1} = \text{Currency with Public} + \text{Demand Deposits} + \text{'Other' Deposits}$ (Most Liquid)
  * $\mathbf{M_2} = M_1 + \text{Post Office Savings Deposits}$
  * $\mathbf{M_3} = M_1 + \text{Time Deposits}$ (**Broad Money / Headline Metric**)
  * $\mathbf{M_4} = M_3 + \text{Total Post Office Deposits (excl. NSC)}$ (Least Liquid)
* **Multiplier**: $m = \frac{M_3}{M_0} = \frac{1+c}{c+r}$. More digital transactions (UPI) $\implies c \downarrow \implies m \uparrow$.
* **Fisher Identity**: $M \cdot V = P \cdot Y$ (Money $\times$ Velocity = Price $\times$ Real Output = Nominal GDP).

## 4.12 Active Recall Diagnostic Cards

#### Card 1 (Immediate vs. Dynamic Impact on Money Supply)
**Question**: An individual visits an ATM and withdraws ₹50,000 in physical currency notes from their bank savings account (demand deposit). Analyze both the immediate effect on India's $M_1$ and $M_3$ money supply, and the secondary dynamic effect on the banking system's total credit creation capacity.
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
1. <strong>Immediate Effect on $M_1$ and $M_3$</strong>:
   - Inside the formula $M_1 = \text{Currency with Public} + \text{Demand Deposits} + \text{'Other' Deposits}$, Demand Deposits decrease by ₹50,000, while Currency with Public increases by exactly ₹50,000.
   - <em>Net Immediate Result</em>: <strong>Zero change in $M_1$ and zero change in $M_3$</strong>.<br/>
2. <strong>Secondary Dynamic Effect</strong>:
   - When ₹50,000 of cash leaves the commercial banking system, the bank's actual cash reserves ($R$) contract.
   - Under fractional reserve banking, each rupee of reserves supports multiple rupees of secondary bank credit ($Credit = \frac{1}{\text{LRR}} \times \text{Reserves}$).
   - Furthermore, the withdrawal increases the public's currency-deposit ratio ($c = \frac{C}{D}$). In the money multiplier equation ($m = \frac{1+c}{c+r}$), a rise in $c$ causes the multiplier $m$ to decrease.
   - <em>Net Dynamic Result</em>: Over time, the banking system's lending capacity is curtailed, causing <strong>total broad money creation to contract</strong>.
</details>

#### Card 2 (Statutory Currency Architecture)
**Question**: Explain why a ₹1 note in India carries the signature of the Union Finance Secretary, whereas a ₹500 banknote carries the signature of the Governor of the Reserve Bank of India. Under which distinct balance sheet entries do coins and banknotes sit?
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
1. <strong>Statutory Division</strong>:
   - Under Section 22 of the RBI Act, 1934, the RBI has the sole monopoly to issue "banknotes" of denominations ₹2 and above. These notes represent the legal liability of the RBI, backed by assets under Section 33, and are therefore signed by the <strong>RBI Governor</strong>.
   - The ₹1 note and all coins are historical sovereign prerogatives minted and printed directly by the <strong>Government of India</strong> (Ministry of Finance). The ₹1 note serves as the foundational base unit of the national currency system, bearing the signature of the <strong>Finance Secretary</strong> as the administrative head of the Ministry of Finance.<br/>
2. <strong>Balance Sheet Treatment</strong>:
   - <em>Coins and ₹1 Notes</em>: Represent the direct liability of the <strong>Central Government</strong>. When RBI receives them from the Government for circulation, RBI buys them, and they are recorded under the <strong>Asset side of the RBI's Balance Sheet</strong>.
   - <em>Banknotes (₹2 to ₹500)</em>: Represent the direct liability of the <strong>RBI</strong>, and are recorded under the <strong>Liabilities side of the Issue Department of the RBI</strong>.
</details>

#### Card 3 (Monetary Multiplier Dynamics)
**Question**: In 2026, the adoption of Unified Payments Interface (UPI) and Central Bank Digital Currency (e-Rupee) expands significantly in semi-urban and rural India. Explain the mathematical transmission mechanism through which this behavioral shift impacts the Currency-Deposit ratio ($c$), the Money Multiplier ($m$), and the broad money supply ($M_3$), assuming the RBI keeps the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) constant.
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
1. <em>Behavioral Shift</em>: Widespread adoption of digital instant payments reduces the need for citizens and shopkeepers to hold physical currency notes for daily transactional liquidity. Households deposit idle cash into commercial bank accounts.
2. <em>Impact on Currency-Deposit Ratio ($c$)</em>: Because currency with the public ($C$) falls relative to bank deposits ($D$), the ratio $c = \frac{C}{D}$ <strong>declines</strong>.
3. <em>Impact on the Money Multiplier ($m$)</em>: The money multiplier formula is:
$$m = \frac{1 + c}{c + r}$$
Mathematically, because $r < 1$ (e.g. $r \approx 0.045$), taking the partial derivative with respect to $c$ yields $\frac{\partial m}{\partial c} < 0$. Therefore, as $c$ decreases, the <strong>money multiplier ($m$) unambiguously increases</strong>.
4. <em>Impact on Broad Money ($M_3$)</em>: With a higher multiplier, each unit of high-powered reserve money ($M_0$) created by the RBI supports a larger volume of bank credit and broad money ($M_3 = m \times M_0$). Even with constant CRR, the overall <strong>money supply expands more efficiently</strong>.
