## 24.1 Conceptual Typology of Urban Settlements in India

The Census of India defines an urban area through strict statutory and demographic criteria, establishing an institutional dichotomy between **Statutory Towns** and **Census Towns**:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   CENSUS OF INDIA: CLASSIFICATION OF URBAN SETTLEMENTS                           │
├────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Urban Category     │ Canonical Definition & Mandatory Statutory Criteria                         │
├────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ **1. Statutory     │ All administrative places governed by a statutory urban local body,        │
│    Towns (ST)**    │ such as a Municipal Corporation (Nagar Nigam), Municipality (Nagar Palika), │
│                    │ Cantonment Board, or Notified Town Area Committee (NTAC).                   │
│                    │ (*Census 2011 count: 4,041 towns*).                                         │
├────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ **2. Census        │ Places that are administratively rural (governed by Gram Panchayats) but    │
│    Towns (CT)**    │ simultaneously satisfy **all three** demographic conditions:                │
│                    │ 1. Minimum population of **5,000 persons**;                                 │
│                    │ 2. At least **75% of the male main working population** engaged in         │
│                    │    non-agricultural pursuits;                                               │
│                    │ 3. Population density of at least **400 persons per sq. km** (1,000/sq. mi).│
│                    │ (*Census 2011 count: 3,894 towns — a 186% surge from 1,362 in 2001!*).      │
├────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ **3. Outgrowths    │ A viable unit such as a village or part of a village contiguous to a town   │
│    (OG)**          │ possessing urban characteristics (railway colony, university campus, port). │
│                    │ (*Census 2011 count: 981 outgrowths*).                                      │
├────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ **4. Urban         │ A continuous urban spread consisting of a town and its adjoining urban      │
│    Agglomeration   │ outgrowths, or two or more physically contiguous towns.                     │
│    (UA)**          │ *Mandatory Rule*: Must consist of at least one statutory town and have a    │
│                    │ total population of not less than **20,000 persons**.                       │
└────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Census City Classification by Population Size
- **Class I Cities**: Population ≥ 100,000 (1 Lakh+).
- **Class II Towns**: Population between 50,000 and 99,999.
- **Class III Towns**: Population between 20,000 and 49,999.
- **Class IV Towns**: Population between 10,000 and 19,999.
- **Class V Towns**: Population between 5,000 and 9,999.
- **Class VI Towns**: Population $< 5,000$.
- **Million-Plus Cities / UAs**: Population ≥ 1,000,000 (10 Lakh+). (*53 cities in Census 2011, up from 35 in 2001*).
- **Mega Cities**: Population ≥ 10,000,000 (1 Crore+). (*Greater Mumbai, Delhi, and Kolkata*).

### Components of Urban Growth (2001–2011)
The 91 million increase in India's urban population was driven by three primary components:
1. **Natural Increase** (Births minus Deaths): Accounted for **~44%** of urban growth.
2. **Reclassification of Rural Areas into Census Towns**: Accounted for **~30%** of growth.
3. **Net Rural-to-Urban Migration**: Accounted for **~22%** of growth.
4. **Jurisdictional Boundary Expansions**: Accounted for **~4%** of growth.

## 24.3 Spatial Morphology: Top-Heavy Primacy & Slum Dynamics

India's urbanization exhibits a distinct spatial structural pathology known as **Top-Heavy Urbanization** or **Macrocephaly**:
- More than **70% of the total urban population** resides in Class-I cities (100,000+ population).
- The 53 million-plus cities account for **42.6% of the total urban population**.
- Secondary and tertiary towns (Class III to Class VI) suffer from chronic fiscal starvation, infrastructure deficit, and stagnant employment generation, forcing migrants to bypass intermediate centers and crowd directly into Tier-1 megacities.

### 2. E. G. Ravenstein's Laws of Migration (1885)
1. **Distance Decay**: Most migrants move only a short distance; long-distance migrants head preferentially to great centers of commerce and industry.
2. **Step Migration**: Expansion proceeds step-by-step from rural hamlets to market towns, then to secondary cities, and finally to metropolises.
3. **Counter-Streams**: Every major migration stream produces a compensating counter-stream (return migration).
4. **Gender Differentials**: Females are more migratory than males over short distances (dominated by marriage), but males predominate over longer inter-state distances for economic employment.
5. **Technology & Economic Gravity**: Development of transport infrastructure and industrialization accelerates migration volumes.

### 3. The Harris-Todaro Model of Rural-Urban Migration (1970)
John Harris and Michael Todaro formulated an equilibrium model explaining why rural-urban migration continues to surge in developing nations despite massive urban unemployment:
- **The Core Axiom**: Migration is an economic decision governed by **expected wages**, not actual current wages.
- **The Expected Wage Formulation**:
  $$E(W_u) = W_u \times P_e$$
  Where $W_u$ is the urban statutory/formal wage, and $P_e$ is the probability of securing an urban formal job (inversely related to the urban unemployment rate):
  $$P_e = \frac{E_u}{L_u}$$
  *(Ratio of urban employment $E_u$ to total urban labor force $L_u$).*
- **Policy Implication**: Creating jobs in urban areas without raising rural productivity simply induces more rural migrants to flood into the city, paradoxically **increasing urban unemployment** (The Todaro Paradox).

## 24.5 Internal Migration Profile of India: Empirical Evidence

According to the **2011 Census**, there were **455.7 million internal migrants** in India, representing **37.6% of the country's total population** (up from 314.5 million / 31.5% in 2001).

### Major Inter-State Migration Corridors
Research in the *Economic Survey 2016–17* utilizing railway unreserved passenger travel data (Cohort-based Migration Metric - CMM) revealed annual interstate migration flows of **~9 million people**, nearly double the estimates of Census 2001:
- **Major Out-Migrant Source States**: Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, West Bengal.
- **Major In-Migrant Destination States**: Maharashtra, National Capital Territory of Delhi, Gujarat, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab.
