## 13.1 Conceptual Foundations & Velocity of Inflation

Inflation is defined as a **persistent and generalized increase in the overall price level of goods and services in an economy over a sustained period**, resulting in a continuous erosion of the purchasing power of money.

$$\text{Purchasing Power of Money} = \frac{1}{\text{Price Level } (P)}$$

> **The Precision Filter**: A price spike in a single commodity (e.g., tomatoes following a seasonal flood) is **not** inflation; it is a relative price shock. Inflation requires price escalation across a wide, representative basket over successive accounting intervals.

## 13.2 Theories & Causes of Inflation

### 1. Demand-Pull Inflation
Originates when Aggregate Demand ($AD$) exceeds Aggregate Supply ($AS$) at or near the economy's full-employment potential capacity:

$$\mathbf{AD > AS} \implies \text{Prices Pulled Upward}$$

* **Key Drivers**:
  * Loose, expansionary monetary policy (excessive money supply growth $\Delta M > \Delta Y$).
  * Fiscal profligacy (unbridled deficit financing, direct cash stimulus).
  * Rapid export expansion or sudden surge in domestic private consumption.

```
Price Level
     ▲
     │             AS (Aggregate Supply)
     │            /
  P₂ ┼───────────/────╭─────────── AD₂
     │          /     │
  P₁ ┼─────────/──────┼─── AD₁
     │        /       │
     └───────┴────────┴────────► Real Output (Y)
            Y₁       Y* (Full Capacity)
```

---

### 2. Cost-Push Inflation
Originates from an exogenous reduction in Aggregate Supply caused by an increase in input costs, shifting the supply curve inward (leftward) while aggregate demand remains constant:

$$\text{Input Costs } \uparrow \implies \text{Supply Curve Shifts Left} \implies \text{Prices Rise and Output Contracts}$$

* **Key Drivers**:
  * Global commodity price shocks (e.g., crude oil spikes, fertilizer price jumps).
  * Wage-push pressure (labor unions demanding wages above productivity gains).
  * Currency depreciation (importing raw materials at significantly higher rupee costs).
  * Natural disasters, droughts, and geopolitical blockades.

---

### 3. Structural Inflation (The Latin American / Indian Structuralist School)
Pioneered by Gunnar Myrdal, Raúl Prebisch, and Indian structural economists:
* Argues that inflation in developing economies is not merely a monetary phenomenon, but is driven by **deep-seated institutional bottlenecks and structural rigidities**:
  1. **Agricultural Supply Inelasticity**: Inelastic farm output and storage deficits cause severe food inflation whenever income rises.
  2. **Infrastructure Deficits**: Transport bottlenecks, high logistics costs (railway freight cross-subsidization), and power shortages.
  3. **Foreign Exchange Bottlenecks**: Inelastic import needs (oil, electronics, defense hardware) force currency depreciation, transmitting imported price shocks.

## 13.4 The Phillips Curve: Short-Run vs. Long-Run

Originated by A.W. Phillips (1958) and expanded by Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow:

### 1. The Short-Run Phillips Curve (SRPC):
* Hypothesizes a stable, **inverse relationship between the rate of inflation and the rate of unemployment**:

$$\mathbf{\text{Unemployment } \downarrow} \implies \mathbf{\text{Labor Market Tightens}} \implies \mathbf{\text{Wages } \uparrow} \implies \mathbf{\text{Inflation } \uparrow}$$

```
Inflation Rate (π)
       ▲
       │        SRPC
       │        \
    π₁ ┼─────────\
       │          \
    π₂ ┼───────────\
       │            \
       └────────┴────┴────────► Unemployment Rate (u)
               u₁    u₂
```

---

### 2. The Long-Run Breakdown: Friedman-Phelps & NAIRU
During the 1970s OPEC oil shocks, economies experienced simultaneously rising inflation and surging unemployment (**Stagflation**), completely demolishing the stable SRPC.
* **Milton Friedman & Edmund Phelps** (Adaptive Expectations):
  * Workers adjust their inflation expectations ($\pi^e$). Once higher inflation is anticipated, workers demand higher nominal wages, restoring unemployment back to its baseline level.
  * In the Long Run, the Phillips Curve is a **vertical straight line** at the **Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)** or Natural Rate of Unemployment ($u^*$):

$$\text{Long-Run Phillips Curve (LRPC) is Vertical at } \mathbf{u^*}$$

```
Inflation Rate (π)
       ▲              LRPC (Vertical at NAIRU)
       │                 │
    π₃ ┼─────────────────┼───────── SRPC (Higher Exp)
       │                 │
    π₂ ┼─────────────────┼───────── SRPC (Medium Exp)
       │                 │
    π₁ ┼─────────────────┼───────── SRPC (Low Exp)
       │                 │
       └─────────────────┴────────► Unemployment Rate (u)
                        u* (NAIRU)
```

> **Takeaway**: Central banks cannot permanently reduce unemployment below NAIRU by inflating the economy; attempts to do so result solely in accelerating inflation without lasting employment gains.

### Why the RBI Switched from WPI to CPI (Combined) in 2014:
Following the recommendations of the **Dr. Urjit Patel Committee (2014)**, the Reserve Bank abandoned WPI and adopted **CPI (Combined)** as its nominal anchor for Flexible Inflation Targeting:
1. **Omission of the Services Sector**: Services account for more than 50% of India's GDP, but WPI captures zero services. WPI completely excluded education, hospital bills, transit, and rents.
2. **Citizen Reality**: Consumers do not buy goods at wholesale mandi rates or factory gates; they buy at local retail shops, paying retail markups and local indirect taxes.
3. **Food Weight**: Food forms a major share of an average Indian citizen's consumption basket (45.86% in CPI vs. 24.38% total food in WPI). An inflation anchor must reflect the cost of living of the vulnerable.

---

### The Labour Bureau Indices
While NSO compiles national headline CPI (Rural, Urban, Combined), the **Labour Bureau** (Ministry of Labour and Employment, Shimla) compiles three specialized segment indices:
1. **CPI-IW (Industrial Workers)**: Base Year updated to **2016 = 100** (previously 2001). Used to calculate **Dearness Allowance (DA)** for Central Government employees and pensioners.
2. **CPI-AL (Agricultural Labourers)**: Base Year **1986–87 = 100**. Used for wage revisions under MGNREGA.
3. **CPI-RL (Rural Labourers)**: Base Year **1986–87 = 100**.

## 13.8 Active Recall Diagnostic Cards

```
CARD 1: Define Core Inflation and state why the RBI focuses on Headline CPI rather than Core CPI for its target.
ANSWER:
1. Core Inflation = Headline Inflation minus volatile Food and Fuel items.
2. Why RBI targets Headline CPI: Food and beverages make up 45.86% of the consumer basket.
Food price inflation severely impacts household inflation expectations and living standards in India.
Ignoring food would disconnect the central bank's policy target from the lived reality of the populace.
```

```
CARD 2: Contrast the Short-Run Phillips Curve with the Long-Run Phillips Curve (Friedman-Phelps thesis).
ANSWER:
1. Short-Run: Predicts an inverse trade-off between inflation and unemployment (lower unemployment
requires accepting higher inflation).
2. Long-Run: Workers and firms adjust expectations (adaptive/rational expectations). Unemployment
inevitably returns to the Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU). The Long-Run Phillips Curve is
a vertical line at NAIRU, meaning monetary expansion generates higher inflation without lowering unemployment.
```

```
CARD 3: Detail the three key structural differences between the Consumer Price Index (Combined) and the Wholesale Price Index (WPI).
ANSWER:
1. Scope: CPI covers both Goods and Services; WPI covers Goods ONLY (zero services).
2. Basket Dominance: CPI is dominated by Food and Beverages (45.86%); WPI is dominated by
Manufactured Products (64.23%).
3. Institutional Origin: CPI is published monthly by NSO (MoSPI, Base 2012); WPI is published
monthly by Office of Economic Adviser, DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce (Base 2011–12).
```
