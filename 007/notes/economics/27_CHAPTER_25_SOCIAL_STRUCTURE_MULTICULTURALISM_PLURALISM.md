## 25.1 Theoretical Paradigms of Multiculturalism

In contemporary political philosophy and sociology, **Multiculturalism** operates on two distinct levels:
1. **Descriptive / Sociological Fact**: Describes the empirical reality of cultural, linguistic, ethnic, and religious diversity within a defined political boundary.
2. **Normative / Political Philosophy**: Refers to institutional policies, constitutional arrangements, and normative principles formulated to ensure the equitable treatment, recognition, and flourishing of diverse cultural groups within a democratic polity.

### Kymlicka's Typology of Group-Differentiated Rights
Will Kymlicka distinguishes between two types of minorities and three classes of special rights:
- **National Minorities**: Distinct cultural groups that were incorporated into a larger state through conquest, colonization, or voluntary federation (e.g., Indigenous tribes, Quebecois in Canada, Kashmiris or Nagas in India). They legitimately claim **Self-Government Rights**.
- **Polyethnic Groups (Immigrant Groups)**: Families who voluntarily left their homelands. They claim **Polyethnic Rights** (e.g., anti-racism policies, religious dress exemptions like turbans for Sikhs in motorcycle helmet laws), not self-governing territories.
- **Internal Restrictions vs External Protections**:
  - *External Protections (Permitted)*: Protecting the minority group from the economic or political dominance of the majority society.
  - *Internal Restrictions (Impermissible in Liberalism)*: A minority group using traditional authority to oppress its own individual members (e.g., denying education to female members).

## 25.3 The Indian Model of Secularism: "Principled Distance" & Constitutional Pluralism

India's constitutional approach to diversity is neither a French strict secularism (*laïcité* — total banishment of religion from the public square) nor an American strict "Wall of Separation" (complete mutual exclusion of state and church).

### Rajeev Bhargava's Model of "Principled Distance"
Indian secularism practices **Principled Distance**:
- The state is not strictly separated from religion, nor does it identify with any particular religion.
- The state maintains an equidistant relationship with all religions, intervening when religious practices violate human dignity or fundamental rights (e.g., abolition of untouchability, banning Triple Talaq, temple entry for Dalits), while protecting and subsidizing minority cultural practices (e.g., state funding of minority educational institutions, Haj subsidies, pilgrim infrastructure).
- It embodies **Sarva Dharma Sambhava** (equal respect for all paths), rather than *Dharma Nirpekshta* (strict indifference to religion).

## 25.4 Demographic Mosaic: Religion, Language & Social Stratification

### 1. Religious Demographics of India (Census 2011)
- **Hindus**: **79.80%** (966.3 Million)
- **Muslims**: **14.23%** (172.2 Million)
- **Christians**: **2.30%** (27.8 Million)
- **Sikhs**: **1.72%** (20.8 Million)
- **Buddhists**: **0.70%** (8.4 Million)
- **Jains**: **0.37%** (4.5 Million)
- **Other Religions & Persuasions**: **0.66%** (7.9 Million)
- **Religion Not Stated**: **0.24%** (2.9 Million)

### 2. Linguistic Demographics & The 8th Schedule
India's linguistic diversity encompasses four major language families:
1. **Indo-Aryan Family**: **78.05%** of speakers (Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Odia, etc.).
2. **Dravidian Family**: **19.64%** of speakers (Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam, etc.).
3. **Austro-Asiatic Family**: **1.11%** of speakers (Santhali, Khasi, Mundari).
4. **Tibeto-Burman Family**: **1.01%** of speakers (Manipuri, Bodo, Ladakhi).

#### The 22 Official Languages of the 8th Schedule
- **Original 14 Languages (1950)**: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu.
- **21st Amendment (1967)**: Added **Sindhi**.
- **71st Amendment (1992)**: Added **Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali** (KMN).
- **92nd Amendment (2003)**: Added **Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali** (BDMS).

#### The 11 Classical Languages of India
Languages recognized by the Government of India as possessing classical status based on strict statutory criteria (high antiquity of early texts/recorded history spanning 1,500–2,000 years, a body of ancient literature considered valuable heritage, and an original literary tradition not borrowed from another speech community):

  1. **Tamil** (Declared 2004 — First language recognized)
  2. **Sanskrit** (Declared 2005)
  3. **Kannada** (Declared 2008)
  4. **Telugu** (Declared 2008)
  5. **Malayalam** (Declared 2013)
  6. **Odia** (Declared 2014)
  7. **Marathi** (Conferred October 2024)
  8. **Pali** (Conferred October 2024)
  9. **Prakrit** (Conferred October 2024)
