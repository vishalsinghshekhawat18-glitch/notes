## 7.1 Historical Evolution of Modern Banking in India

Indian banking developed through three distinct structural phases:

```
[PHASE 1: PRE-INDEPENDENCE TO 1969] ──> Private joint-stock banks; urban & trade bias;
                                         frequent bank runs and failures.
                    │
                    ▼
[PHASE 2: NATIONALIZATION (1969–1991)] ──> Social banking; rapid rural branch expansion;
                                           severe operational inefficiencies & high NPAs.
                    │
                    ▼
[PHASE 3: POST-1991 LIBERALIZATION]    ──> Narasimham Committees; prudential regulation;
                                           private sector entry; Basel III standards.
```

### Key Milestones in Indian Banking History:
1. **The Imperial Bank of India (1921)**: Formed by amalgamating the three Presidency Banks (Bank of Bengal, Bank of Bombay, Bank of Madras). Acted as a quasi-central bank until RBI was established in 1935.
2. **Creation of State Bank of India (1955)**: Based on the recommendations of the **All India Rural Credit Survey Committee (A.D. Gorwala Committee)**, the Imperial Bank was nationalized to create the State Bank of India (SBI) to drive rural agricultural credit.
3. **The 14 Major Banks Nationalization (July 19, 1969)**:
   * Promulgated via the *Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969* by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi.
   * Target: 14 largest commercial banks holding deposits **$\ge$ ₹50 Crore** (e.g. PNB, Bank of Baroda, Canara Bank, Central Bank of India).
   * Objective: Break the monopoly of corporate business houses over bank credit and direct lending into rural agriculture, small-scale industries, and priority sectors (**Social Banking**).
4. **The Second Wave of Nationalization (April 15, 1980)**:
   * 6 additional commercial banks with deposits **$\ge$ ₹200 Crore** were nationalized (including Andhra Bank, Corporation Bank, Oriental Bank of Commerce).
5. **The M. Narasimham Committee Reports (1991 & 1998)**:
   * **Narasimham Committee I (1991)**: Recommended reducing crushing CRR/SLR requirements, phasing out directed credit, ending interest rate controls, establishing prudential norms for asset classification, and licensing **new private sector banks** (ushering in HDFC, ICICI, Axis).
   * **Narasimham Committee II (1998)**: Recommended capital adequacy norms (Basel I/II), legal frameworks for asset recovery (laying the foundation for the SARFAESI Act, 2002), and consolidation of public sector banks into fewer, globally competitive lenders.
6. **P.J. Nayak Committee (2014)**:
   * Examined governance deficits in Public Sector Banks (PSBs). Recommended repealing the Bank Nationalization Acts, establishing a **Bank Investment Company (BIC)** to hold government shares, and creating the **Banks Board Bureau (BBB)**—now revamped as the **Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB)** in 2022—to select whole-time directors and heads of state-owned financial institutions on a meritocratic basis.

### A. Scheduled vs. Non-Scheduled Commercial Banks

To be classified as a **Scheduled Commercial Bank (SCB)** under Section 42(6)(a) of the **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934**, a bank must satisfy two statutory conditions:
1. Its paid-up capital and accumulated reserves must be of an aggregate value of **not less than ₹5 Lakh** (statutory floor established in 1934).
2. It must satisfy the RBI that its affairs are **not being conducted in a manner detrimental to the interests of its depositors**.

#### Privileges and Duties of Scheduled Status:
* **Privilege**: Entitled to borrow overnight liquidity from the RBI under the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (Repo, SDF, MSF) and has direct access to the inter-bank clearinghouse.
* **Duty**: Must maintain statutory Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) with the RBI on a strict fortnightly reporting basis.

---

### B. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)
* **Established**: October 2, 1975 under the *Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976*, based on the recommendations of the **M. Narasimham Working Group**.
* **Mandate**: Provide rural institutional credit to small/marginal farmers, agricultural laborers, and village artisans to eradicate rural reliance on predatory moneylenders.
* **Statutory Shareholding Architecture**:
  $$\text{Central Government: } \mathbf{50\%} \quad \Big| \quad \text{Sponsor Public Sector Bank: } \mathbf{35\%} \quad \Big| \quad \text{State Government: } \mathbf{15\%}$$
* **Priority Sector Target**: **75.0% of their total lending** must go to Priority Sector Lending (PSL).

## 7.4 Capital Adequacy & The Basel Regulatory Framework

Why do banks require capital? A bank's assets (loans to businesses and households) carry default risk, while its liabilities (deposits) are repayable on demand. If loans sour, the bank must absorb those losses using its own **equity capital** so that depositors' money remains 100% safe.

### The Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR)

$$\mathbf{\text{CRAR}} = \frac{\text{Eligible Regulatory Capital (Tier 1 + Tier 2)}}{\text{Total Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)}} \times 100$$

* **Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)**: Not all assets carry equal risk. The RBI assigns statutory risk weights:
  * Cash and Government of India Securities (G-Secs) = **0% Risk Weight** (Risk-free sovereign backing).
  * Prime Home Loans = **35% to 50% Risk Weight**.
  * Commercial Real Estate & Unsecured Personal Loans / Credit Cards = **100% to 150% Risk Weight**.

### Basel III Norms vs. RBI's Stricter Mandate in India

The Reserve Bank of India has historically maintained a more conservative, robust regulatory standard than the international Basel Committee baseline:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┐
│ Capital / Buffer Component              │ Basel III Global Norm   │ RBI Mandate in India    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)             │ 4.5% of RWA             │ **5.5% of RWA**         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Minimum Tier 1 Capital                  │ 6.0% of RWA             │ **7.0% of RWA**         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Minimum Total Capital (CRAR)            │ **8.0% of RWA**         │ **9.0% of RWA**         │
│                                         │                         │ (12.0% for PSBs)        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB)       │ 2.5% of RWA (in CET1)   │ **2.5% of RWA (in CET1)**│
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Total Capital + CCB Requirement         │ **10.5% of RWA**        │ **11.5% of RWA**        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCCB)   │ 0% to 2.5% of RWA       │ 0% to 2.5% of RWA       │
│                                         │                         │ (Activated in booms)    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┘
```

#### Key Basel III Structural Innovation Ratios:
1. **Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB - 2.5%)**: Built up during normal economic expansions. When a bank’s capital dips into the CCB during a downturn, it faces statutory curbs on distributing dividends, share buybacks, and discretionary executive bonuses.
2. **Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCCB - 0 to 2.5%)**: Mandates extra capital during excessive credit booms to lean against systemic over-lending, which can be drawn down when the credit cycle turns recessionary.
3. **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**: Banks must hold an unencumbered stock of **High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA)**—primarily cash and sovereign G-Secs—sufficient to survive an acute **30-day severe liquidity stress scenario** ($LCR = \frac{\text{Total HQLA}}{\text{Total Net Cash Outflows over 30 days}} \ge 100\%$).
4. **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)**: Promotes structural resilience over a 1-year horizon by requiring banks to fund long-term illiquid assets with reliable, stable long-term liabilities ($NSFR = \frac{\text{Available Stable Funding}}{\text{Required Stable Funding}} \ge 100\%$).
5. **Leverage Ratio**: A non-risk-based backstop calculated as $\frac{\text{Tier 1 Capital}}{\text{Total Exposure}} \ge 3.5\%$ (4.0% for D-SIBs) to prevent banks from masking risks through low risk-weight models.

## 7.5 Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs) — "Too Big to Fail"

The failure of a mega-bank threatens the financial stability of the entire real economy. In 2014, the RBI established the **D-SIB Regulatory Framework**, evaluating banks across four criteria:
1. **Size** (Must have assets $> 2.0\%$ of India's GDP).
2. **Interconnectedness** with other financial institutions.
3. **Substitutability** (Lack of alternate providers for critical payment/lending infrastructure).
4. **Complexity** of cross-border operations.

### Designated D-SIBs in India:
The RBI places designated banks into 5 systemic risk buckets, requiring them to hold **Additional Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital surcharges**:

## 7.6 The Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework

Introduced in 2002 and modernized in November 2021, the **Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)** framework is the RBI’s structured early-intervention mechanism to nurse financially fragile banks back to health before they become insolvent.

### The Three Operational Trigger Metrics:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┐
│ Tracked Financial Metric        │ Healthy Benchmark               │ Risk Threshold 1 Trigger        │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Capital Adequacy (CRAR/CET1) │ $\text{CRAR} \ge 9.0\%$, $\text{CET1} \ge 5.5\%$ │ $\text{CRAR} < 9.0\%$ or $\text{CET1} < 5.5\%$  │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Asset Quality (Net NPA Ratio)│ $\text{Net NPA} < 6.0\%$        │ **$\text{Net NPA} \ge 6.0\%$**  │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Leverage Ratio (Tier 1)      │ $\ge 3.5\%$                     │ $< 3.5\%$                       │
└─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┘
```

*(Note: Return on Assets (RoA) was dropped as a standalone trigger metric in the 2021 revision to focus strictly on capital, asset quality, and leverage).*

### Statutory Restrictions Imposed on a PCA Bank:
* **Mandatory Restrictions**:
  1. Complete bar on dividend distribution and remittance of profits to shareholders.
  2. Promoters/Owners mandated to infuse fresh equity capital.
  3. Total restrictions on branch expansion (domestic and overseas).
  4. Cap on executive management compensation and director bonuses.
* **Discretionary Restrictions**:
  1. Ban on lending to high-risk, low-rated corporate sectors.
  2. Aggressive reduction in loan concentration and high-cost wholesale deposits.
  3. Mandatory reduction of operational overhead costs.

## 7.8 Examiner Traps & Warning Vault

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 1: The Payments Bank Lending Fallacy**
> *Exam Trap*: "Payments Banks can disburse micro-loans up to ₹50,000 to rural artisans and issue credit cards."
> *Correction*: **ABSOLUTELY FALSE.** Under RBI guidelines, Payments Banks are **STRICTLY PROHIBITED FROM LENDING** in any form. They cannot disburse loans, and they **CANNOT issue credit cards**. They can only issue ATM/debit cards. Small Finance Banks, by contrast, CAN lend.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 2: Global vs. Indian Basel III Minimum CRAR**
> *Exam Trap*: "Under Basel III norms, the RBI mandates a minimum Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR) of 8.0% for Indian commercial banks."
> *Correction*: **FALSE.** The international BCBS baseline is 8.0%, but the **RBI mandates a stricter minimum of 9.0%** for commercial banks (and 12.0% for Public Sector Banks). With the 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer, the total minimum is **11.5% in India** (vs. 10.5% globally).

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 3: Term Deposits in Payments Banks**
> *Exam Trap*: "An individual can open a 5-year Fixed Deposit of ₹1,00,000 in India Post Payments Bank."
> *Correction*: **FALSE.** Payments Banks can accept **Demand Deposits ONLY** (Savings and Current accounts). They are legally barred from accepting Time (Term/Fixed) Deposits. Furthermore, their demand deposits are capped at **₹2,00,000 per customer**.

## 7.9 The 60-Second Memory Skeleton (Rapid Recall)

* **History**: Imperial Bank (1921) $\rightarrow$ SBI (1955: Gorwala); 14 Banks (1969: $\ge$₹50 Cr); 6 Banks (1980: $\ge$₹200 Cr); Narasimham I (1991: private banks, prudential norms) & II (1998: Basel, recovery).
* **Scheduled Status**: Second Schedule of RBI Act 1934; Capital $\ge$ ₹5 Lakh; Not detrimental to depositors; Gets LAF access.
* **RRBs (1975)**: Center **50%**, Sponsor Bank **35%**, State **15%**. PSL = 75%.
* **Differentiated Banks (Nachiket Mor, 2014)**:
  * **Payments Banks**: Demand deposits only (cap ₹2 Lakh); **NO lending, NO credit cards**; 75% in G-Secs (SLR); Min capital ₹100 Cr (Airtel, IPPB).
  * **Small Finance Banks**: Deposits + **Lending permitted**; PSL = 75%; 50% loans $\le$ ₹25 Lakh; Min capital ₹200 Cr (AU, Equitas).
* **Capital Adequacy (CRAR)**: $\frac{\text{Tier 1} + \text{Tier 2}}{\text{RWA}} \times 100$.
  * Tier 1 = Going-concern (CET1: equity/reserves + AT1: perpetual bonds).
  * Tier 2 = Gone-concern (Subordinated debt/provisions).
  * RBI Mandate: **CET1 = 5.5%**, **Tier 1 = 7.0%**, **CRAR = 9.0%** (Global = 8%), **CCB = 2.5%** $\implies$ **Total = 11.5%**.
* **D-SIBs ("Too Big to Fail")**: Assets > 2% of GDP $\implies$ **SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank** (maintain extra CET1 buffer).
* **PCA Framework (2021)**: Triggers = CRAR (<9%), Net NPA ($\ge 6\%$), Leverage (<3.5%). Bar on dividends, branches, bonuses.

#### Card 2 (Capital Adequacy & Risk-Weighted Mechanics)
**Question**: Commercial Bank Alpha holds ₹1,000 Crore in Government of India dated securities, ₹500 Crore in prime residential housing loans (assigned a 50% risk weight), and ₹400 Crore in unsecured credit card receivables (assigned a 150% risk weight). Calculate Bank Alpha's Total Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA), and calculate the minimum total regulatory capital it must hold under RBI's Basel III guidelines (including the Capital Conservation Buffer).
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
1. <strong>Calculation of Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)</strong>:
   - <em>Government Securities (₹1,000 Cr)</em>: Sovereign risk weight = 0%.
     $$\text{RWA}_{\text{G-Sec}} = ₹1,000 \times 0\% = \mathbf{₹0 \text{ Crore}}$$
   - <em>Residential Mortgages (₹500 Cr)</em>: Risk weight = 50%.
     $$\text{RWA}_{\text{Mortgages}} = ₹500 \times 50\% = \mathbf{₹250 \text{ Crore}}$$
   - <em>Credit Card Receivables (₹400 Cr)</em>: Risk weight = 150%.
     $$\text{RWA}_{\text{Credit Card}} = ₹400 \times 150\% = \mathbf{₹600 \text{ Crore}}$$
   - <strong>Total RWA</strong> = $₹0 + ₹250 + ₹600 = \mathbf{₹850 \text{ Crore}}$.<br/>
2. <strong>Minimum Total Capital Requirement Under RBI Basel III Norms</strong>:
   - The RBI mandates a minimum CRAR of 9.0% plus a Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) of 2.5%, totaling **11.5% of RWA**.
   - $$\text{Required Regulatory Capital} = 11.5\% \times ₹850 \text{ Crore} = \mathbf{₹97.75 \text{ Crore}}$$
   - Of this, at least $8.0\%$ ($5.5\% \text{ CET1} + 2.5\% \text{ CCB}$) must be held in Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital ($8.0\% \times ₹850 = ₹68.0 \text{ Crore}$).
