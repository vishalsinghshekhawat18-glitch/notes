## 1. Rajasthan’s hydrographic architecture

Rajasthan is one of India’s most water-stressed states. Its rivers, streams, reservoirs and lakes reflect the interaction of rainfall variability, relief, geology, soil permeability and human water management. The key geographical contrast is between **rivers that ultimately reach a sea** and **inland drainage streams that terminate in depressions, saline playas, wetlands or sandy tracts**.

### 1.1 Three broad drainage systems

The following are widely used **textbook-level area shares**, not immutable survey measurements. The exact percentages vary by map scale and by whether a source classifies transitional catchments and “outside-basin” areas separately.

| Drainage category | Common approximate share | Ultimate outlet / terminal condition | Major examples |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Inland or endorheic drainage (*अंतःप्रवाही अपवाह*) | About 60% | Ends in desert sands, playas, saline lakes or inland wetlands | Ghaggar, Kantli, Sabi, Ruparel, Sambhar catchment streams, Kakni |
| Bay of Bengal drainage | About 22–23% | Generally reaches the Yamuna–Ganga system and Bay of Bengal | Chambal, Banas, Banganga and Gambhir systems |
| Arabian Sea drainage | About 17–18% | Reaches the Rann of Kutch, Little Rann or Gulf of Khambhat | Luni, Mahi, Sabarmati, West Banas |

**Exam caution:** Do not treat 60.2%, 22.4% and 17.4% as precise hydrological constants without citing a named map or source. Use them as a commonly reproduced Rajasthan-geography convention if the question is clearly based on that scheme.

### 1.2 The drainage divide and the Aravallis

The Aravalli Range forms an important regional drainage divide, trending broadly southwest–northeast. It separates several west-flowing catchments from catchments that drain east or southeast. However, it is **not a single straight wall separating every river in the state**. Local plateaus, saddles, river captures and low-relief tracts create exceptions.

- **West and southwest-facing catchments:** Luni and its tributaries; parts of the Sabarmati and West Banas systems; some streams flow only to inland depressions.
- **East and southeast-facing catchments:** Banas, Chambal tributaries and the Mahi system.
- **Inland basins:** Streams may end in desert sands or terminal saline lakes without reaching either sea.

Some textbooks trace the principal divide from the Delhi Ridge and northeastern Aravalli uplands through the Alwar–Ajmer region and onward across the southern Aravallis. Exact line traces vary by the scale of the map. For revision, focus on **which catchment reaches which outlet**, rather than memorising an overly precise line across individual villages.

### 1.3 The 14-basin classification used in groundwater and catchment planning

Central Ground Water Board material lists 14 broad basin categories for Rajasthan. In that classification, the fourteenth category is a grouped category for **other nallahs/catchments**, rather than one single named river basin. Older basin documents use the district boundaries and terminology current when they were compiled.

| No. | Basin / category | Revision point |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Luni | Largest named basin in Rajasthan by area in commonly used basin tables |
| 2 | Banas | Largest major river basin wholly within Rajasthan in many standard references |
| 3 | Chambal | Part of the Yamuna–Ganga drainage |
| 4 | Mahi | Flows through southern Rajasthan and Gujarat to the Gulf of Khambhat |
| 5 | Banganga | Eastern Rajasthan; associated with the Bharatpur–Keoladeo wetland water system |
| 6 | Sabarmati | Rises in the Aravalli region and flows through Gujarat |
| 7 | West Banas | Flows into the Little Rann of Kutch |
| 8 | Gambhir | Eastern Rajasthan river ultimately connected with the Yamuna |
| 9 | Parbati | Chambal tributary system |
| 10 | Ruparail / Ruparel | Alwar–Bharatpur region |
| 11 | Sabi / Sahibi | Flows from northeastern Rajasthan into Haryana |
| 12 | Shekhawati | Internal drainage catchments |
| 13 | Sukli | Small west-draining catchment in the southwest |
| 14 | Other nallahs/catchments | A grouped category used in basin-based resource tables |

CGWB’s older basin tables report estimated areas such as Luni 69,302 km², Banas 47,060 km², Chambal about 31,243 km², Mahi 16,611 km² and Shekhawati 9,751 km². Treat these as **source- and mapping-period-specific basin estimates**, not as new district areas. In some tables, a large portion of the state is classed as “outside basin” or “other nallahs”; this should not be confused with a separately named river basin. See the source register.

## 2. Arabian Sea drainage system

### 2.1 Luni River (*लूनी*)

**Core identity:** The Luni is the most important river of western Rajasthan. It rises in the Aravalli region near Ajmer, flows generally southwest, and ultimately dissipates into marshes and salt flats associated with the Rann of Kutch.

- **Origin:** Nag Pahar / Nag Hills near Ajmer is the conventional exam answer. Traditional geographical descriptions say the Sagarmati and a stream associated with Pushkar/Saraswati meet near Govindgarh, after which the river is called Luni. Different books simplify this local headwater description differently.
- **Length:** Commonly given as roughly **495 km** overall; estimates for the portion within Rajasthan vary by source and method.
- **General course:** Ajmer region → Nagaur/Pali–Jodhpur region → Balotra/Barmer area → Sanchore region → Rann of Kutch.
- **Outlet:** Inland marshes of the Rann of Kutch, not a year-round open estuary.
- **Character:** Strongly seasonal. Flow depends on monsoon rainfall and upstream runoff; discharge decreases downstream because of evaporation, infiltration and local abstractions.

#### Why does the Luni become increasingly saline downstream?

The popular description is that the river is “sweet in the upper course and saline downstream, especially after Balotra.” This is a useful broad association, but the precise boundary shifts with season and flow conditions.

The main processes are:
1. **High evaporation** in an arid climate concentrates dissolved salts.
2. **Saline and evaporitic soils/sediments** contribute dissolved ions to runoff and shallow groundwater.
3. **Low and intermittent discharge** provides less dilution in dry periods.
4. Local human water use and wastewater inputs can affect water quality, but they should not be treated as the sole cause of the basin’s natural salinity.

Do not state as established geology that all of the river’s salt comes from a direct “Tethys Sea deposit” or from a single capillary-action mechanism.

#### Luni tributaries

| Tributary | High-yield association | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Jojari (*जोजड़ी*) | A major tributary associated with the Jodhpur region; often listed as a right-bank tributary | The claim that it is the only non-Aravalli tributary is a textbook convention that should be checked against the particular basin map |
| Bandi (*बांडी*) | Pali region; Hemawas reservoir association | Industrial pollution has been a major concern in the Pali textile cluster; avoid treating every reach as uniformly polluted |
| Guhiya (*गुहिया*) | Pali hills / local western catchment | Course and joining point are shown differently in local maps |
| Sukri (*सुकड़ी*) | Western tributary; Bankli Dam association | Do not confuse with Sukli, a separately named basin/stream |
| Jawai (*जवाई*) | Important Luni tributary; Jawai Dam near Sumerpur, Pali | Jawai Dam is important for water storage in western Rajasthan; do not claim it alone supplies all drinking water to Jodhpur |
| Khari and Sagi | Additional west-Rajasthan tributaries | Small channels may be seasonal and local maps can vary in naming |

**Jawai Dam:** Built on the Jawai River in the Pali region, it is an important water-storage and irrigation structure. Inter-basin diversion through the Sei system is associated with augmenting storage. Exact current supply allocations, storage ranking and transfer-tunnel statistics should be used only when backed by a current Water Resources Department document.

### 2.2 Mahi River (*माही*)

The Mahi is a major west-flowing river of southern Rajasthan. It originates in Madhya Pradesh and flows through southern Rajasthan before entering Gujarat and discharging into the **Gulf of Khambhat**.

- **Origin:** Amjhoru / the Mehad Lake vicinity in the Dhar region of Madhya Pradesh is a commonly used description; exact source points differ across references.
- **Rajasthan entry:** Banswara region; Khandu village is used in some Rajasthan exam accounts.
- **Route:** Banswara and adjoining southern Rajasthan catchments → Gujarat → Gulf of Khambhat.
- **Length:** Often cited at about **576 km** overall, with approximately **171 km in Rajasthan** in common state-geography references. Lengths differ across sources.
- **Tropic of Cancer:** The Mahi crosses the Tropic of Cancer twice along its course. This is a reliable Indian-geography association. Do **not** assert that it is the only river in the entire world to do so.
- **Regional names:** “Vagad ki Ganga” / “Ganga of the tribals” are popular cultural epithets, not formal hydrological classifications.

#### Mahi Basin projects

| Structure | Location / river | Key role |
|---|---|---|
| Mahi Bajaj Sagar Dam | Mahi River, Banswara region | Major multipurpose reservoir for irrigation and hydropower |
| Kagdi Pick-up Dam | Downstream of Mahi Bajaj Sagar system | Regulates water for feeder canals |
| Kadana Dam | Gujarat, on the Mahi | Important downstream/interstate structure; it is not in Rajasthan |

The Central Electricity Authority lists **Mahi Bajaj Sagar Stage I and II at 140 MW installed capacity in total** in its hydropower profile. The capacity is a better-grounded exam figure than unsourced claims about exact annual generation. [CEA, State Profile on Hydro Development for Rajasthan](https://cea.nic.in/wp-content/uploads/hepr/2022/08/State_Power_8.pdf)

#### Mahi tributaries and Baneshwar

- **Som:** A major tributary of the southern Rajasthan Mahi system; associated with the Som–Kamla–Amba irrigation project.
- **Jakham:** Rises in the Pratapgarh region and is associated with Jakham Dam.
- **Anas:** Enters Rajasthan from Madhya Pradesh and joins the Mahi system.
- **Other streams:** Erav/Iru, Chap and Moran appear in local basin lists; details should be checked against the basin map before memorising a precise sequence.

**Baneshwar Dham:** The sacred confluence is traditionally described as the meeting of **Mahi, Som and Jakham** in the Dungarpur region. It is known as the *Kumbh of the Tribals* and is associated with the Magh Purnima fair. This is an important cultural-geography association.

### 2.3 Sabarmati River (*साबरमती*)

- **Origin:** Aravalli hills in the Udaipur region; specific named hills differ between references.
- **Course:** Rajasthan → Gujarat → Gulf of Khambhat.
- **Length:** Often cited at about 416 km overall; the length within Rajasthan is short relative to the Gujarat course and is reported differently in summaries.
- **Major Rajasthan tributaries:** Wakal and Sei are important local names. The Sei is also associated with the Jawai augmentation/diversion scheme.
- **Exam distinction:** Sabarmati is a west-flowing river, despite rising in Rajasthan. Its main urban and economic association is with Gujarat, especially Ahmedabad.

### 2.4 West Banas (*पश्चिमी बनास*)

- **Origin:** Sirohi / western Mount Abu region; Naya Sanwara is a commonly cited origin point.
- **Course:** Sirohi region → Gujarat → Little Rann of Kutch.
- **Outlet:** Little Rann, not the Gulf of Khambhat directly.
- **Tributary association:** Sukli/Sipu is commonly listed in regional summaries. Do not confuse this with Luni’s Sukri.

## 3. Bay of Bengal drainage system

### 3.1 Chambal River (*चम्बल; चर्मण्वती*)

The Chambal is one of Rajasthan’s most important perennial rivers and the state’s largest river system in terms of major surface-water storage and flow among its principal rivers.

- **Origin:** Janapav Hills near Mhow, in Madhya Pradesh.
- **Course:** Madhya Pradesh → southeastern Rajasthan → along part of the Rajasthan–Madhya Pradesh border → Uttar Pradesh.
- **Confluence:** Yamuna River in Uttar Pradesh; the Yamuna subsequently joins the Ganga.
- **Length:** About **965 km** is the traditional exam figure. Other modern measurements differ; use the figure specified by the question or prescribed source.
- **Rajasthan significance:** Hydropower, irrigation, ravine landscapes and the Chambal River ecosystem.
- **Geomorphology:** The river has incised gorges and, in parts of its wider basin, extensive gullies and badlands (*बीहड़ / उत्खात भूमि*).

#### Chambal Valley Project

The multipurpose Chambal project is a joint project of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. The four major structures in the usual project sequence are Gandhi Sagar, Kota Barrage, Rana Pratap Sagar and Jawahar Sagar.

| Structure | Location | Installed hydropower capacity | Main role |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Gandhi Sagar Dam | Madhya Pradesh | Commonly listed at about 115 MW | Upper storage reservoir and hydropower |
| Kota Barrage | Kota, Rajasthan | **0 MW** | Irrigation diversion; feeds the canal system |
| Rana Pratap Sagar Dam | Rawatbhata region, Rajasthan | 172 MW | Storage, hydropower and downstream regulation |
| Jawahar Sagar Dam | Kota–Bundi region, Rajasthan | 99 MW | Hydropower and flow regulation |

The commonly cited installed total is about **386 MW** using the 115 + 172 + 99 MW figures. The three hydro stations’ generation is shared between the two states under the project arrangements; the exact allocation is governed by project agreements. Avoid inferring entitlement merely from the location of a station. The Kota District disaster-management plan summarises the project and reports the irrigation area served by its canals. [Kota District Disaster Management Plan: Chambal Valley Project](https://dmrelief.rajasthan.gov.in/content/dam/dmr/files/documents/ddmp2025/Kota.pdf)

#### Major Chambal tributaries

| Tributary | Important association |
|---|---|
| Banas | Major tributary joining Chambal near Rameshwaram |
| Kali Sindh | Rises in Madhya Pradesh; joins Chambal in Rajasthan |
| Parbati | Crosses into Rajasthan from Madhya Pradesh and joins the Chambal system |
| Ahu | Joins Kali Sindh near Gagron |
| Parwan | Important southeastern tributary |
| Mej | Drains the Bundi region |
| Brahmani/Bamani | Joins Chambal in the Bhainsrorgarh region; associated with Chulia Falls |

**Gagron Fort:** Situated near the confluence of the Kali Sindh and Ahu rivers, making it a classic example of a water-protected or *jal durg* setting.

**Chulia Falls:** A waterfall on the Chambal in the Bhainsrorgarh region. The exact height varies by source; use “about 18 m” only when following the relevant Rajasthan exam reference.

**Dang and ravines:** Karauli and Dholpur are associated with the Dang landscape; slogans such as “Dang ki Rani” and “Dang ka Raja” are popular regional epithets rather than formal geographic designations.

### 3.2 Banas River (*बनास; वन की आशा*)

The Banas is commonly described as the **longest major river flowing entirely within Rajasthan**.

- **Origin:** Khamnor Hills, in the Aravalli region of Rajsamand district.
- **Course:** Through the Mewar and eastern plains, then joins the Chambal at Rameshwaram near Khandar in Sawai Madhopur district.
- **Length:** Common exam references use about **480 km**; other surveys quote a longer figure. State the source when giving an exact length.
- **Drainage:** The Banas system is part of the Bay of Bengal drainage through the Chambal–Yamuna system.

#### Three confluences commonly taught in Rajasthan geography

| Place | Rivers named in the standard exam convention |
|---|---|
| Bigod (Bhilwara region) | Banas + Bedach/Berach + Menal |
| Rajmahal (Tonk region) | Banas + Khari + Dai |
| Rameshwaram (Sawai Madhopur region) | Banas + Chambal; Seep is included in some local accounts of the broader confluence area |

The Rameshwaram entry is best memorised as **Banas–Chambal confluence**. The exact inclusion of Seep in a “Triveni” list varies across references.

#### Major Banas dams and reservoirs

- **Bisalpur Dam:** On the Banas near Bisalpur village in Tonk district; major drinking-water source for Jaipur and several towns in the wider region. It is also important for irrigation.
- **Isarda Dam:** Downstream on the Banas system, planned/built for storage and drinking-water supply to eastern Rajasthan.
- **Matrikundiya Dam:** Associated with the Banas in the Chittorgarh region.
- **Nand Samand:** A local reservoir important for Rajsamand region.

#### Banas tributaries

| Tributary | High-yield point |
|---|---|
| Bedach/Berach | Also called Ayad in its upper course; associated with Udaipur and Udaisagar |
| Kothari | Rises in the Aravalli region and joins the Banas; Meja Dam is on this system |
| Khari | Joins Banas near Rajmahal in the common exam account |
| Dai | Tributary associated with the Rajmahal confluence |
| Morel | Eastern Rajasthan tributary; Morel Dam is in its basin |
| Mashi | Tributary in the Ajmer–Tonk area |

**Ayad–Bedach naming:** Regional sources commonly describe the stream through Udaipur as Ayad and its downstream continuation as Bedach after Udaisagar Lake. Treat the exact naming transition as a local cartographic convention rather than a universal rule applied by every map.

### 3.3 Banganga River (*बाणगंगा*)

- **Origin:** Bairath/Viratnagar hills in the Kotputli–Behror region.
- **Course:** Through the Jaipur–Dausa–Bharatpur region.
- **Hydrological character:** Seasonal, with water captured, diverted or stored in reservoirs and wetland systems along its eastern course.
- **Key structures / wetlands:** Jamwa Ramgarh Dam is historically associated with the Jaipur region; Banganga water has also been associated with the Ajan Bund and Keoladeo wetland system near Bharatpur.

Some summaries say the Banganga no longer reaches its former downstream channel or the Yamuna directly. The term **“Rundhit Sarita” (severed river)** is not a standard universal hydrological class and should not be presented as a formal scientific definition. Government sources describe Banganga and Gambhir as part of the hydrological system feeding the Keoladeo/Bharatpur region. Actual continuity is seasonal and modified by water diversions.

### 3.4 Gambhir and Gambhiri: do not confuse them

- **Gambhir (*गम्भीर*):** Eastern Rajasthan river associated with the Karauli region, Panchana Dam, Bharatpur wetland area and ultimately the Yamuna system.
- **Gambhiri (*गम्भीरी*):** A different river that rises in the Madhya Pradesh region and joins the Bedach/Berach near Chittorgarh.

This spelling difference is a high-yield identification trap.

**Panchana Dam:** Located in Karauli district on the Gambhir system. The five small streams commonly listed in Rajasthan exam material are **Ata, Machi, Bhadrawati, Barkheda and Bhainsawat**. The “largest earthen dam in Rajasthan” description appears in popular state-geography sources; do not use an unqualified national or engineering superlative without an up-to-date technical source.

**Keoladeo connection:** Rajasthan Forest Department describes Keoladeo National Park as lying at the confluence of the Gambhir and Banganga systems, both linked to the Yamuna drainage. The park’s current water regime depends on managed water supply and seasonal availability, not just unaltered natural river flow. [Rajasthan Forest Department: Keoladeo National Park](https://forest.rajasthan.gov.in/content/raj/forest/hi/forest-department/kelodev-national-park.html)

## 4. Inland drainage system (*अंतःप्रवाही अपवाह*)

A substantial part of western and northeastern Rajasthan lies within endorheic basins. Such streams may flow only after rainfall and often terminate in sand, saline depressions or inland wetlands. “Inland drainage” describes the outlet pattern; it does **not** mean that every stream is permanently dry or that all inland channels have one single origin.

### 4.1 Ghaggar River (*घग्घर; हकरा downstream*)

- **Source region:** Shivalik foothills in the Himachal Pradesh region.
- **Course:** Through Haryana into northern Rajasthan, especially Hanumangarh and Sri Ganganagar; the channel can continue farther under flood conditions.
- **Character:** Seasonal / ephemeral in Rajasthan, with highly variable discharge.
- **Nali:** A local name used for parts of the fertile Ghaggar floodplain/channel in northern Rajasthan.
- **Hakra:** Name commonly used for a downstream paleochannel/channel in present-day Pakistan.
- **Archaeology:** Kalibangan and other settlements are associated with the Ghaggar–Hakra palaeochannel landscape.

#### Ghaggar and the Saraswati question

Satellite imagery and geological studies identify palaeochannels across northwestern India, and archaeological sites occur along several of these old drainage lines. ISRO-related studies have mapped palaeochannels and discussed possible links with the ancient Saraswati described in texts. However, the **identification of the present Ghaggar as one unchanged, fully established Vedic Saraswati is disputed**. Some sedimentological studies indicate that parts of the major palaeochannel were abandoned before the mature Harappan period.

Exam-safe wording: **“The Ghaggar–Hakra system is associated with extensive palaeochannels; its relationship to the ancient Saraswati remains a subject of research and debate.”**

Do not describe Kalibangan, Pilibangan and Rangmahal together as a single Indus Valley Civilization group. Kalibangan is a major Harappan site; other sites have their own chronological and cultural contexts.

### 4.2 Kantli River (*कांतली*)

- **Origin:** Khandela hills in the Sikar region.
- **Course:** Through the Sikar–Neem Ka Thana–Jhunjhunu region, where it dissipates in inland sand/dune tracts.
- **Length:** Commonly quoted at about **100 km**, but estimates can vary.
- **Cultural region:** Associated with Torawati.
- **Archaeological association:** Ganeshwar, associated with early copper-working, and Sunari, known for ancient iron-smelting evidence, are important sites within the wider region.

Kantli is often presented in Rajasthan exam sources as the longest river whose course both begins and ends entirely inside the state’s inland drainage. Treat “longest” as the prescribed textbook convention, not an absolute ranking of every seasonal stream.

### 4.3 Sabi / Sahibi (*साबी / साहिबी*)

- **Origin:** Northeastern Aravalli uplands in the Kotputli–Behror region.
- **Course:** Northeastern Rajasthan → Haryana.
- **Terminal system:** Associated with the Najafgarh drain and the wider Yamuna drainage/wetland system.
- **Archaeological association:** Jodhpura is associated with the Sabi/Sahibi landscape and Painted Grey Ware / early iron-working in regional archaeological accounts.

### 4.4 Ruparel / Ruparail (*रूपारेल*)

- **Origin:** Udainath hills, Alwar region.
- **Course:** Through parts of Alwar and Bharatpur/Deeg region.
- **Regional significance:** Historically associated with water supply, reservoirs and disputes over water sharing between former princely states.
- **Moti Jheel and Sujan Ganga:** Local water-management structures in the Bharatpur region are associated with the Ruparel and its linked canal/reservoir system. Avoid stating that a single river directly supplies the entire modern Bharatpur system without qualification.

### 4.5 Sambhar Lake catchment streams

Sambhar is an **endorheic saline lake**. Seasonal streams named in common Rajasthan geography sources include Mendha, Rupangarh, Khari and Khandela. They carry runoff and dissolved minerals into the lake.

- **Mendha:** Often described as an important northern/northeastern inflow.
- **Rupangarh:** Associated with the southern side of the lake and with the Salemabad area.
- **Exam caution:** Claims that one stream invariably contributes the “highest salt concentration” are too specific without a referenced chemical survey.

### 4.6 Kakni / Kakney River (*काकनी*)

- **Region:** Jaisalmer.
- **Course:** A short ephemeral stream associated with Bhuj/Bujh Lake and nearby saline depressions.
- **Length:** Often given as approximately 17 km in exam material.
- **Caution:** “Shortest river in Rajasthan” depends on which named and mapped seasonal channels are included, so it is safer to write “often cited as one of the shortest named streams in Rajasthan.”

## 5. Lakes of Rajasthan

Rajasthan’s lakes can be classified broadly as:
1. **Saline lakes / terminal playas:** Sambhar, Pachpadra, Didwana, Lunkaransar and smaller salt depressions.
2. **Freshwater or reservoir lakes:** Jaisamand, Rajsamand, Pichola, Fateh Sagar, Ana Sagar, Pushkar, Nakki and numerous local tanks.

The division is useful for exam preparation, but salinity and freshwater status may change with season, inflow and water management.

### 5.1 Saline lakes

#### Sambhar Lake (*सांभर झील*)

- **Type:** Large inland saline wetland; India’s best-known inland salt lake.
- **Location:** Extends across parts of the present Jaipur / Ajmer / Didwana-Kuchaman administrative region; exact administrative description depends on the map or boundary reference used.
- **Importance:** Natural brine and salt production; important wintering ground for waterbirds including flamingos.
- **Ramsar status:** Listed as a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance on **23 March 1990**, site no. 464, with a Ramsar-listed area of 24,000 ha. [Ramsar Sites Information Service: Sambhar Lake](https://rsis.ramsar.org/sites/default/files/rsiswp_search/exports/Ramsar-Sites-annotated-summary-India.pdf)
- **Salt-production share:** A figure near 8.7% of national production is repeated in study material, but output fluctuates; do not treat the percentage as a current annual constant without a dated production statistic.
- **Ecological issue:** The lake is sensitive to changes in inflow, brine extraction, land use, pollution and waterbird mortality events. Avoid citing one historic mortality incident as proof that the entire lake is permanently degraded.

#### Pachpadra Lake / Salt Works (*पचपदरा*)

- **Location:** Balotra region (formerly Barmer district).
- **Type:** Saline depression and salt-production landscape.
- **Craft/industry association:** Traditional salt extraction is associated with the Kharwal community and local crystallisation practices.
- **Caution:** “98% NaCl” or “highest-purity salt in India” is a product-quality claim; purity depends on a measured batch and should not be stated as an invariant natural property of the lake.

#### Didwana Lake (*डीडवाना*)

- **Location:** Didwana-Kuchaman district.
- **Importance:** Salt and chemical-salt extraction; brines can be rich in sodium sulphate.
- **Caution:** Do not automatically describe all Didwana salt as “non-edible” without specifying the particular product or processing context. Sodium sulphide and sodium sulphate are different chemical compounds; use the correct substance where documented.

#### Other saline depressions

| Name | Regional association | Revision note |
|---|---|---|
| Lunkaransar | Bikaner region | Small saline lake/depression; extent changes seasonally |
| Phalodi salt depressions | Phalodi region | Local salt-making and saline-groundwater association |
| Kavod / Pokhran playas | Jaisalmer region | Inland salt depressions |
| Rewasa and Kuchaman/Degana depressions | Sikar and Nagaur–Didwana-Kuchaman region | Local playa and saline-groundwater features |

**Formation:** Rajasthan’s saline lakes are best explained through closed-basin drainage, evaporation under an arid climate, saline groundwater/soil and local geological conditions. Avoid presenting “left by the Tethys Sea” as a complete or universally accepted explanation for each modern lake.

### 5.2 Major freshwater lakes and reservoirs

#### Jaisamand / Dhebar Lake (*जयसमंद / ढेबर झील*)

- **Builder:** Maharana Jai Singh of Mewar.
- **Date:** Construction is commonly dated to the late 17th century, especially 1685–1691.
- **River:** Gomati River.
- **Location:** The lake is now within the Salumbar administrative region (older sources refer to Udaipur district).
- **Importance:** A very large artificial freshwater lake and reservoir; Rajasthan Tourism describes it as one of Asia’s largest artificial freshwater lakes.
- **Embankment:** Contains marble cenotaphs and a Shiva shrine; local accounts describe seven islands.
- **Caution:** Ranking it “second largest in India” is not supported consistently; lake rankings vary by surface area, storage and definition.

Sources: [Rajasthan Tourism: Jaisamand Lake](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/jaisamand-lake.html); [Rajasthan Forest Department: Jaisamand Wildlife Sanctuary brochure](https://forest.rajasthan.gov.in/content/dam/raj/forest/rajasthan-wild-life/pdf/Eco-Tourism/Brouchers/Jaisamand%20WL%20Sanctuary.pdf)

#### Rajsamand Lake (*राजसमंद झील*)

- **Builder:** Maharana Raj Singh I.
- **Date:** 1662–1676 is the common construction period.
- **Rivers:** Gomati, Kelwa and Tali are associated with its catchment/embankment system.
- **Purpose:** A major famine-relief and water-management work, including irrigation and employment during drought.
- **Nauchowki ki Pal:** The marble embankment and steps are a signature feature.
- **Raj Prashasti:** A major Sanskrit eulogy/inscription associated with the embankment and Maharana Raj Singh’s reign. It is traditionally described as consisting of 25 slabs, 24 cantos and about 1,106 verses. It is often called the “largest stone inscription,” but this superlative should be attributed to the source rather than treated as a universally measured global record.
- **Nearby landmark:** Dwarikadhish Temple at Kankroli.

Source: [Rajasthan Tourism: Rajsamand](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/content/rajasthan-tourism/en/tourist-destinations/rajsamand.html)

#### Pichola Lake (*पिछोला झील*)

- **Location:** Udaipur.
- **Origin:** The name is linked to Picholi village. A traditional account credits a Banjara grain merchant, often named Pichhu/Chhitarmal Banjara, with an early damming of the stream in the 14th century; Maharana Udai Singh II later enlarged/strengthened the lake system around the founding of Udaipur.
- **Inflow:** Sisarma and local streams.
- **Key island palaces:** Jag Mandir and Jag Niwas / Lake Palace.
- **Jag Mandir:** Construction took place in stages under Mewar rulers; it is associated with sheltering Prince Khurram during his conflict with Jahangir.
- **Caution:** Claims that the palace directly inspired the Taj Mahal are popular heritage lore and should not be stated as proven architectural causation.

Source: [Rajasthan Tourism: Lakes and Rivers](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/lakes-rivers-to-visit.html)

#### Fateh Sagar Lake (*फतेहसागर*)

- **Location:** Udaipur, north of Pichola.
- **Origin and reconstruction:** Initially constructed by Maharana Jai Singh in 1678; later reconstructed during Maharana Fateh Singh’s reign after flood damage.
- **Connaught Dam:** Named in connection with the visit of the Duke of Connaught and reconstruction of the embankment; the lake became known as Fateh Sagar.
- **Islands:** Three islands, including Nehru Park and the island housing the Udaipur Solar Observatory.
- **Urban function:** Part of Udaipur’s linked lake and reservoir system.
- **Caution:** Keep the lake’s original construction date separate from the date of later reconstruction.

Source: [Rajasthan Tourism: Fateh Sagar Lake](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/fateh-sagar-lake.html)

#### Nakki Lake (*नक्की झील*)

- **Location:** Mount Abu, Sirohi district; a high-elevation lake.
- **Cultural narrative:** Local tradition says it was excavated by the gods with their nails, associated with the word *nakh*.
- **Natural setting:** Surrounded by granite outcrops, including Toad Rock.
- **Caution:** Do not state as established geology that Nakki is definitely a volcanic caldera, or that it is the only lake in Rajasthan that freezes solid. Those claims need geological or meteorological evidence.

#### Ana Sagar Lake (*आना सागर*)

- **Location:** Ajmer.
- **Builder:** Chauhan ruler Arnoraja (Ana Ji), son of Ajaypal, during the 12th century; a common date range is 1135–1150.
- **Type:** Artificial lake/reservoir created by an embankment across a local seasonal stream.
- **Mughal additions:** Jahangir developed the Daulat Bagh garden; Shah Jahan added marble baradaris.
- **Correction to the source story:** Avoid stating as settled history that the lake was built across the Luni or that it was designed specifically to wash away blood from a battlefield. Official tourism sources describe it as an artificial lake created by Arnoraja.

Source: [Rajasthan Tourism: Ajmer](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/ajmer.html)

#### Pushkar Lake (*पुष्कर सरोवर*)

- **Location:** Pushkar, Ajmer district.
- **Status:** Important sacred lake, known in Hindu tradition as *Tirtha Raj*.
- **Ghats:** 52 bathing ghats are the standard figure used by Rajasthan Tourism.
- **Mythological origin:** The fall of Brahma’s lotus is a religious narrative, not geological explanation.
- **Brahma Temple:** The nearby temple is one of the best-known shrines dedicated to Brahma.
- **Fair:** Pushkar Fair peaks around Kartik Purnima.
- **Caution:** Do not call the lake a volcanic caldera without evidence; official tourism describes it in the landscape of the Aravalli valley.

Source: [Rajasthan Tourism: Pushkar Lake](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/pushkar-lake.html)

## 7. Twenty-five high-yield Prelims traps

1. **Named river basins vs internal drainage:** The CGWB’s 14-category listing includes “other nallahs/catchments”; it is not a list of 14 individual rivers.
2. **Drainage percentages:** Inland ~60%, Bay ~22–23%, Arabian ~17–18% are approximate textbook area shares, not universal constants.
3. **Aravalli divide:** It separates many catchments, but local relief creates exceptions; do not treat it as a straight wall.
4. **West-flowing river outlet:** Luni ends in Rann of Kutch; Mahi and Sabarmati reach Gulf of Khambhat; West Banas reaches the Little Rann.
5. **Luni’s salinity:** Evaporation, saline sediments/groundwater and weak dilution are important. Do not attribute all salinity to Tethys Sea deposits or pollution alone.
6. **Jawai Dam:** It is on the Jawai tributary of Luni; it should not be described as the sole source of drinking water for all of Jodhpur.
7. **Mahi and Tropic of Cancer:** Mahi crosses the Tropic of Cancer twice; the “only river in the world” phrasing is not justified.
8. **Mahi Bajaj Sagar:** CEA lists 140 MW installed capacity for Stage I and II combined.
9. **Chambal sequence:** Gandhi Sagar (MP), Kota Barrage (irrigation diversion), Rana Pratap Sagar and Jawahar Sagar (Rajasthan).
10. **Kota Barrage:** Its purpose is irrigation diversion; it has no hydropower station.
11. **Chambal hydropower:** Common aggregate = 115 + 172 + 99 = approximately 386 MW. Use current technical records for updated operating status.
12. **Banas:** Traditionally called the longest major river flowing wholly within Rajasthan; exact length differs by source.
13. **Bigod confluence:** Common exam pairing is Banas + Bedach + Menal.
14. **Rajmahal confluence:** Common exam pairing is Banas + Khari + Dai.
15. **Rameshwaram:** The main confluence is Banas–Chambal; the addition of Seep to a “Triveni” list varies.
16. **Banganga:** Seasonal flow and diversions affect its downstream continuity; “Rundhit Sarita” is not a standard formal hydrological class.
17. **Gambhir vs Gambhiri:** Gambhir is the Karauli–Panchana–Bharatpur river; Gambhiri is the Chittorgarh tributary of Bedach.
18. **Ghaggar–Saraswati:** Palaeochannels are documented; the identification with one unchanged Vedic Saraswati course remains debated.
19. **Nali vs Hakra:** Nali is used for parts of the Ghaggar floodplain in northern Rajasthan; Hakra is used for the downstream channel tradition in Pakistan.
20. **Kantli:** Associated with Torawati, Ganeshwar and Sunari; its 100-km length is a common conventional figure.
21. **Sambhar:** Ramsar site since 23 March 1990; salt output share varies by year.
22. **Pachpadra:** Balotra region; avoid turning a single salt purity statistic into a permanent natural property.
23. **Didwana:** Brines/salts include sodium sulphate; don’t confuse sulphate with sulphide.
24. **Jaisamand:** Built by Maharana Jai Singh on the Gomati in the late 17th century; old sources may place it in Udaipur district, while the current administrative district is Salumbar.
25. **Lake superlatives:** “Largest,” “deepest,” “highest,” “only freezing lake” and “largest inscription in the world” claims need defined metrics or explicit attribution. Prefer stable location, builder, river, and cultural association facts.

## 9. RAS Mains answer frameworks

### Q1. Explain Rajasthan’s major drainage systems.

Rajasthan’s drainage can be divided broadly into inland drainage, Bay of Bengal drainage and Arabian Sea drainage. Inland streams such as Ghaggar, Kantli and Sabi terminate in inland channels, sandy tracts or depressions. The Chambal–Banas systems drain eastward through the Yamuna to the Bay of Bengal. The Luni, Mahi, Sabarmati and West Banas systems reach the Rann of Kutch, Little Rann or Gulf of Khambhat. The Aravallis form an important, though locally complex, divide between catchments. Aridity, rainfall variability, evaporation, reservoirs and water abstraction all influence seasonal river flow and lake levels.

### Q2. Discuss the hydrological and economic significance of the Chambal Valley Project.

The Chambal Valley Project is a joint multipurpose development of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. Its major structures are Gandhi Sagar, Kota Barrage, Rana Pratap Sagar and Jawahar Sagar. Gandhi Sagar provides major upstream storage; Kota Barrage diverts water into irrigation canals; Rana Pratap Sagar and Jawahar Sagar generate hydropower and regulate flows. The project supports irrigation, power generation and regional water management. Its outcomes must be considered alongside interstate water-sharing agreements, reservoir sedimentation, ecological flows and changing irrigation demand.

### Q3. Explain the importance of Rajasthan’s saline lakes.

Sambhar, Pachpadra and Didwana are important saline lake and salt-production landscapes. Their salinity reflects closed drainage basins, high evaporation, saline groundwater and local geology. Sambhar is particularly important as a Ramsar wetland and for wintering waterbirds as well as salt extraction. Saline lakes also support local livelihoods but face pressures from altered inflows, abstraction, pollution, land-use change and ecological disturbance. Sustainable management requires water-quality monitoring, catchment protection, responsible brine/salt extraction and coordination between industry and wetland conservation.

### Q4. Explain the significance and uncertainty of the Ghaggar–Hakra palaeochannel.

The Ghaggar is a seasonal river system flowing from the Shivalik foothills into Haryana and northern Rajasthan. Its channel and downstream Hakra palaeochannel are associated with archaeological sites and a wider network of ancient drainage lines. Satellite imagery and field research support the existence of palaeochannels; however, the precise relationship between these channels, ancient Himalayan river courses and the Vedic Saraswati remains disputed. A balanced answer should separate observable geomorphology from historical identification. Kalibangan is a major Harappan site associated with this landscape.

### Q5. Compare major rivers reaching the two seas.

Rivers in the Arabian Sea system include Luni, Mahi, Sabarmati and West Banas. Luni runs southwest and terminates in the Rann of Kutch; Mahi and Sabarmati reach the Gulf of Khambhat; West Banas drains toward the Little Rann. The Bay of Bengal system includes Chambal and Banas, which flow through the Yamuna network, along with eastern rivers such as Banganga and Gambhir. In contrast, many streams in the western and northeastern parts of Rajasthan are endorheic and terminate in sand, wetlands or saline depressions.
