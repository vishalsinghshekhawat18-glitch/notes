# Chapter 09 — Prajamandal Movements and Modern Political Awakening in Rajasthan

**For:** RPSC RAS Prelims and Mains  
**Revision status:** Rebuilt after a source-critical audit  
**Scope:** Political associations in the princely states of Rajputana, the demand for responsible government and civil liberties, the role of the nationalist press, and major episodes from the 1930s to integration.

> **Study rule:** Learn the secure chronology first. Treat precise casualty counts, “first/last” claims, popular epithets, and dramatic incident details as lower-confidence unless they are supported by contemporary or official records. Princely-state organizations often changed their names, operated in exile, or had precursor bodies; a single year in a coaching table does not always capture their institutional history.

## 1. What the Prajamandal movements were

**Prajamandal** literally means a people’s council or people’s association. In Rajasthan’s princely states, Prajamandals and related bodies such as **Praja Parishads** challenged concentrated royal and feudal authority and demanded political representation, civil liberties, and responsible government.

Their agenda commonly included:

- **Responsible government (*uttardayi shasan*):** ministers and public institutions accountable to representative bodies rather than only to the ruler.
- **Civil liberties:** freedom of speech, association, assembly, publication, and political organization.
- **Anti-feudal demands:** ending forced labour (*begar*), arbitrary levies (*lag-bag*), and abuses by jagirdars and state officials.
- **Social reform:** expansion of education, opposition to untouchability, and participation by women, peasants, workers, and tribal communities.
- **National integration:** alignment of local demands with the wider struggle for independence and, eventually, the integration of princely states into the Indian Union.

The movements did not all use the same tactics. Some petitioned, negotiated, or participated in limited representative institutions; others used satyagraha, hartals, flag demonstrations, and civil disobedience. The line between a Prajamandal and a Praja Parishad was not uniform across states.

### Analytical frame: why did these movements grow?

| Driver | How it mattered |
|---|---|
| Autocratic administration | Rulers and officials often restricted political associations, public meetings, and press activity. |
| Agrarian and feudal grievances | Peasant movements against begar, cesses, and jagirdari abuses created a wider base for political awakening. |
| Nationalist influence | Congress campaigns and leaders, the All-India States’ Peoples’ Conference (AISPC), and nationalist newspapers connected local struggles to all-India politics. |
| Education and print | Schools, libraries, vernacular newspapers, and reform associations helped circulate political ideas. |
| Limited constitutional reforms | Advisory bodies or restricted assemblies often failed to satisfy demands for accountable government. |
| Grassroots participation | Women, students, peasants, tribal communities, and local volunteers made political movements harder to suppress as elite petitioning alone. |

**Important nuance:** Prajamandals did not single-handedly create democracy or guarantee an effortless integration. Their contribution was to organize public demands, establish political leadership, contest autocracy, and increase pressure for representative institutions. Integration also required negotiations, decisions by rulers, and action by the Government of India.

## 2. Precursors and the all-India context

Before the most prominent Prajamandals emerged in the 1930s, social reform bodies, peasant associations, and regional political organizations were creating networks of public activity.

|---|---|---|---|
| **Desh Hiteshini Sabha** | Udaipur, 1877 | Associated with Maharana Sajjan Singh and efforts to restrain extravagant marriage expenditure and certain Rajput social customs. | An important social-reform precursor, not a Prajamandal in the later constitutional-political sense. |
| **Sarvahitkarini Sabha** | Churu, 1907 | Associated with Swami Gopal Das and local reformers; education and social uplift, including schools described in regional histories as serving disadvantaged communities and girls. | Core association is well established in Rajasthan-history literature; exact institutional details should be checked against local records. |

| **Rajasthan Seva Sangh** | Founded at Wardha in 1919; shifted its activity to Ajmer in the following period | Associated with Vijay Singh Pathik, Ramnarayan Chaudhary, and other activists; supported peasant struggles and nationalist publishing. | Distinguish this body from similarly named regional organizations. |

### 2.1 The All-India States’ Peoples’ Conference (AISPC)

The **AISPC**, established in 1927, linked political organizations working in princely states. It gave state-level struggles a forum for exchange, public advocacy, and coordination. The Congress and AISPC were related but distinct organizations; the AISPC was not simply another name for the Indian National Congress.

Frequently cited facts include the founding in Bombay in December 1927 and the association of Dewan Bahadur Ramchandra Rao with its early leadership. Jai Narayan Vyas and other Rajasthan activists participated in this wider political network. Conference numbering and some office-holder details should be treated carefully because sources do not always use the same sequence of annual and special sessions.

### 2.2 Haripura Congress, 1938: a shift in approach to the states

The **Haripura session of the Indian National Congress (February 1938), presided over by Subhas Chandra Bose**, marked an important change in the political climate around princely states. Congress reiterated that the freedom of India could not be conceptually separated from the freedom of people living in princely states, and it gave support to legitimate, peaceful struggles for responsible government and civil liberty.

However, the simplified claim that Congress simply “abandoned non-intervention and directly took over the Prajamandal movements” is misleading. Contemporary statements, including Jawaharlal Nehru’s explanation of Congress policy, show a distinction between supporting state peoples’ struggles and the organizational rule that local struggles should be conducted through the appropriate state-level bodies rather than automatically under the direct name of Congress committees.

**Exam formulation:** Haripura was a landmark in Congress policy toward princely-state peoples’ movements; it strengthened the political legitimacy of demands for responsible government and civil liberties, while the struggles remained substantially organized by state-level associations.

## 4. Major Prajamandals and turning points

### 4.1 Jaipur Prajamandal, Gentlemen’s Agreement, and Azad Morcha

The Jaipur Prajamandal is commonly dated to **1931**, when it was formed under **Kapur Chand Patni**. It was reorganized in 1936–37 with the involvement of **Jamnalal Bajaj**, **Chiranjilal Mishra**, and **Hiralal Shastri**. Its first major public session is commonly given as 8–9 May 1938, presided over by Jamnalal Bajaj; Kasturba Gandhi addressed a women’s meeting in the same period.

#### Gentlemen’s Agreement, 1942

During the Quit India Movement, Jaipur Prime Minister **Sir Mirza Ismail** and Prajamandal President **Hiralal Shastri** reached what is described in important accounts as an **unwritten agreement**. The commonly cited date is **17 September 1942**, though some summaries give only September 1942.

A commonly taught outline of the understanding is:

- the Jaipur Prajamandal would not initiate a Quit India agitation against the state;
- the state would permit a degree of peaceful political activity and move toward responsible government;
- Jaipur would not materially assist the British war effort, according to many exam-oriented summaries.

The exact wording and implementation of the terms should not be presented as if a single signed treaty survives in a universally agreed form. The key historical significance is the political compromise—and the split it produced inside the nationalist camp.

#### Azad Morcha

Dissatisfied activists, led by **Baba Harish Chandra** and including **Ramkaran Joshi**, **Daulatmal Bhandari**, **Gulab Chand Kasliwal**, and **Hans D. Roy** in commonly cited lists, formed the **Azad Morcha** and pursued Quit India activity more directly. Their position challenged Shastri’s compromise with the Jaipur administration. The Morcha was reunited with the Prajamandal in 1945, commonly associated with Jawaharlal Nehru’s mediation or intervention.

**Do not memorize as fact:** that Shastri “boycotted” Quit India to preserve institutional stability, that the written agreement contained a definitive exhaustive list of clauses, or that the agreement itself ended all repression. These are interpretive or overly precise formulations.

### 4.2 Mewar Prajamandal and the Udaipur AISPC meeting

The Mewar Prajamandal was organized on **24 April 1938** in Udaipur, associated with **Manikya Lal Verma**, **Balwant Singh Mehta**, and **Bhuralal Baya**. The first office-holders are generally listed as Balwant Singh Mehta (president), Bhuralal Baya (vice-president), and Manikya Lal Verma (general secretary). It was declared illegal soon after formation; Verma and other activists were subjected to restrictions, expulsion, arrest, or imprisonment.

The organization’s first major session is commonly dated to **1941**, with Verma presiding and Acharya J. B. Kripalani and Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit among the national figures associated with it. The organization’s demands included responsible government and a representative assembly.

On **31 December 1945**, the AISPC met at Udaipur under **Jawaharlal Nehru**; the proceedings extended into early January 1946 in many accounts. Some sources call it the seventh annual session, while others use different session numbering. For exams, the stable facts are the Udaipur venue, the turn-of-year date, and Nehru’s presidency—not the session number without checking the syllabus source.

The subsequent constitutional changes were incremental. A new state constitution was introduced in May 1947; political bargaining continued, and Mewar joined the United State of Rajasthan in 1948. Avoid describing this as an immediate or frictionless transition to full democracy.

### 4.3 Marwar: Prajamandal, Lok Parishad, and Balmukund Bissa

The **Marwar Prajamandal**, commonly dated to **1934**, demanded responsible administration and civil rights. Its leadership included **Jai Narayan Vyas**, **Bhanwarlal Saraf**, **Anand Raj Surana**, and others. The **Marwar Lok Parishad**, established in 1938, became an important vehicle for mass politics and public demands.

**Balmukund Bissa** (often spelled Bissa/Bisa in transliteration) was a nationalist worker associated with Jodhpur’s movement and Jawahar Khadi Bhandar. He was imprisoned in June 1942. The Government of India’s commemorative profile states that he died on **19 June 1942** at a Jodhpur hospital after a hunger strike protesting poor prison food. Do not repeat the claim that he died specifically of “pneumonia caused by force-feeding” unless a medical or contemporary record is supplied.

In 1947, the **Dabra incident** and other anti-feudal struggles demonstrated the continuing overlap between agrarian rights and the wider movement for political change. The Dabra episode is also treated in the chapter on peasant movements; avoid counting it twice as if it were a separate Prajamandal organization.

### 4.4 Bikaner: distinguish the Prajamandal from the Praja Parishad

Rajasthan-history summaries sometimes date an early **Bikaner Prajamandal** to 1936 and associate it with **Magharam Vaidya** and **Laxmi Das Swami**, with Calcutta as its place of organization. Other accounts emphasize the **Bikaner Rajya Praja Parishad**, founded by **Raghuvar Dayal Goyal** on **12 July 1942**, followed by flag satyagraha, arrests, and disputes over recognition. These may refer to different phases or institutional labels and should not be collapsed into a single founding event without evidence.

A major event was the **Raisinghnagar tragedy**, associated with the AISPC/state peoples’ conference meeting on **30 June 1946**. Government of India commemorative material records that the police and military fired on a crowd after unrest around the national flag; revolutionary youth **Birbal Singh** was killed with the tricolour in his hand. **17 July 1946** came to be observed as **Birbal Day** in Bikaner accounts. Some exam sources date his death to 1 July; learn 30 June as the event date and treat the precise death-date variation carefully.

### 4.5 Dungarpur Prajamandal and the Rastapal incident, 1947

The Dungarpur Prajamandal is commonly dated to **1 August 1944**, with **Bhogilal Pandya**, **Haridev Joshi**, and other activists associated with its organization. Education was central to the work of the Vagad Seva Sangh and its schools. State action against such schools became a flashpoint for nationalist and tribal resistance.

At **Rastapal** in June 1947, the state police attempted to close a Seva Sangh school. The Government of India’s commemorative account describes **Nanabhai Kant** being beaten and later dying from his injuries; a teacher named **Sangabhai/Sengabhai** was tied to a vehicle and dragged. **Kalibai**, a Bhil schoolgirl, cut the rope with her sickle and was shot by police. The official account identifies her as **12 years old** and says she died before receiving medical assistance. Rajasthan exam material frequently gives 19 June 1947 for the incident and sometimes 21 June as a later death date; spelling, age, and exact death chronology should be recorded as variants rather than treated as universally settled.

**Safe exam core:** Dungarpur; June 1947; Nanabhai; Kalibai; Sengabhai; a Seva Sangh school; rope cut with a sickle; police firing. The source-supported official narrative should take precedence over embellished versions.

### 4.6 Jaisalmer and Sagar Mal Gopa

**Sagar Mal Gopa** (1900–1946) criticized Jaisalmer’s administration and was imprisoned after being arrested in **May 1941**. His writings are commonly listed as *Jaisalmer Ka Gundaraj*, *Azadi Ke Diwane*, and *Raghunath Singh Ka Muqadma*. The Jaisalmer Prajamandal is commonly dated to 1945 and associated with **Mithalal Vyas**, organized at Jodhpur because political activity was restricted within Jaisalmer.

A vital primary-source correction is available. In a public statement dated **28 April 1946**, Jawaharlal Nehru recorded that Gopa was burned to death in prison on **3 April 1946**. The authorities claimed suicide; Nehru said this claim was exceedingly doubtful, reported evidence of mistreatment and shackling, and stated that Jaisalmer’s authorities had not conducted an inquiry at that time. This contemporaneous statement does **not** establish the detailed claim that a named jailor poured kerosene over Gopa, nor does it support the original chapter’s assertion that a Gopal Swaroop Pathak Commission issued a “whitewash report” in 1946. Those details are excluded from the secure core unless an original report can be produced.

### 4.7 Kota and the 1942 Quit India uprising

The Hadoti Prajamandal is commonly associated with **Pt. Nayanuram Sharma** and colleagues in 1934; a more specifically named Kota Prajamandal followed in the later 1930s. This distinction matters because sources place the formal Kota organization in either 1938 or 1939.

During the Quit India Movement in 1942, Kota saw a major local uprising involving students, Prajamandal workers, and other activists. Accounts describe the crowd taking control of the Kotwali and parts of the city administration, hoisting the national flag, and temporarily exercising popular authority before a settlement restored administrative control. Some popular accounts dramatize this as complete control of the city for exactly three days and the capture of Kota Fort. Unless the particular source is required by a syllabus, use the more cautious formulation: **there was a brief seizure of public authority in Kota, including the Kotwali and flag-raising, during the 1942 uprising**.

### 4.8 Shahpura and responsible government

Shahpura is often credited in Rajasthan exam history with establishing an early form of responsible government on **14 August 1947**. The account associated with the state’s political history names **Raja Sudarshan Dev** and **Gokul Lal Asawa**; the latter became prime minister of the popular government. This was a significant step, but it should not automatically be equated with a fully mature parliamentary democracy or universal franchise.

## 5. Women, students, and socially marginalized communities

## 6. Nationalist press and political print culture

The vernacular press publicized events that princely administrations might otherwise restrict, and linked local struggles with broader nationalist politics. Publication dates and editor attributions vary across sources; the list below emphasizes the commonly tested associations rather than claiming an exhaustive bibliography.

| Newspaper / periodical | Commonly cited association | Exam use and caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Rajasthan Kesari** | Associated with Vijay Singh Pathik, Ramnarayan Chaudhary, and Rajasthan Seva Sangh; initially published from Wardha in the early 1920s. | Helped circulate peasant and political issues; check specific issues for exact editor/date. |
| **Naveen Rajasthan** | Associated with Rajasthan Seva Sangh and its Ajmer activity, around 1922. | Banned or suppressed; later publication names and dates vary in secondary lists. |
| **Tarun Rajasthan** | Successor/reconstituted publication commonly linked with Shobhalal Gupta and Jai Narayan Vyas. | Do not assume it had the same editorial team or uninterrupted run as *Naveen Rajasthan*. |
| **Tyagbhoomi** | Associated with Haribhau Upadhyaya and Gandhian constructive work. | Useful for press-and-reform topics. |
| **Aagiwan** | Commonly cited as a political periodical in Rajasthani/Marwari associated with Jai Narayan Vyas and Beawar, 1932. | “First political newspaper in Rajasthani” is a textbook convention; exact priority depends on the definition of political paper and language. |
| **Prabhat** | Associated with Siddhraj Dhadda and Jaipur public life. | Verify dates and editorial roles through surviving issues when exact chronology matters. |
| **Navjyoti** | Associated with Durga Prasad Chaudhary and Ajmer. | Important later regional newspaper; do not call it the first newspaper in Rajasthan. |
| **Sangram** | Associated with Bhupendranath Trivedi and political mobilization in southern Rajasthan. | Exact founding date and publication details require issue-level evidence. |
| **Lokvani** | Associated with Devi Shankar Tiwari and Jaipur. | Keep publisher/funder assertions separate from the editor’s role. |

**Concept link:** agrarian movements exposed local exploitation; political associations converted these grievances into demands for public rights and accountable government; print media circulated accounts, built solidarity, and brought local struggles to the attention of national leaders.

| 1948–49 | Major phases of political integration | Prajamandals contributed to a broader transition; they were not the only cause |

The table is ordered chronologically; where a range or a convention-dependent date is given, the note in the corresponding section takes priority over false precision.

## 9. Prelims trap list: what to memorize and what to qualify

1. **Earliest commonly cited Prajamandal:** Jaipur, 1931; associated with Kapur Chand Patni.
2. **Jaipur reorganization:** Jamnalal Bajaj, Chiranjilal Mishra, Hiralal Shastri in the 1936–37 period.
3. **Gentlemen’s Agreement:** Sir Mirza Ismail and Hiralal Shastri; commonly dated 17 September 1942; described as unwritten in important accounts.
4. **Azad Morcha:** Dissident faction under Baba Harish Chandra and other activists; formed because they opposed the agreement’s abstention from Quit India.
5. **Mewar Prajamandal’s first president:** Balwant Singh Mehta; Bhuralal Baya vice-president; Manikya Lal Verma general secretary in the initial office-holder list.
6. **Mewar Prajamandal founding date:** 24 April 1938.
7. **AISPC Udaipur meeting:** late December 1945 into early January 1946; Jawaharlal Nehru presided; session numbering varies.
8. **Marwar Lok Parishad:** 1938; distinct from the earlier Marwar Prajamandal and Marwar Hitkarini Sabha.
9. **Balmukund Bissa:** died 19 June 1942 after hunger strike protesting prison food, according to Government of India commemorative material.
10. **Bikaner distinction:** early Bikaner Prajamandal (often dated 1936 in exam lists) versus Bikaner Rajya Praja Parishad (associated with Raghuvar Dayal Goyal in 1942).
11. **Raisinghnagar:** event 30 June 1946; Birbal Singh; Birbal Day 17 July 1946.
12. **Shahpura:** 14 August 1947 is the standard date for the introduction of a popular/responsible government under Gokul Lal Asawa.
13. **Rastapal:** Dungarpur, June 1947; Nanabhai Kant/Khanṭ (spellings vary), Sengabhai/Sangabhai, Kalibai.
14. **Kalibai’s age:** Government of India commemorative account says 12; some teaching material says 13.
15. **Sagar Mal Gopa:** burned to death in prison on 3 April 1946; Nehru’s contemporary statement doubted the official suicide version.
16. **Tasimo:** Dholpur, 11 April 1947; Chhatar Singh and Pancham Singh commonly listed as martyrs.
17. **Gandhi of Vagad:** Bhogilal Pandya.
18. **Gandhi of Rajasthan:** Gokulbhai Bhatt.
19. **Jugal Kishore Chaturvedi:** commonly called the “Nehru of Rajasthan” in regional GK.
20. **Haripura:** Congress policy landmark, but not a simplistic transfer of all state-level movements to direct Congress control.
21. **Organization count:** the source matrix titled “all 19” actually had 20 entries because Hadoti was counted in addition to Kota; treat Hadoti as a predecessor body.
22. **Prajamandal / Praja Parishad:** nomenclature varies; not every state organization had exactly the same title or structure.
23. **“First woman arrested”:** Anjana Devi Chaudhary is commonly cited, but qualify the absolute “first” claim unless supported by a comprehensive record.
24. **Aagiwan:** often cited as an early political newspaper in Rajasthani/Marwari; “first” requires a clear definition and issue-level verification.
25. **Integration:** Prajamandal campaigns contributed to the political context of integration but did not alone create Rajasthan or guarantee a seamless transition.

## 10. Practice questions

### Q1. Which statement best describes the significance of the Haripura Congress of 1938 for princely-state politics?

A. Congress absorbed all Prajamandals into its formal party structure.  
B. Congress gave stronger support to state peoples’ demands for responsible government and civil liberty while local struggles remained organized through state-level bodies.  
C. All princely states immediately obtained elected governments.  
D. AISPC was dissolved.

**Answer: B.** Haripura was a landmark in policy, not an immediate constitutional transfer of power.

### Q2. The Jaipur Gentlemen’s Agreement of 1942 is associated with which pair?

A. Jai Narayan Vyas and Maharaja Umaid Singh  
B. Sir Mirza Ismail and Hiralal Shastri  
C. Jawaharlal Nehru and Gokul Lal Asawa  
D. Jamnalal Bajaj and Gokulbhai Bhatt

**Answer: B.** It was commonly dated 17 September 1942 and is described in some important accounts as an unwritten understanding.

### Q3. Why did the Azad Morcha emerge in Jaipur in 1942?

A. It opposed the formation of the AISPC.  
B. It opposed the Jaipur Prajamandal leadership’s decision not to launch Quit India agitation under the agreement.  
C. It sought restoration of British direct rule.  
D. It was a peasant organization founded at Bijolia in 1903.

**Answer: B.** Dissident nationalists pursued Quit India activity more directly.

### Q4. Which pairing is correct?

A. Mewar Prajamandal — Balwant Singh Mehta as initial president  
B. Bikaner Rajya Praja Parishad — founded in 1931 by Kapur Chand Patni  
C. Dungarpur Prajamandal — founded by Sagar Mal Gopa  
D. Jaisalmer Prajamandal — formed in Udaipur in 1938

**Answer: A.** The initial Mewar office-holder list places Balwant Singh Mehta as president.

### Q5. Which event is associated with Birbal Singh?

A. Rastapal, Dungarpur  
B. Raisinghnagar, Bikaner region  
C. Tasimo, Dholpur  
D. Dabra, Marwar

**Answer: B.** Birbal Singh was killed during the Raisinghnagar tragedy on 30 June 1946; 17 July became Birbal Day.

### Q6. What does the contemporary Nehru Archive statement on Sagar Mal Gopa establish most securely?

A. A named jailor was proven in court to have poured kerosene on him.  
B. A Gopal Swaroop Pathak Commission’s 1946 report definitively proved murder.  
C. Gopa burned to death in prison on 3 April 1946, and Nehru doubted the official suicide explanation.  
D. Gopa died in 1952.

**Answer: C.** Nehru’s statement reports the death and challenges the official account; it does not prove every later dramatic detail.

### Q7. The Rastapal incident is associated with which sequence?

A. Nanabhai was killed; Sengabhai was dragged behind a vehicle; Kalibai cut the rope and was shot.  
B. Birbal Singh hoisted a flag at Dholpur and was killed.  
C. Sagar Mal Gopa cut a rope to rescue his teacher.  
D. Hiralal Shastri formed Azad Morcha.

**Answer: A.** The event is associated with a Seva Sangh school in Dungarpur in June 1947.

### Q8. Which statement is the safest exam formulation about Shahpura?

A. It was the first territory in India with universal adult franchise.  
B. It is commonly credited in Rajasthan history with introducing a responsible/popular government on 14 August 1947 under Gokul Lal Asawa.  
C. It joined the AISPC in 1927 and immediately became independent.  
D. It hosted the first Prajamandal in Rajasthan in 1931.

**Answer: B.** Avoid inflating a major political milestone into a claim about universal franchise or full modern parliamentary government.

### Q9. Which distinction about Bikaner is useful for careful revision?

A. Prajamandal and Praja Parishad are always interchangeable names for a single body.  
B. Accounts distinguish an earlier Prajamandal, often dated 1936, from the Praja Parishad associated with Raghuvar Dayal Goyal in 1942.  
C. There was no political organization in Bikaner before 1947.  
D. Birbal Singh was associated with Rastapal.

**Answer: B.** Names and phases should not be collapsed.

### Q10. Which is the most defensible statement about Prajamandals and Rajasthan’s integration?

A. They were the sole cause of integration and rulers played no role.  
B. They contributed to popular political pressure and leadership; integration also depended on negotiations and decisions by rulers and the Government of India.  
C. They began only after integration was complete.  
D. They demanded the permanent continuation of autocracy.

**Answer: B.** This captures the movements’ important contribution without monocausal exaggeration.

## 11. Mains answer frameworks

### 2 marks: What were Prajamandals?
Prajamandals were political associations in princely states that demanded responsible government, civil liberties, representative institutions, and relief from autocratic and feudal abuses.

### 5 marks: Explain the significance of the Haripura Congress resolution for Rajasthan.

- It strengthened the legitimacy of demands for civil liberty and responsible government in princely states.
- It encouraged state-level organizations to act more openly and connected their struggles with the wider national movement.
- It contributed to the growth of Prajamandals in several states during 1938 and after.
- It did not mean that every princely-state agitation became a direct Congress campaign; state-level organization remained important.

### 5 marks: Explain the Jaipur Gentlemen’s Agreement and Azad Morcha.

The 1942 understanding between Sir Mirza Ismail and Hiralal Shastri attempted to manage political pressure during Quit India. The Prajamandal agreed not to launch direct agitation against the Jaipur state, while the state was said to have promised greater space for peaceful activity and movement toward responsible government. Dissident activists led by Baba Harish Chandra formed Azad Morcha because they opposed abstention from Quit India. The episode illustrates the tension between negotiation for political gains and participation in a national mass movement.

### 10 marks: Assess the contribution of Prajamandals to modern political awakening in Rajasthan.

**Thesis:** Prajamandals helped turn grievances against autocratic and feudal administration into organized demands for accountable government and civil liberties.

1. **Institutional change:** created political bodies, petitions, conferences, and organized campaigns across the princely states.
2. **Civil rights:** demanded freedom of speech, press, assembly, and association, and opposed arbitrary detention and suppression of political activity.
3. **Socio-economic links:** connected anti-begar, anti-cess, and peasant struggles with constitutional questions.
4. **Social participation:** broadened public politics through women, students, tribal communities, and disadvantaged groups.
5. **Press and networks:** used newspapers, inter-state conferences, and AISPC links to circulate local grievances nationally.
6. **Limits and nuance:** organizations differed in ideology, tactics, scale, and social base; not all included all sections equally, and some made compromises that others rejected.
7. **Integration:** helped prepare political leadership and public opinion for representative institutions and integration, alongside negotiations and actions by rulers and the Government of India.

**Conclusion:** The Prajamandal movement was a crucial bridge between anti-feudal protest and constitutional politics in Rajasthan, but its achievements should be understood as part of a broader process rather than as the work of a single organization or leader.

### Claims intentionally downgraded or removed from the secure core

- Exhaustive lists of exact clauses for the Gentlemen’s Agreement without an original copy.
- The claim that the Haripura Congress directly “authorized” every Prajamandal to operate as a Congress organization.
- A single indisputable session number for the 1945–46 Udaipur AISPC meeting.
- Unsupported claims that a named jailor poured kerosene on Sagar Mal Gopa or that a 1946 Pathak Commission definitively ruled his death a suicide.
- “All 19 Prajamandals” when the source itself listed 20 rows by adding Hadoti as well as Kota.
- Exact casualty, attendance, first/last, and “only/never” claims without a traceable source.
- The absolute assertion that the people of Kota controlled Kota Fort and all administration for exactly three days; the safer core is a temporary uprising, flag raising, and control of public institutions as described by regional histories.

**Revision status:** This is a substantially improved working source for exam preparation. Several institution-specific dates remain convention-dependent, and full verification of every state-level association would require original princely-state files, association records, and local newspaper runs.
