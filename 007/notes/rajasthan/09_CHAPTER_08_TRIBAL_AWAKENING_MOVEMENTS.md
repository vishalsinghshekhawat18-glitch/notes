# CHAPTER 08 — TRIBAL AWAKENING MOVEMENTS OF RAJASTHAN
## RPSC RAS Prelims and Mains | Source-led revised study chapter

**Version:** Revised after a factual and source-quality audit of the supplied chapter.  
**Scope:** Bhil resistance in the nineteenth century; the 1881 Rikhabdeo uprising; the Bhagat movement and Govind Guru; Mangarh (1913); the Eki movement and Motilal Tejawat; Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav (1922); repression in the Sirohi–Bhomat belt; and the Meena movement against criminalisation and compulsory police surveillance.

> **Revision rule:** Memorise well-supported dates, leaders, organisations and causes. Treat exact casualty counts, disputed place names, specific police rules, and highly detailed treaty/settlement claims with caution. This chapter distinguishes exam convention from claims that are independently documented.

---

## 1. Evidence labels: what can safely be memorised?

- **Documented core:** Supported by legislation, official records, or multiple independent historical accounts.
- **Exam convention:** Repeated in Rajasthan exam materials and potentially testable, but not necessarily established by accessible primary documentation.
- **Contested / source-dependent:** Accounts disagree, especially on casualty totals, exact location labels, dates, names of officers, or the interpretation of an event.
- **Local memory / commemorative language:** Valuable to community history and memorial culture, but should not automatically be treated as an audited statistic or a verbatim contemporary record.

These categories are not value judgements about tribal memory. Colonial records themselves may minimise violence or classify a political confrontation as a minor “skirmish”; oral memory can preserve events that official archives failed to record fully. Where records disagree, the correct study habit is to retain the disagreement—not invent precision.

## 2. Syllabus fit and the chapter’s central argument

Rajasthan-history syllabi include tribal and peasant movements within the wider study of freedom struggle, social change and political awakening. The current RPSC RAS syllabus frames Rajasthan’s history through freedom movement and political awakening and gives attention to tribal aspects of society and culture; other official RPSC Rajasthan-history syllabi explicitly name tribal and peasant movements. See Sources S1–S2.

**Central argument:** Tribal awakening in southern and eastern Rajasthan was not one continuous, uniform revolt. It developed through different but overlapping responses to the expanding power of princely states and colonial institutions: loss or restriction of customary rights, revenue and cesses, forced labour (*begar*), police surveillance, interference in community practices, and the weakening of local authority. Govind Guru’s Bhagat movement emphasised social-religious reform and collective discipline; Motilal Tejawat’s Eki movement foregrounded shared agrarian grievances and non-cooperation; the Meena movement challenged inherited criminalisation and intrusive policing.

### The sequence at a glance

| Period | Event / movement | Core significance | Location anchor |

# PART I — BHIL RESISTANCE AND THE CHANGING FRONTIER

## 3. Communities, geography and customary authority

### 3.1 The region

The movements discussed here were concentrated in southern Rajasthan and its adjoining historical borderlands:

- **Vagad:** chiefly the Dungarpur–Banswara area; closely associated with the Bhil population and Govind Guru’s movement.
- **Bhomat / Mewar hill tract:** a rugged hill-and-forest region where Bhil village communities and their headmen (*gametis*) held local influence.
- **Mewar–Gujarat borderland:** movement networks crossed princely boundaries and what are now state boundaries. The Eki movement’s major 1922 firing site, Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav, lies in present-day Gujarat, although Rajasthan accounts frequently include it in their chapter on Rajasthan’s tribal movement.
- **Sirohi and the Garasia areas:** another arena of repression during the wider Eki-era unrest.

**Geographical caution:** “Rajasthan” is often used retrospectively in exam notes for a movement that occurred in a princely state or borderland later incorporated into Rajasthan. For a historical answer, identify the contemporary jurisdiction as well as the present-day state.

### 3.2 Customary authority and livelihood

Bhil communities in the hill tracts relied on a combination of farming, forest produce, local exchange, customary duties and village-level political arrangements. Terms often used in accounts include:

- **Gameti:** a village or local community headman in the Bhil areas.
- **Bhomat / Pal:** names associated with the hill-and-village political geography of Mewar; use them as regional terms rather than treating them as a single, formally bounded modern district.
- **Bolai:** a traditional fee or customary claim associated in accounts with safe passage/escort through particular routes.
- **Rakhwali:** a customary watch-and-protection payment in some accounts.
- **Begar:** compulsory labour without ordinary wages or meaningful consent.

Princely and colonial authorities sought to control roads, policing, taxation and the movement of people. Restrictions on customary payments such as *bolai* and *rakhwali*, expanding police presence, intervention in social practices and the introduction of census operations could be experienced as more than administrative reforms: they affected income, authority, and the relationship between local communities and the state. The details differed by locality and year. It is therefore too simple to say that one uniform British order instantly abolished all customary tribal rights throughout Rajasthan. See the Rajasthan government heritage account and the historical study listed as S3–S4.

### 3.3 Mewar Bhil Corps (MBC), 1841

**High-yield facts**

- The Mewar Bhil Corps was raised in **1841**, with its headquarters at **Kherwara** and a detachment at **Kotra**.
- It operated under British officers and became part of the policing and military apparatus in the Mewar hill region.
- Its creation combined recruitment/employment of Bhils with the political objective of securing a difficult frontier and maintaining order under the princely state–British arrangement.
- **Funding correction:** It was **not financed entirely from Mewar’s treasury**. Historical accounts describe a shared arrangement: Mewar paid a substantial contribution, while the British Indian government also contributed. An Imperial Gazetteer-derived account gives a total annual cost of approximately Rs 1,20,000, with Mewar paying Rs 50,000; exact financial arrangements could vary over time. See S5.

**Interpretive point:** The corps illustrates how colonial rule could recruit some members of a community into an institution that was also used against other members of that community. It should not be described only as “an army created to suppress tribals,” nor only as a welfare employment measure: both recruitment and political control were involved.

## 4. The early Bhil uprisings and the Rikhabdeo settlement, 1881

### 4.1 Background to the 1881 rising

Bhil resistance followed the 1818 political settlement between Mewar and the British and continued intermittently through the 1820s. Later official heritage writing places important confrontations in 1826 and further negotiations in 1828. The chronology in older administrative and historical accounts is not always identical; avoid reducing the entire period to one precisely dated “revolt” unless a particular source is being followed. See S3–S4.

The **1881 rising** is better documented as a sequence of confrontations than as a single moment. Commonly identified grievances include:

1. **Census operations:** rumours and apprehension that enumeration might bring new taxes, forced recruitment or other restrictions.
2. **Taxes and customary claims:** especially the *barar* or war-tax issue discussed in historical accounts, alongside disputes over *bolai* and related rights.
3. **Policing and state intrusion:** new or expanded police posts and efforts to compel local headmen to cooperate with officials.
4. **Interference in community practices:** some accounts connect the conflict to state intervention in religious/social matters, including practices around witchcraft accusations. These claims should be contextualised, not used to imply that every customary practice was harmless or that all state intervention had the same cause.

### 4.2 The rising and negotiations

The rising spread through localities including **Padona and Barapal** and disrupted communications. State forces were sent into the hills; the difficult terrain and the ability of Bhil groups to disperse and reassemble made a purely military solution difficult. The Maharana’s personal secretary **Shyamaldas**, the historian associated with *Vir Vinod*, played a role in negotiations. British political officials and Mewar officers also became involved.

Negotiations took place at or around **Rikhabdeo (Kesariyaji)** in April 1881. Historical accounts describe Bhil representatives presenting a list of demands—often given as approximately **24**, not 21—and the state agreeing to concessions that included remission of **half the *barar* tax** and suspension of census work for the time being. Other accounts describe concessions concerning road security and the customary *bolai* claim for particular pilgrim routes. Unrest did not disappear permanently; further disturbances occurred in 1882.

### Correction ledger: claims not safe as absolute facts

- **“The Sacred Rikhabdeo Accord was signed on 25 April 1881”:** the exact day is not consistently supported by the sources reviewed. Describe it as an agreement reached through negotiations in April 1881 unless the cited text supplies the precise date.
- **“A 21-clause compromise”:** not supported by the strongest historical account located; the Bhil demands are reported as around 24 in one detailed narrative.
- **“Land taxes were cut in half”:** overbroad. The specific concession reported is remission of **half the *barar* tax**, not a proven half-cut in all land revenue.
- **“Customary forest use was temporarily recognised”:** the accessible accounts reviewed do not establish this as a clause of the 1881 settlement. Do not present it as a verified settlement term.

**Prelims capsule:** 1881 Bhil uprising → census/tax/police and customary-rights grievances → negotiations at Rikhabdeo → half-*barar* remission and census suspension reported → unrest persisted into 1882.

# PART II — GOVIND GURU, THE BHAGAT MOVEMENT AND MANGARH

## 5. Govind Guru (Govindgiri): life and approach

### 5.1 Identity and background

- **Name:** Govind Guru / Govindgiri.
- **Birth:** generally dated to **1858**.
- **Birthplace:** Bansia/Bansiya in the Dungarpur region is the common account.
- **Community background:** usually described as belonging to a **Banjara** family, not a Bhil family by birth.
- **Death:** **30 October 1931**; his later years and memorial are associated with **Kamboi** in present-day Gujarat.

Some biographical narratives give an exact birth day, identify a specific spiritual mentor, or describe a definite meeting with Swami Dayanand Saraswati. Those details are not necessary to the secure exam core unless they are corroborated by the prescribed textbook. Govind Guru’s reformist ideas are often discussed in relation to the wider nineteenth-century reform milieu, but the precise route of influence should not be treated as established merely because it appears in coaching notes. See S6–S7.

### 5.2 The Bhagat movement: core ideas

The movement associated with Govind Guru drew Bhils and other local followers into a moral and social reform network. Commonly reported practices included:

- abstinence from alcohol and intoxicants;
- vegetarianism or restrictions on meat and animal sacrifice in the movement’s reform ethic;
- cleanliness, truthful conduct and disciplined daily life;
- regular worship at a **dhuni** (sacred fire) and the use of a flag or *nishan* as a visible sign of collective identity;
- opposition to *begar*, illegal exactions and exploitative practices;
- use of community meetings and local panchayats to resolve disputes.

The central political point is that moral reform and material grievances reinforced one another. Temperance could reduce dependence on liquor networks and improve household resources; refusal of *begar* directly challenged demands for unpaid labour; a shared ritual practice could turn dispersed settlements into a recognisable organisation.

### 5.3 Samp Sabha (standard exam date: 1883)

**Samp Sabha** is associated with Govind Guru and is commonly dated to **1883**. Many Rajasthan exam references give **Sirohi district** as its founding location. Because secondary accounts vary in how they state the founding site and because the movement’s network spread across princely borders, the venue should be classified as a **textbook convention**, not an equally secure fact in every detail.

The word **samp** is commonly explained as unity, harmony, fraternity or solidarity. The Sabha promoted ethical discipline, communal solidarity and organised resistance to exploitation among Bhil followers. It was supported by a network of **dhunis**, flags and local organisers across the Vagad–Mewar–Gujarat border region.

#### The “ten vows” problem

The original source claims a fixed set of ten vows but lists only seven. More importantly, different popular accounts count and phrase the rules differently. Do **not** memorise an invented ten-item list. Remember the recurring core: sobriety, ethical conduct and cleanliness, reform of harmful social practices, worship through the *dhuni*, community solidarity, and rejection of exploitation and forced labour. If an examination asks for a precise list, use the exact list in the prescribed Rajasthan textbook and keep it separate from this broader conceptual summary.

### 5.4 Why the movement alarmed authorities

The Bhagat network was more than a private religious association. Its growth could:

- weaken alcohol-related revenue and the interests of liquor contractors;
- make communities less willing to perform *begar* or pay arbitrary levies;
- reduce dependence on feudal officials and state courts through local collective organisation;
- create a cross-border mobilisation of followers who shared a leader, symbols and a programme.

These mechanisms help explain the state response without assuming that every participant had exactly the same political objective or that the movement had a single, fully documented written constitution.

## 6. Mangarh Dham massacre, 17 November 1913

### 6.1 Location and gathering

**Mangarh Hill** lies in the Rajasthan–Gujarat border region and is today a shared memorial landscape for the Bhil and other tribal communities of the area. On **17 November 1913**, a large gathering associated with Govind Guru’s Bhagat movement was fired upon by British and princely-state forces. Government of India material states that more than **1.5 lakh** people had rallied at Mangarh and gives a commemorative estimate of approximately **1,500** tribals killed. These figures are widely repeated in official commemorations; they should not be mistaken for a casualty roll audited person by person. See S6.

The gathering combined religious observance, collective identity and political-economic grievances. Accounts describe authorities as fearing a challenge to the established order, while followers were gathered around their movement’s leadership and practices.

### 6.2 What is secure—and what is not

| Claim | Assessment | Safe formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Date: 17 November 1913 | Strongly established in official commemorative and historical accounts | Memorise the date. |
| Govind Guru led the Bhagat movement associated with the gathering | Strongly established | Memorise. |
| British and princely-state forces opened fire | Strongly established | Memorise. |
| Roughly 1,500 killed | Widely used official estimate; exact total is not independently audited | Say “about 1,500 in official commemorative accounts; exact toll remains uncertain.” |
| More than 1.5 lakh gathered | Officially repeated estimate; precise attendance cannot be treated as a census | Learn as the standard government/exam estimate, but label it an estimate. |
| Exact military units and command chain | Secondary accounts differ | Avoid presenting a long list of regiments/officers as a settled fact. |
| “Govind Guru was shot in the leg” and every detail of capture | Reported in some narratives; primary documentation not fully verified here | Attribute to the account being used; not required as a core fact. |
| “Jallianwala Bagh of Rajasthan” | Widely used commemorative comparison | Use as a memorial analogy, not a claim that the two events were identical. |

The Rajasthan government’s tribal freedom-struggle museum page refers to a commander named **I. P. Stokley**, while other secondary accounts list different officers or distinguish the political and field command. The supplied draft named Major Hamilton and Captain Stoppford as though the command structure were settled. That exact roster was not confirmed in the sources reviewed and has therefore been removed from the secure core. See S3, S6–S7.

### 6.3 Arrest, sentence, release and later life

After the firing, Govind Guru was arrested and tried. Common biographies report that an initial death sentence was commuted and that he was released around **1923**, subject to restrictions on movement/entry into certain princely states. He later lived at **Kamboi** in present-day Gujarat and continued social-reform work until his death in 1931.

The broad sequence—arrest, severe sentence, commutation, release with restrictions and later life at Kamboi—is repeated in biographical accounts. The precise chain of court decisions, duration of each sentence and exact wording of territorial restrictions should not be asserted as “secure core” without the trial and release instruments themselves.

### 6.4 Historical significance

Mangarh is important for at least four reasons:

1. It demonstrates the ability of social-religious reform to produce mass mobilisation.
2. It shows that tribal struggles often linked dignity and cultural change with material issues such as unpaid labour and exactions.
3. It exposes the limits of a freedom-struggle narrative centred only on urban organisations and regions with abundant newspaper coverage.
4. Its casualty figures and command details show why official records, contemporary reports and community memory must be read together rather than treated as interchangeable evidence.

# PART III — MOTILAL TEJAWAT AND THE EKI MOVEMENT

## 7. Motilal Tejawat: background and political method

Motilal Tejawat (usually dated **1886–1963**, though birth-year variants appear) was born at **Koliyari/Koliyari**, in the Jhadol region of Mewar. Accounts commonly describe him as belonging to an **Oswal Jain** family and having worked as a *kamdar* or functionary associated with a thikana before turning to tribal mobilisation. His experience of exploitation and his contact with the Bijolia agitation are often cited as influences on his political activity.

He became known to followers by honorifics such as **Bavji/Bawji**. “Gandhi of the tribals” is a popular epithet, not a neutral institutional title. It should not be used to imply that Gandhi officially authorised, controlled or fully endorsed Tejawat’s movement.

## 8. Eki movement: grievances, organisation and demands

### 8.1 The meaning and development of Eki

**Eki** means unity. The movement developed around **1920–21**, bringing Bhils and Garasias together against exploitative practices by jagirdars and princely-state authorities. A major mobilisation and oath-taking episode is associated with **Matrikundiya** in the Chittorgarh region, especially the fair/meeting commonly dated to **Vaishakhi Purnima in 1921**. Government material also describes the movement as launched at Matrikundiya in 1920 and the 1921 fair as a major leadership moment. The safest formulation is therefore: *the Eki movement developed in 1920–21; Matrikundiya was its key mobilisation site, with a major oath-taking meeting in 1921.* See S7.

### 8.2 Main grievances

The movement linked multiple grievances that should be learned as a package:

- **Begar:** forced, unpaid or under-compensated labour demanded by local authorities and landlords.
- **Lāg-bāg and cesses:** arbitrary or burdensome levies.
- **Revenue:** demands against oppressive or unaffordable land-revenue arrangements.
- **Forest and grazing access:** restrictions affecting collection of firewood/forest produce and use of common resources.
- **Crop damage and wild animals:** complaints about crop-destroying boars and limits on local action against them in some accounts.
- **Justice and official conduct:** opposition to extortion, corruption and the inability of peasants to secure redress.

A commonly repeated slogan is **“Na Hakim, Na Hukam”** or a closely related form—expressing rejection of oppressive official and feudal commands. Spelling and wording vary across notes; the underlying political point is non-cooperation with exploitative authority.

### 8.3 *Mewar Ki Pukar*: avoid false precision

Tejawat is associated with a **21-point charter of demands** commonly called *Mewar Ki Pukar*. Rajasthan exam material often says that the Maharana accepted 18 points and rejected three. This 18/3 formulation is repeated in a Government of Rajasthan culture repository, which describes the rejected demands as including gathering wood, gathering grass from grasslands, and hunting crop-destroying swine. Other popular notes list different combinations—often substituting the abolition of *begar* or “forest rights” as a rejected item.

**Audit decision:** Retain the **21-point charter** as the standard exam fact. Treat the exact list of the three rejected demands as **source-dependent** until the original demand charter and the state’s written response are consulted. The supplied draft’s categorical list “begar, forest rights and wild boars” is not reliable enough to teach as an uncontested verbatim record.

### 8.4 The Pal-Chitariya / Dadhvav massacre, 7 March 1922

This is the most important factual and geographical correction in the supplied chapter.

- **Date:** 7 March 1922.
- **Names:** **Pal-Chitariya / Pal-Chitariya–Dadhvav / Pal-Dadhvav** are common names in historical and Gujarat-focused accounts. The location was then in **Idar State** and is now in the **Vijaynagar area of Sabarkantha district, Gujarat**.
- **Connection to Rajasthan materials:** A Government of India Culture repository describes a *Jan Sabha* at **Neemda** near the Gujarat–Rajasthan border on this date and links it to Dadhvav. Some Rajasthan exam material consequently calls this the “Neemda/Neemra” event. These location labels should not be silently merged into a claim that the massacre occurred in a Rajasthan village; the more geographically specific Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav identification places the firing in the then-Idar jurisdiction, now Gujarat. See S7, S9–S10.
- **Forces:** The Mewar Bhil Corps, under British officer **Major H. G. Sutton** in the commonly cited account, opened fire on the gathering.
- **Casualties:** The number is deeply disputed. A colonial account reportedly described the action as a “skirmish” with 22 deaths; oral accounts and later press/historical investigations have given estimates around **1,000–1,500**, often approximately **1,200**. The Government of India Culture repository repeats the higher estimate. There is no basis for treating 1,200 as a precisely audited count. Learn it as the conventional estimate, alongside the dispute.

This discrepancy is not a reason to erase the event. It is an example of how an under-reported episode can have a small colonial casualty figure and a much larger remembered toll. A strong Mains answer recognises both source asymmetry and the evidence limits.

### 8.5 Repression elsewhere in the Sirohi–Bhomat belt

The Eki movement was followed by further confrontations in the southern Rajasthan and adjoining Gujarat borderlands. Accounts refer to incidents at **Siawa** and at **Bhula–Balolia/Balohiya** in April–May 1922, including killings, arson and destruction of houses/grain stores. Exact names, dates and figures vary by source; one widely circulated narrative describes 1,800 deaths and 640 houses burnt in a particular incident, while other Rajasthan study material gives markedly lower casualty totals and different house-destruction figures. The supplied draft’s claim that Major Pritchard burnt **1,800 dwellings** at “Rasia-Valoriya” conflates inconsistent claims and is not retained as a secure statistic.

**Safe exam formulation:** After the Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav firing, police and state forces also suppressed Eki-linked communities in the Sirohi–Bhomat region, including incidents commonly labelled Siawa and Bhula–Balolia/Balohiya; casualty and property-loss numbers vary substantially in the sources.

### 8.6 Tejawat’s surrender and later life

Standard accounts place Tejawat’s surrender at **Khedbrahma** in **1929**, following efforts to persuade him to surrender peacefully. The claim that Gandhi’s advice was conveyed through **Manilal Kothari** is repeated in secondary accounts; the exact chain of communication should be attributed to those accounts unless the correspondence is cited. Many biographies describe later custody or restrictions before his subsequent public activity. He died in **1963**.

### 8.7 Historiographical nuance: not simply “Gandhian”

The Eki movement used collective oaths, non-payment/non-cooperation and agrarian demands, but it also developed in a political context where armed followers and state repression were reported. Gandhi publicly denied that Tejawat was his disciple in a *Young India* statement, as reproduced in later accounts. This matters because “Gandhi of the tribals” can wrongly imply full organisational endorsement by Gandhi. Use the epithet as popular memory, while describing the movement’s own organisation and grievances separately. See S9–S10.


## 9. Later Bhil protest against forest controls, 1943

The Rajasthan government heritage narrative also records a later Bhil protest around **Gogunda**. It describes a meeting at the **Rameshwar Mahadev** pilgrimage site, followed by demonstrations on **31 May 1943** against forest-related rules and restrictions. The account reports that more than **450 people were arrested**. Because the page is a heritage summary rather than the complete case file, use this as a useful late-period example of continuing conflict over forest access and colonial administration, while treating the arrest figure as the figure reported by that source. See S3.

This episode connects the earlier Bhil resistance over customary practices and access to resources with later protests against the formalisation of forest control. It also demonstrates that tribal political awakening did not end with Mangarh or the Eki movement.

# PART IV — MEENA REFORM AND RESISTANCE TO CRIMINALISATION

## 10. Historical setting and the two terms

The supplied chapter divides Meenas into two social categories:

- **Zamindari Meena:** associated in regional accounts with cultivation and landholding.
- **Chaukidari Meena:** associated with hereditary village-watchman or policing duties in certain local arrangements.

These labels describe historical social positions and should not be treated as a fixed, uniform classification covering every Meena in every princely state. Regional practices varied. In some arrangements, village watchmen could be held responsible for failures of security or for thefts in their beat, exposing them to fines, punishment or coercive duties. Any precise rule should be tied to its relevant state, statute and period.

The Meena movement should be understood as a struggle against **collective criminalisation, surveillance, stigma and coercive local obligations**, not as evidence that criminality was an inherent characteristic of any community. “Criminal tribe” was a state-imposed administrative category under a discriminatory legal regime, not a valid description of a people.

## 11. The legal timeline: do not collapse three different things

The supplied chapter presents the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871, the 1924 act, Jaipur’s Jairam Pesha rules and the 1952 repeal as if these were one measure. They are related, but not interchangeable.

### 11.1 Central Criminal Tribes legislation

- **1871:** the first Criminal Tribes Act was enacted under British rule.
- **1924:** a later Criminal Tribes Act consolidated the applicable legal framework. It allowed authorities to declare a tribe, gang or class—or part of one—a “criminal tribe” by official notification. The statute’s text is available in S11.
- **Application in Rajputana:** historical scholarship on the Meenas dates the application/classification in Rajputana to **1924**, with further state-level arrangements in Jaipur in **1930**. It is therefore inaccurate to say without qualification that every Meena had been legally declared criminal by a single all-India act in 1871. See S12.

### 11.2 Jaipur State’s Jairam/Jarayam Pesha regime, commonly dated 1930

Rajasthan exam literature commonly refers to the Jaipur State’s **Jairam Pesha**, **Jarayam Pesha** or related “criminal profession” law/rules around **1930**. The regime imposed registration and police-attendance/surveillance obligations on notified Meenas. It intensified humiliation, constrained movement and reinforced the idea that community identity itself was grounds for suspicion.

**Critical caution about the attendance rules:** Popular notes conflict about whether the rule applied to all Meena males and females, only registered/notified people, only Chaukidari Meenas, people over 12, or adults within a different age band; the schedule is also variously described as daily, twice daily or otherwise. The original draft’s categorical formulation—“every male, female and child aged 12+ had to attend morning and evening; penalty was immediate public flogging”—was not verified against the original Jaipur notification and also conflicts with its own later claim that only Chaukidari Meenas were covered.

**What to learn securely:** Jaipur State introduced a coercive local regime around 1930 associated with compulsory registration/attendance and surveillance of Meenas. The precise eligible categories, age threshold, frequency and penalty must be checked against the prescribed textbook or the original state rule before use in a detail-sensitive answer.

### 11.3 Repeal and denotification in 1952

The **Criminal Tribes Laws (Repeal) Act, 1952 (Act XXIV of 1952)** repealed the Criminal Tribes Act, 1924 and corresponding laws, with repeal taking effect on **31 August 1952** unless an earlier date was notified. The primary statutory text is hosted by India Code (S13).

This legal fact needs to be phrased carefully:

- The all-India repeal of the 1924 act and corresponding laws in 1952 is secure.
- Historical literature dates the full denotification of Meenas to **31 August 1952**.
- Rajasthan exam notes often state that the Jairam Pesha law was abolished in 1952, with the efforts of leaders including **Hiralal Shastri** and **Tikaram Paliwal** contributing to the change. However, the central repeal act should not be cited as proof of every separate Jaipur rule’s exact repeal instrument unless the relevant state order is produced.
- Denotification ended this legal category; it did not immediately remove social stigma or all forms of policing and habitual-offender legislation.

**Prelims distinction:** 1871 = original Criminal Tribes Act; 1924 = later consolidated act and commonly cited Rajputana application year; 1930 = Jaipur’s Jairam/Jarayam Pesha regime; 31 August 1952 = central repeal/denotification milestone.

## 12. Meena reform organisations and mass protest

### 12.1 Association building

Accounts describe the establishment of a **Meena Kshatriya Sabha / Meena Kshetriya Mahasabha** around **1933**. Naming varies among sources. These associations promoted social reform, collective dignity, petitions and organised opposition to humiliating laws and police surveillance.

### 12.2 Muni Magan Sagar and *Meena Puran*

**Muni Magan Sagar**, a Jain ascetic, is associated with *Meena Puran* and with the articulation of a more affirmative history of the Meena community. The text and its genealogical claims are best understood as part of a **community reform and identity-formation movement**. Its claims about ancient ancestry should not be substituted for independent evidence of every early political event.

### 12.3 Neem Ka Thana conference, 1944

A major Meena gathering at **Neem Ka Thana** is commonly dated to **April 1944**. Study materials often cite around **50,000 attendees** and associate the meeting with Muni Magan Sagar, Bansidhar Sharma and other organisers. The precise attendance figure and the names/titles of every office-holder are not established from a primary attendance record in the sources reviewed. Retain the meeting’s role in organised mobilisation; treat the round number as an estimate/convention.

### 12.4 Baghawas and *Mukti Diwas*, 28 October 1946

Community and examination accounts commonly mark **28 October 1946** at **Baghawas/Bagawas** as *Mukti Diwas*. The story is that a large number of Chaukidari Meenas resigned collectively from hereditary watchman duties; a figure of **26,000** is widely repeated.

This event is important as a form of collective civil disobedience: it turned a dispersed grievance into an unmistakable public refusal to continue a coercive role. The exact figure should be labelled a reported estimate, not a verified headcount. The supplied draft’s claim that this immediately forced the state to exempt all women and children from attendance was not securely established and has been removed as a definitive cause-and-effect statement.

## 15. Frequently confused facts

1. **Govind Guru’s background:** commonly described as Banjara by birth; he led a movement strongly associated with Bhils. Do not automatically label him Bhil by birth.
2. **Samp Sabha:** 1883 is the usual exam date; Sirohi is the common exam location. The exact foundation venue should be treated as an exam convention when a primary source is absent.
3. **Mangarh date:** 17 November 1913.
4. **Mangarh casualties:** about 1,500 is the commonly repeated government estimate; not an audited total.
5. **Mangarh vs Jallianwala:** Mangarh occurred in 1913, nearly six years before Jallianwala Bagh in 1919. “Jallianwala Bagh of Rajasthan” is a commemorative comparison.
6. **Mewar Bhil Corps:** raised in 1841; Kherwara headquarters, Kotra detachment; not funded entirely by Mewar.
7. **1881 Bhil uprising:** Rikhabdeo negotiations concerned multiple grievances. A reported concession was half-*barar* remission and a pause in census work—not a verified universal 50% land-revenue cut.
8. **Eki movement:** developed in 1920–21; Matrikundiya is the key mobilisation site.
9. **Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav:** 7 March 1922 firing in former Idar State, now Gujarat. Some Rajasthan educational material calls the associated assembly “Neemda/Neemra.” State both labels with the geography caveat.
10. **Major Sutton:** commonly named in accounts of the Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav firing; do not confuse him with a fully verified command roster for Mangarh.
11. **Eki casualty figures:** commonly cited as about 1,200, with markedly different estimates in colonial and later accounts.
12. **Mewar Ki Pukar:** the 21-point charter is the common exam fact. The exact list of three rejected demands is not consistent in secondary material.
13. **Sirohi-region repression:** do not equate “1,800 houses burnt,” “1,800 people killed” and “1,800 dwellings” as if the figures refer to the same event. Sources vary on names and totals.
14. **Meena law chronology:** 1871 original CTA; 1924 consolidated central act and commonly cited application in Rajputana; Jaipur State’s Jairam/Jarayam Pesha regime around 1930; repeal of the 1924 act and corresponding laws effective 31 August 1952.
15. **Jairam Pesha attendance:** source versions conflict on age thresholds and the categories subject to daily attendance. Do not combine incompatible versions into one “exact rule.”
16. **Baghawas:** 28 October 1946 is the common date for *Mukti Diwas*; 26,000 is a reported figure, not a verified count.
17. **Muni Magan Sagar:** *Meena Puran* should be understood as part of community identity and reform history, not as independent proof of every genealogical claim.
18. **Popular titles:** “Bavji,” “Gandhi of the tribals,” and “Jallianwala Bagh of Rajasthan” are popular/commemorative expressions, not formal legal or organisational titles.

## 16. Short chronology to revise from memory

- **1818:** Mewar–British political settlement; Bhil resistance follows and continues through the 1820s.
- **1841:** Mewar Bhil Corps raised.
- **1881:** Bhil uprising and Rikhabdeo negotiations; further unrest in 1882.
- **1883:** Samp Sabha associated with Govind Guru (standard exam date).
- **17 November 1913:** Mangarh firing/massacre.
- **1920–21:** Eki movement develops; Matrikundiya becomes a key mobilisation site.
- **7 March 1922:** Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav firing; also called Neemda/Neemra in some Rajasthan material.
- **1924:** Criminal Tribes Act, 1924; Meena classification in Rajputana is commonly dated to this period in historical scholarship.
- **1930:** Jaipur State’s Jairam/Jarayam Pesha regime (standard exam date).
- **1933:** Meena association-building phase (name varies by source).
- **April 1944:** Neem Ka Thana Meena conference (common date).
- **28 October 1946:** Baghawas *Mukti Diwas* in community/exam accounts.
- **31 August 1952:** effective repeal date under the Criminal Tribes Laws (Repeal) Act, 1952, for the 1924 act and corresponding laws unless an earlier date was notified.

**Answer: B.** Historical gazetteer-derived accounts describe shared financial responsibility, not complete funding by one authority.

A. Dabi, Bundi  
B. Govindpura, Begun

**Answer: B.** Learn the date and significance; treat the headline count cautiously.

# PART VII — MAINS ANSWER FRAMEWORKS

## 17. Five-mark framework: Explain the significance of Govind Guru and the Bhagat movement.

**Suggested answer structure**

Govind Guru’s Bhagat movement helped turn scattered Bhil communities into a more organised social and political network in the Vagad–Mewar border region. It promoted temperance, ethical conduct, collective worship through *dhunis*, and resistance to exploitation. Its opposition to *begar* and arbitrary exactions connected religious reform to material grievances. The Mangarh firing of 17 November 1913 became a powerful symbol of colonial-princely repression. Its importance lies not only in the reported casualty estimate but also in the movement’s role in building collective identity and making tribal grievances visible in regional political history.

**Evidence discipline:** Use “approximately 1,500 in commonly cited government accounts,” not “exactly 1,500 confirmed deaths.”

## 18. Five-mark framework: Explain the objectives and trajectory of the Eki movement.

The Eki movement developed in 1920–21 under Motilal Tejawat and mobilised Bhils and Garasias around a shared rejection of exploitative revenue demands, cesses, *begar*, and restrictions on customary resources. Matrikundiya became a key mobilisation site, and *Mewar Ki Pukar* is commonly described as a 21-point charter. The firing of 7 March 1922 at Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav, in former Idar State (now Gujarat), was a defining act of repression. The death toll is contested: colonial accounts gave a far lower figure than later local memory and historical reporting. The movement shows both the strength of cross-border tribal mobilisation and the limits of a freedom-struggle narrative that relies only on contemporary press coverage.

## 19. Ten-mark framework: Compare the Bhagat and Eki movements.

| Dimension | Bhagat movement | Eki movement |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | Govind Guru | Motilal Tejawat |
| Main organisational form | Samp Sabha, dhunis, common moral discipline | Unity oath, mass meetings, demand charter |
| Primary emphasis | Social-religious reform with political-economic implications | Agrarian and political non-cooperation |
| Shared grievances | Begar, exactions, unequal state–community relations | Begar, cesses, revenue demands, forest/grazing restrictions |
| Major repression | Mangarh, 17 Nov 1913 | Pal-Chitariya/Dadhvav, 7 Mar 1922; subsequent borderland repression |
| Historiographical caution | Casualty estimates and exact command details | Place-name variations, casualty dispute and uneven national press coverage |

**Conclusion:** The movements differed in leadership and tactics, but both show how collective identity, local grievances and organised refusal could challenge feudal-colonial power. Their histories must be reconstructed from state records, later scholarship and community memory together.

## 20. Ten-mark framework: Evaluate the Meena movement against criminalisation.

The Meena movement emerged against discriminatory classification, compulsory registration/attendance, police surveillance and social stigma. It combined social reform organisations, community-history writing, conferences and collective protest. The 1930 Jaipur Jairam/Jarayam Pesha regime is a key exam landmark, but exact eligibility and attendance rules vary across secondary accounts and should not be overspecified without the original regulation. The Neem Ka Thana conference of 1944 and the Baghawas event of 28 October 1946 are commonly cited as milestones in organisation and collective refusal by Chaukidari Meenas. The Criminal Tribes Laws (Repeal) Act, 1952, effective for repeal on 31 August 1952 unless an earlier date was notified, marked a decisive legal break. However, denotification did not instantly eradicate prejudice or every form of coercive surveillance.
