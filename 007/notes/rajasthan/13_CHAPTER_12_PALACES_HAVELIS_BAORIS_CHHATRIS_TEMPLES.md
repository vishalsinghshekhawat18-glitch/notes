# Chapter 12 — Palaces, Havelis, Baoris, Chhatris and Temples of Rajasthan

**For:** RPSC RAS Prelims and Mains; Rajasthan state competitive examinations  
**Revision status:** Rebuilt after factual and source-quality audit  
**Coverage:** Royal palaces; mercantile havelis and fresco traditions; stepwells and water systems; chhatris and royal memorial grounds; temple architecture and major shrines; comparison tables, examination traps and practice questions.

> **How to read this chapter:** A monument’s location, broad date, patron, material and visible architectural features are usually safer to memorise than a tourist superlative, a precise count from an unattributed source, or a dramatic story preserved only in local tradition. This chapter distinguishes **well-supported baseline facts**, **common exam conventions**, and **claims to qualify**.

## 1. A framework for remembering Rajasthan’s built heritage

Rajasthan’s architecture developed in response to climate, terrain, political power, religion, commerce and social organisation. Its monuments should not be learned as an unconnected list of names.

| Building type | Main function | Typical architectural clues | Useful examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fort-palace / royal palace** | Defence, residence, court, administration and ceremony | Enclosing walls, courtyards, audience halls, screened balconies, pavilions and gardens | City Palace, Udaipur; City Palace, Jaipur; Umaid Bhawan, Jodhpur |
| **Haveli** | Large urban or merchant residence; sometimes office and commercial centre | Inward-facing courtyards, carved facades, jharokhas, jalis, painted walls and family wings | Patwon ki Haveli, Jaisalmer; Nathmal ki Haveli, Jaisalmer; Shekhawati havelis |
| **Baori / bawdi / vav** | Access to groundwater and dependable water storage | Long descending stairs, a well shaft, landings, galleries and sometimes a pillared pavilion | Chand Baori, Abhaneri; Raniji ki Baori, Bundi |
| **Kund / jhalra / tanka / khadin** | Water collection, storage, ritual use or agricultural runoff management | Open stepped tank, seepage-fed reservoir, covered cistern or runoff-catching embankment | Panna Meena ka Kund; Mahila Bagh Jhalra; desert tank and khadin systems |
| **Chhatri / deval / cenotaph** | Memorial to a deceased ruler, noble, warrior or revered person | Pillared pavilion, dome, memorial slab or commemorative sculpture | 84-Pillared Cenotaph, Bundi; Moosi Maharani ki Chhatri, Alwar; Jaswant Thada, Jodhpur |
| **Temple** | Worship, pilgrimage, ritual and patronage of sculpture, painting and learning | Garbhagriha, mandapa, shikhara or other superstructure, sculpted doorways and iconographic programme | Osian, Kiradu, Dilwara, Ranakpur, Jagdish Temple |

### Essential vocabulary

- **Garbhagriha:** The sanctum housing the principal deity or image.
- **Mandapa:** A pillared hall used for gathering, worship or movement toward the sanctum.
- **Shikhara:** The superstructure over a North Indian temple sanctum.
- **Jagati:** A raised platform or plinth.
- **Jharokha:** A projecting enclosed or semi-enclosed balcony/window.
- **Jali:** A perforated screen, often stone-carved, that permits light and air while limiting direct visibility.
- **Chhatri:** Literally an umbrella-like canopy; in Rajasthan, commonly a domed memorial pavilion.
- **Baori / bawdi:** A stepped well that allows access to water as the water level changes.
- **Jhalra:** A stepped water tank, often supplied by seepage or linked to a larger water system; local usage varies.
- **Khadin:** An agricultural runoff-harvesting system, especially associated with the arid western desert region.
- **Tanka:** A covered cistern or tank for storing rainwater, often at household or settlement scale.

**Important distinction:** These terms overlap in popular speech and local usage. Do not assume every open stepped tank is technically a baori, or that a khadin is just another kind of well.

## 2. Royal palaces: architecture, patronage and urban setting

Rajasthani palaces are often complexes rather than single buildings. They combine ceremonial and private spaces, defensive architecture, water storage, court administration and landscape design. Mughal and later European influences appear alongside local forms; describing them as wholly isolated from other architectural traditions is inaccurate.

### 2.1 City Palace, Udaipur

- **Location:** Eastern side of Lake Pichola, Udaipur.
- **Patronage:** The foundation of the palace complex is associated with Maharana Udai Singh II in **1559 CE**; later rulers repeatedly expanded it. Udaipur’s foundation date is given as 1553 or 1559 in different tourism and historical summaries, so for exams keep **1559 as the conventional palace-foundation date** and distinguish it from the city’s broader founding chronology.
- **Form:** A large, multi-courtyard palace complex built on a ridge, combining fort-like walls, terraces, balconies, courtyards, apartments and lake-facing views.
- **Major features:** Badi Pol and Tripolia Pol; Manek Chowk; Zenana Mahal; Moti Mahal; Sheesh Mahal; Mor Chowk; and Chini Chitrashala.
- **Mor Chowk:** Noted for three peacock mosaics with intricate coloured-glass inlay. The peacock court is one of the most recognisable examples of Mewar decorative craftsmanship.
- **Badi Mahal:** A garden and palace space located at the upper level of the complex. The dramatic effect comes from its elevated natural-rock setting and relationship to the surrounding palace rooms.
- **Tripolia and weighing ceremonies:** The entrance area is associated with royal ceremonial movement and traditional weighing or *tuladan* accounts.
- **Interpretation:** The palace is a record of successive additions, not a single design completed by one ruler. Its forms show Mewar traditions together with changing courtly and regional influences.

**Qualification:** The phrase **“Windsor of Rajasthan”** is sometimes attributed to James Fergusson in exam-oriented material. The original chapter supplied an exact page reference, but that quotation should not be treated as verified without checking the cited edition and page. Do not confuse a popular epithet with an inscriptional fact.

Official references: [City Palace Museum, Udaipur](https://citypalacemuseum.org/planyourvisit); [Incredible India — City Palace, Udaipur](https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/rajasthan/udaipur/city-palace); [Rajasthan Tourism — Udaipur](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/udaipur.html).

### 2.2 Jag Mandir, Jag Niwas and Sajjangarh

| Monument | Location and patronage | High-yield features | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jag Mandir** (Lake Garden Palace) | Island in Lake Pichola. Construction began under Maharana Karan Singh around **1620** and continued under Jagat Singh I; the complex is commonly dated to completion around **1652**. | Island palace, sculpted stone elephants and pavilions; traditionally associated with shelter given to Prince Khurram (later Shah Jahan) during his revolt against Jahangir in 1623–24. | The popular story that Jag Mandir directly inspired the Taj Mahal should be presented as a possible influence/tradition, not a proven single-source design claim. |
| **Jag Niwas / Lake Palace** | Island in Lake Pichola, built by Maharana Jagat Singh II around **1743–46**. | White-marble palace with courtyards, terraces and lake-facing rooms; later adapted as a heritage hotel. | Not the same monument as Jag Mandir. |
| **Sajjangarh / Monsoon Palace** | Bansdara hill above Udaipur; associated with Maharana Sajjan Singh in the late 19th century. | Elevated skyline landmark and monsoon retreat; Sajjan Singh reportedly conceived an astronomical observatory, but the plan was not completed as intended. | Avoid claiming it was completed entirely during Sajjan Singh’s lifetime. |

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Udaipur](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/udaipur.html).

### 2.3 City Palace, Jaipur

- **Context:** Developed after Sawai Jai Singh II founded Jaipur in 1727 and shifted the Kachhwaha capital from Amber.
- **Planner:** Vidyadhar Bhattacharya is traditionally associated with Jaipur’s plan and the palace-city design. The complex developed over time, with additions by later rulers.
- **Chandra Mahal:** A seven-storeyed palace within the larger City Palace complex and a continuing royal residence.
- **Mubarak Mahal:** A late 19th-century building associated with Sawai Madho Singh II and architect Sir Samuel Swinton Jacob. Its design is commonly described as Indo-Saracenic, combining Indian, Mughal and European elements.
- **Pritam Niwas Chowk:** Famous for four painted gates. The usual symbolic associations are:
  - **Peacock Gate:** autumn and Vishnu;
  - **Lotus Gate:** summer and Shiva–Parvati;
  - **Green / Lehariya Gate:** spring and Ganesha;
  - **Rose Gate:** winter and Devi/Durga.
  The iconography and seasonal labels are a common visitor and exam convention; retain them as an aid to recognition, not as the primary architectural significance of the complex.
- **Gangajalis:** Two exceptionally large silver vessels traditionally associated with Sawai Madho Singh II’s desire to take Ganga water on his 1902 journey to England for Edward VII’s coronation. Commonly repeated details include their large capacity, weight and manufacture from silver coinage. The exact coin count and measurements should be memorised only if required by a current exam source; avoid treating every popular figure as equally well evidenced.

Official reference: [City Palace, Jaipur](https://citypalace.org/).

### 2.4 Hawa Mahal, Jaipur

- **Date and patron:** Built in **1799** under Sawai Pratap Singh.
- **Architect:** Ustad Lal Chand Ustad is the conventional attribution.
- **Form:** Five-storeyed, tapering street façade with numerous small windows, balconies and perforated screens. The distinctive façade is often said to resemble Krishna’s crown, consistent with the ruler’s devotion to Krishna.
- **Function:** Royal women could observe street processions and public life while maintaining *purdah*.
- **Ventilation:** The many jharokhas and jalis improve air movement and shade. Describe it as an example of **passive climate-responsive design**, not as a mechanical refrigeration system or a scientifically proven “adiabatic cooling loop.”
- **953 openings:** This number is widely quoted by official tourism and exam sources. It is a useful conventional fact, but “jharokha,” “window” and “opening” are not always counted in identical ways.

Official reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Hawa Mahal](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/hawa-mahal.html).

### 2.5 Other important palaces

| Palace | Location | Patronage / period | Important associations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Umaid Bhawan Palace** | Jodhpur | Begun in **1929**, associated with Maharaja Umaid Singh; architect Henry Vaughan Lanchester; construction continued into the 1940s. | Built partly as drought-relief employment; known as Chittar Palace for its use of local Chittar sandstone; combines Art Deco and European classical design with local elements. Exact “room/suite” counts vary by source and are not essential architectural facts. |
| **Lalgarh Palace** | Bikaner | Commissioned by Maharaja Ganga Singh in the early 20th century; designed by Sir Samuel Swinton Jacob. | Red Dulmera sandstone, Indo-Saracenic character and European-influenced details; associated with Maharaja Ganga Singh’s modernising court. |
| **Deeg Palace complex** | Deeg, Bharatpur region | Developed under Badan Singh and Suraj Mal during the 18th century. | Garden pavilions, water channels and fountain systems; Gopal Bhavan, Suraj Bhavan and Sawan-Bhadon are notable. “500+ fountains” appears in some descriptions but should not be treated as a fixed count without a site inventory. |
| **Jal Mahal** | Man Sagar Lake, Jaipur | Associated with the Jaipur rulers; often linked with Sawai Jai Singh II and later restoration. | Five-storey lake palace, with lower levels submerged when water is high. Attribution of every construction phase to one ruler is too simple. |

| **Kota’s Jagmandir** | Kishore Sagar, Kota | Commonly associated with Queen Braj Kanwar in the 18th century. | Island pavilion set within a tank; do not confuse with Jag Mandir of Udaipur. |

## 3. Havelis: mercantile life and regional craftsmanship

Havelis were substantial domestic complexes commissioned by merchants, bankers, ministers and nobles. Their architecture often combines a street-facing display façade with inward-facing courtyards, private rooms, storage, reception spaces and family or business functions. In desert settlements, shaded courtyards, thick walls, stone screens and carefully placed openings also responded to heat, dust and privacy.

### 3.1 Jaisalmer’s carved sandstone havelis

#### Patwon ki Haveli

- **Location:** Jaisalmer.
- **Patronage:** Guman Chand Patwa commissioned the first mansion around **1805**; the broader complex developed through separate mansions over a longer period.
- **Form and material:** A group of five closely connected yellow-sandstone havelis with ornate carved balconies, brackets, arches and screens.
- **Exam association:** Think **Patwa family + five-mansion cluster + yellow sandstone + early 19th-century mercantile wealth**.
- **Caution:** Do not describe every wing as having been completed in 1805. The first mansion and the later complex have different construction histories.

References: [Incredible India — Patwon ki Haveli](https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/rajasthan/jaisalmer/patwon-ki-haveli); [Rajasthan Tourism — Jaisalmer](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/jaisalmer.html).

#### Salim Singh ki Haveli

- **Location:** Jaisalmer.
- **Period:** First half of the 18th century is the safer general dating; the precise year **1815** in the original chapter is not supported by the Rajasthan Tourism page used for cross-checking.
- **Feature:** Narrower lower façade and a dramatically projecting, arched upper portion supported by carved brackets, some in peacock forms.
- **“Jahaz Mahal” story:** The ship-like appearance and story that two additional wooden storeys were dismantled by the ruler are familiar local narratives. Attribute the story as tradition rather than verified administrative history.

#### Nathmal ki Haveli

- **Location:** Jaisalmer.
- **Patronage:** Built in the 19th century for Diwan Nathmal; Hathi and Lalu, two mason brothers, are traditionally associated with work on opposite wings.
- **Feature:** Yellow sandstone, sculpted elephants, carved jharokhas and a highly ornamented façade. The two sides are broadly harmonious but contain variations.
- **Local narrative:** The claim that each brother worked without seeing or consulting the other’s work is a popular story, not a securely demonstrated construction record.
- **Dating caution:** The original’s exact **1885** date and precise claims about particular machines carved from second-hand descriptions need better documentary support; use **19th century** unless the exam source specifically expects the date.

### 3.2 Shekhawati’s painted havelis

Shekhawati—across the districts of Jhunjhunu, Sikar and Churu—is noted for merchant havelis, frescoed temples, chhatris, dharamshalas, bazaars, wells and fortified settlement cores. Its built environment links architecture to overland trade, merchant migration, regional patronage and adaptation to a semi-arid climate.

**Current UNESCO status:** In **June 2026**, *The Painted Haveli Towns of Shekhawati* was added to India’s **UNESCO Tentative List**. A Tentative List entry is a step in the nomination process; it does **not** mean the property has been inscribed on the World Heritage List. The proposed serial nomination identifies ensembles in fourteen towns and describes the integration of havelis, bazaars, forts, temples, chhatris and water systems. [UNESCO Tentative List entry](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/7028/).

#### Regional clusters

| Town / cluster | Important examples | Recognition cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Nawalgarh** | Anandilal Poddar Haveli; Chokhani and other merchant havelis | Dense concentration of painted merchant residences. |
| **Mandawa** | Gulab Rai Ladia Haveli; Binsidhar Newatia Haveli; Goenka and Saraf havelis | Murals depicting mythology alongside colonial-period objects and technologies. |
| **Fatehpur** | Nandlal Singhania Haveli; Goenka and Saraogi havelis | Historic settlement with painted havelis and conservation projects. |
| **Ramgarh** | Tarachand Rathi and other merchant havelis; painted chhatris | Merchant-sponsored townscape with havelis, temples and water features. |
| **Churu** | Surana, Mantri and other havelis | Painted façades; “1,111 doors and windows” is a popular numerical claim for Surana Haveli, not a number to assert without qualification. |
| **Mahansar, Bissau, Alsisar, Dundlod** | Havelis, forts, temples and painted memorial structures | The broader regional ensemble matters, not one building alone. |

#### Fresco and mural techniques

- **Wet-plaster / true fresco approach:** Pigment is applied while lime plaster is still wet. As lime carbonates during drying, pigments can become integrated with the plaster surface. The exact process varies by workshop, surface and period.
- **Dry-plaster / secco approach:** Pigment is applied to a dry surface, usually with a binding medium; attachment and durability differ from a wet-plaster method.
- **Arayash / ala-gila:** Terms used in regional accounts of lime-based plaster and mural preparation. Their usage is not always technically uniform, so avoid assuming that every Shekhawati painting used one identical recipe.
- **Subject matter:** Early imagery often includes religious narratives, local legends and courtly scenes. Later murals also show railway trains, motorcars, aeroplanes, gramophones, telephones and colonial figures. The phases overlap; they are not a rigid, universal chronology.

#### Other havelis

- **Rampuria Havelis, Bikaner:** Merchant-sponsored complexes, often associated with red Dulmera sandstone and an eclectic blend of regional, Mughal and European details.
- **Bagore ki Haveli, Udaipur:** Late 18th-century mansion near Gangaur Ghat, associated with Mewar’s prime minister Amarchand Barwa; now used for cultural display and museum functions.
- **Kota havelis:** Some retain murals and decorative schemes connected to the Kota school of painting and courtly themes.
- **Pushkarna Brahmin houses, Jodhpur:** Commonly noted for narrow street façades and internal courtyards; treat as a regional house type rather than one discrete monument.

## 4. Water architecture: baoris, kunds, jhalras and tanks

Water architecture in Rajasthan is a response to seasonal rainfall, groundwater access, evaporation, settlement density and ritual practice. Baoris also provided shaded gathering spaces and could express the patronage of rulers, queens, merchants or religious communities. “Water structure” is not one uniform category: a well, a stepped tank, a covered cistern and a field-runoff system work differently.

### 4.1 Chand Baori, Abhaneri

- **Location:** Abhaneri, Dausa district.
- **Dating and patronage:** Conventionally dated to the **8th–9th century** and associated with King Chanda of the Nikumbha lineage.
- **Form:** A deep, approximately thirteen-tiered stepwell with a highly regular pattern of descending steps on three sides and a multi-storeyed pavilion on the fourth.
- **Step count:** Rajasthan Tourism quotes approximately **3,500 symmetrical steps**.
- **Function:** Water access, water conservation and a shaded communal space. The adjoining Harshat Mata Temple reinforces the settlement’s religious and social setting.
- **Qualification:** Avoid calling it, without qualification, “the deepest stepwell in the world.” Depth figures differ between descriptions, and the superlative is not a standard UNESCO-style comparative ranking.

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Chand Baori](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/chand-baori.html).

### 4.2 Raniji ki Baori, Bundi

- **Patronage and date:** Constructed in **1699** by Rani Nathavati Ji, the younger queen of Maharao Raja Anirudh Singh of Bundi.
- **Dimensions:** Rajasthan Tourism describes it as **46 metres deep** and the largest of Bundi’s many baoris.
- **Architecture:** Multi-storeyed stepped construction, a high-arched entrance, sculpted pillars and elephant figures; shrines and decorated niches accompany the descent.
- **Function:** Water access in drought-prone conditions and a community gathering place.
- **Exam cue:** **Bundi + Rani Nathavati + 1699 + 46 m**.

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Raniji ki Baori](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/raniji-ki-baori.html).

### 4.3 Other water monuments and systems

| Structure | Location | What to remember | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Panna Meena ka Kund** | Amber, Jaipur | Symmetrical crisscrossing stairways and corner pavilions; a prominent example of a stepped tank. | Do not invent a founding date or named patron without a reliable local record. |
| **Neemrana Baori** | Neemrana area | Large, multi-storeyed stepwell, often described as having nine levels. | Precise date and structural labels vary across visitor sources; focus on form and location. |
| **Hadi Rani ki Baori** | Todaraisingh, Tonk | Important stepped water structure in the Tonk region. | Verify any exact date/patron attribution separately before memorising. |
| **Mahila Bagh Jhalra** | Jodhpur | A stepped reservoir associated with the urban water system. | Distinguish a jhalra from a deep shaft-style stepwell. |
| **Naulakha Baori** | Dungarpur | Regional historic baori. | The original’s exact 1586 date and Rani Premal Devi attribution require a local inscription or archaeology reference. |
| **Gadsisar / Gadisar Lake** | Jaisalmer | Historic man-made water reservoir associated with Rawal Gadsi Singh; Tilon ki Pol is a notable entrance. | Date often given as 14th century; distinguish original construction from later additions. |
| **Bataadu ka Kuan** | Baytu, Barmer region | Frequently called the “Water Palace of the Thar” in popular accounts; white-marble appearance is its distinctive feature. | This epithet is a local/tourism label; exact patron and construction details need local documentation. |
| **Khadin** | Western Rajasthan, particularly Jaisalmer region | Earthen embankment captures runoff for soil moisture and post-monsoon cultivation. | It is an agricultural runoff system, not a baori or covered cistern. |
| **Tanka** | Arid settlements of western Rajasthan | Covered tank/cistern stores rainwater. | Household, community and settlement-scale forms vary. |

### 4.4 How to compare water structures in an answer

A strong Mains answer should compare **hydrology, design and social function**:

1. **Hydrology:** groundwater access, rainwater storage, seepage or captured runoff;
2. **Design:** descending shaft, open stepped tank, covered cistern or field embankment;
3. **Climate response:** shaded descent, thick masonry, reduced direct exposure and storage through dry months;
4. **Social setting:** community access, patronage, ritual uses and the status of the donor;
5. **Conservation:** groundwater and water quality, silt, structural stability and modern reuse.

Avoid reducing all baoris to “women’s gathering places” or claiming that one modern hydrological benefit was the sole historical purpose of every monument.

## 5. Chhatris and royal memorial architecture

A **chhatri** is a pillared canopy, often domed, that may serve as a funerary monument or memorial. Related Rajasthan forms include **deval**, **samadhi**, hero stones and royal cremation-ground monuments. The word “cenotaph” is used for a memorial that does not necessarily contain the deceased’s remains; local usage is not always identical to the strict architectural definition.

### 5.1 Major royal memorial landscapes

| Site | Dynasty / location | Recognition cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Ahar / Mahasatiyan** | Mewar rulers, Udaipur | Royal cremation-ground complex with many cenotaphs; an architectural record of successive Mewar rulers. |
| **Gaitore** | Jaipur Kachhwaha rulers | Marble and stone cenotaphs, including that of Sawai Jai Singh II. Do not assume every Jaipur ruler’s memorial is at Gaitore; Sawai Ishwari Singh is commonly associated with Talkatora/Jai Niwas Bagh. |
| **Mandore** | Marwar / Jodhpur Rathores | Historic devals and memorial structures, including the important group associated with Marwar rulers. |
| **Jaswant Thada** | Jodhpur | Built by Maharaja Sardar Singh in memory of Maharaja Jaswant Singh II; fine white marble gives the complex its popular “Taj of Marwar” epithet. |
| **Devkund Sagar** | Bikaner | Royal cremation complex with cenotaphs of Bikaner rulers. |
| **Bada Bagh** | Jaisalmer | Desert-edge garden landscape with royal cenotaphs, particularly associated with Jaisalmer’s Bhati rulers. |
| **Kshar Bagh** | Bundi | Cenotaph group of Hada rulers. |
| **Chhatra Vilas Garden** | Kota | Memorials associated with Kota’s rulers. |

### 5.2 Specific chhatris

#### 84-Pillared Cenotaph, Bundi

- **Patronage:** Commissioned by Rao Raja Aniruddha Singh of Bundi in memory of Deva, his beloved wet nurse, according to Rajasthan Tourism.
- **Feature:** Two-storeyed structure supported by 84 pillars, with sculpted details and a Shiva lingam in the lower level.
- **Exam cue:** **Bundi + Rao Aniruddha + Deva + 84 pillars**.
- **Correction:** The original chapter described Deva as a foster-brother. The official tourism source identifies Deva as the ruler’s wet nurse; use the latter formulation unless another reliable record establishes otherwise.

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Bundi](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/bundi.html).

#### Moosi Maharani ki Chhatri, Alwar

- **Location:** Near Alwar City Palace and Sagar reservoir.
- **Form:** Two-level memorial structure combining red sandstone below with white marble above; columned pavilions and domes frame painted and sculptural detail.
- **Association:** Dedicated to Maharaja Bakhtawar Singh and Moosi Rani in local and tourism accounts.
- **Qualification:** Details relating to sati and an exact construction date should be handled sensitively and attributed to historical/traditional accounts, rather than presented as an architectural measurement.

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Alwar](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/alwar.html).

#### Jaswant Thada, Jodhpur

- **Patron and date:** Commissioned by Maharaja Sardar Singh in **1899** in memory of Jaswant Singh II.
- **Material:** Fine white marble, including thin, carved surfaces that appear to glow in sunlight.
- **Exam label:** “Taj Mahal of Marwar” is a conventional epithet, not a formal heritage classification.

### 5.3 Other memorials and the need for care

- **Cenotaph of Maharana Pratap:** Commonly associated with Bandoli near Chavand in the Udaipur region.
- **Chetak memorial:** A commemorative site associated with Maharana Pratap’s horse at Balicha/Haldighati region; popular tradition shapes the story.
- **Ranthambore and Mandalgarh 32-pillared chhatris:** The original source mixes different structures and attributions. Before memorising a precise “32-pillar” patron or function, verify which monument is intended.
- **Chhatris of warrior-saints and local heroes:** A name may refer to a cenotaph, shrine or later memorial structure. Location and tradition should be checked individually.

## 6. Temple architecture in Rajasthan

Rajasthan’s temples encompass early post-Gupta and Gurjara-Pratihara structures, later regional Nagara forms, Jain marble architecture, Vaishnava *haveli*-style worship spaces and local shrines. The labels **Maha-Maru** and **Maru-Gurjara** are useful in art-history writing, but they should not be treated as perfectly rigid chronological boxes used uniformly by all scholars.

### 6.1 Temple elements and stylistic language

- **Nagara:** Broad North Indian temple tradition, typically associated with a curvilinear *shikhara* above the sanctum.
- **Maha-Maru:** A term used in art-historical discussion for early medieval western Indian temple forms, especially those associated with the Gurjara-Pratiharas and related regional traditions.
- **Maru-Gurjara:** A later western Indian idiom strongly associated with intricate surface carving, articulated temple parts, complex ceilings and marble craftsmanship, particularly visible in Jain temples.
- **Panchayatana:** A plan in which a principal shrine is accompanied by four subsidiary shrines around it; usage and exact layout vary.
- **Torana:** An ornamental gateway or arch-like entrance element.

Temple styles should be identified through **plan, superstructure, material, carving, inscription and patronage**, not solely by a tourist nickname such as “Khajuraho of Rajasthan.”

### 6.2 Sheetleshwar Mahadev, Jhalrapatan

- **Location:** Jhalrapatan, Jhalawar district, near the Chandrabhaga river.
- **Inscription:** Widely cited for a Sanskrit inscription dated **Vikram Samvat 746 / 689 CE**, associated with ruler Durgagana and artisan Voppa.
- **Importance:** Often described in Rajasthan exam literature as the state’s earliest dated temple. A safer formulation is **“one of the earliest dated surviving temples in Rajasthan, known for its early inscription”** unless the question specifically requires the conventional label.
- **Style:** Early Nagara features and an important point in the study of early medieval temple development.

**Source caution:** Exact wording such as “officially the first dated temple in Rajasthan” should ideally be cross-checked against the inscription publication or ASI catalogue. The original source named institutional records but did not provide a direct document identifier.

### 6.3 Osian, Jodhpur region

- **Date and setting:** An important early medieval temple centre with Hindu and Jain temples, broadly spanning the 8th–11th centuries.
- **Mahavira Temple:** A key Jain shrine known for a richly carved doorway, sculpture and torana-like architectural detail.
- **Sachiya Mata Temple:** Important pilgrimage shrine whose architectural phases should be understood as cumulative rather than as one single date.
- **Why it matters:** Osian is useful for studying regional temple patronage and the development of early medieval western Indian temple architecture.
- **Qualification:** Claims such as “oldest surviving dated Jain temple in western India” are broad superlatives and should not be repeated without a precise scholarly citation.

### 6.4 Kiradu temples, Barmer

- **Location:** Near Barmer; a group of five important temples.
- **Style:** Commonly described in Rajasthan tourism material as Solanki-style architecture, with elaborate stone sculpture.
- **Someshwara Temple:** Generally considered the most prominent surviving temple in the group.
- **Exam epithet:** “Khajuraho of Rajasthan” is a familiar label, but the academically useful description is the sculptural richness and stylistic transition of the temples.
- **Dating:** Several sources associate an inscription dated Vikram Samvat 1218 / 1161 CE with the complex. Attribute exact dates to the relevant inscription or catalogue; do not assume all five temples were built simultaneously.

Reference: [Rajasthan Tourism — Barmer/Kiradu](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/barmer.html).

### 6.7 Ranakpur Chaumukha Jain Temple

- **Location:** Sadri area, Pali district, in an Aravalli valley.
- **Patronage:** Commissioned by the Jain merchant **Dharanashah/Dhanna Shah**, under the patronage of **Maharana Kumbha** of Mewar.
- **Date:** Commonly dated to **1439 CE** in exam literature; the Rajasthan Tourism account describes it as an early 15th-century structure and refers to the *Soma-Saubhagya Kavya* and a copper-plate inscription.
- **Dedication:** Adinatha, the first Jain Tirthankara.
- **Architect:** Depaka/Deepak is the commonly cited master architect.
- **Form:** Principal Chaumukha shrine has a four-faced image, signifying access to the deity from the four cardinal directions. The complex combines multiple shrines, halls, domes and carved marble columns.
- **Pillar count:** Rajasthan Tourism gives **1,444 intricately carved pillars**, with the familiar observation that their carving is not identical. It also describes 24 pillared halls and 80 domes. Different summaries vary on some architectural totals; for exam purposes, treat the 1,444-pillar count as the standard convention.
- **Interpretation:** The significance is not merely the number of columns. It lies in the integration of plan, carved marble, lighting, repeated but varied supports, ritual circulation and patronage.

References: [Rajasthan Tourism — Ranakpur Temple](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/content/rajasthan-tourism/en/tourist-destinations/ranakpur-temple.html); [Rajasthan Tourism Development Corporation — Rajasthan Darshan](https://rtdc.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/Client/PackageTourDetail.aspx?PackageTourid=Rajasthan+Darshan).

## 8. Twenty examination traps to avoid

1. **Hawa Mahal:** It is associated with Sawai Pratap Singh and Ustad Lal Chand Ustad, not Sawai Jai Singh II.
2. **Hawa Mahal’s purpose:** Its screened façade allowed royal women to observe street life; it was not simply a royal residence in the same sense as a large courtyard palace.
3. **Jaipur’s Pritam Niwas Chowk:** Distinguish it from Udaipur’s Mor Chowk.
4. **Jag Mandir vs Jag Niwas:** Both are island palaces on Lake Pichola, but have different patronage histories.
5. **Udaipur City Palace:** 1559 is the conventional foundation-stone/palace date; the city’s foundation date varies in summaries.
6. **Umaid Bhawan:** A 20th-century palace with drought-relief origins; do not place it in the Mughal era.
7. **Patwon ki Haveli:** Five-haveli cluster; 1805 refers to the first mansion’s commissioning in standard descriptions, not necessarily every building’s completion.
8. **Salim Singh ki Haveli:** Rajasthan Tourism dates it broadly to the first half of the 18th century; 1815 should not be treated as settled without another source.
9. **Nathmal ki Haveli:** Hathi and Lalu are the associated mason brothers; the “never saw each other’s work” story is traditional lore.
10. **Shekhawati UNESCO status:** As of June 2026 it is on the Tentative List, not the inscribed World Heritage List.
11. **Chand Baori:** Abhaneri, Dausa; approx. 3,500 steps. “Deepest in the world” is a popular superlative, not a universal ranking.
12. **Raniji ki Baori:** Bundi, 1699, Rani Nathavati Ji, 46 m depth—do not assign Chand Baori’s details to it.
13. **Khadin vs baori:** A khadin captures surface runoff for agriculture; it is not a stepped well.
14. **84-Pillared Cenotaph:** Bundi; Rao Aniruddha; dedicated to Deva, the ruler’s wet nurse in Rajasthan Tourism’s account—not a foster-brother.
15. **Moosi Maharani ki Chhatri:** Alwar; recognisable by red sandstone below and white marble above.
16. **Jaswant Thada:** Jodhpur; built by Sardar Singh in memory of Jaswant Singh II, completed in 1899 by common convention.
17. **Sheetleshwar:** The 689 CE / VS 746 inscription is important; phrase “oldest dated temple” cautiously unless citing the inscription catalogue.
18. **Dilwara:** Five Jain temples; Vimal Vasahi is associated with 1031 and Adinatha; Luna Vasahi with 1230 and Neminatha.
19. **Ranakpur:** Patron Dharanashah, royal patron Maharana Kumbha; principal deity Adinatha; 1,444 pillars is the common exam number.
20. **Jagdish Temple:** Udaipur, Vishnu, built in 1651 by Maharana Jagat Singh I according to Rajasthan Tourism.

## 10. Mains answer frameworks

### Q1. Explain the environmental and social importance of Rajasthan’s baoris and other water structures. (5 marks)

Rajasthan’s water architecture responded to seasonal rainfall and uncertain groundwater availability. Stepwells such as Chand Baori at Abhaneri and Raniji ki Baori at Bundi provided access to water across changing water levels. Their stairways, landings and shaded pavilions made them usable communal spaces. Other structures served distinct roles: tanks stored rainfall, jhalras collected water through seepage or urban linkages, tankas protected stored water from evaporation and contamination, and khadins retained runoff for cultivation. The design combined utility, patronage and sometimes religious decoration. Today, conservation must address structural damage, silt, water quality, urban pressure and loss of traditional catchments.

### Q2. Discuss the architectural and historical significance of Shekhawati’s havelis. (5 marks)

Shekhawati’s historic towns in Jhunjhunu, Sikar and Churu grew through local political authority, overland trade and merchant patronage. Their built fabric includes fortified cores, bazaars, inward-facing havelis, temples, chhatris, dharamshalas and water systems. From the 19th to early 20th centuries, merchant wealth funded richly painted façades and courtyards. Murals include mythology, folk narratives, portraits, daily life and colonial-era technologies, making the built landscape a visual record of social and economic change. Conservation requires maintaining not only isolated façades, but also the townscape, plaster and pigments, water infrastructure, courtyard morphology and relationships between homes and bazaars. The Painted Haveli Towns of Shekhawati entered UNESCO’s Tentative List in June 2026, a step toward possible future nomination rather than an inscription.

### Q3. Compare Rajasthan’s royal palaces and merchant havelis. (5 marks)

Royal palaces were centres of residence, administration, ceremony and political authority, often integrated with forts, gardens and lake settings. Examples include the City Palaces of Udaipur and Jaipur and Umaid Bhawan in Jodhpur. Merchant havelis were large family residences and sometimes commercial premises; their inward-facing courtyards balanced privacy and climate response, while their street façades communicated family wealth and status. Jaisalmer’s carved sandstone havelis and Shekhawati’s painted merchant mansions illustrate distinct regional expressions. Both forms reflect patronage and social hierarchy, but a palace is generally organised around courtly and state functions while a haveli centres on household, kinship and mercantile life.

### Q4. Describe the major features of the Ranakpur Chaumukha Jain Temple. (5 marks)

Ranakpur’s Chaumukha Temple in Pali district is a major Jain monument associated with merchant patron Dharanashah and the patronage of Maharana Kumbha. The principal shrine is dedicated to Adinatha and houses a four-faced image oriented toward the cardinal directions. The complex is constructed in carved marble and is known for its large number of intricately decorated pillars, commonly given as 1,444. Halls, domes, subsidiary shrines and patterned ceilings create a complex interior spatial sequence. Its significance lies in the relationship among Jain ritual, merchant patronage, royal support, marble workmanship and the organisation of light and circulation—not only in the number of pillars.

### Q5. What do chhatris reveal about Rajasthan’s memorial culture? (5 marks)

Chhatris and devals commemorated rulers, nobles, relatives, warriors or other persons whose memory mattered to a local community. Their forms range from open pillared canopies to large royal memorial groups at Ahar, Gaitore, Mandore, Devkund and Bada Bagh. Carved columns, domes, memorial slabs and painted ceilings express regional craftsmanship and political memory. Chhatris are useful historical sources but need to be distinguished from burial monuments, temples and later memorials. An answer should name the site, patronage and material features, then explain how memorial architecture expressed dynastic continuity, status and remembrance.

### Q6. Outline the development of temple architecture in Rajasthan. (10 marks)

Rajasthan’s temple architecture developed over many centuries through regional forms of the wider North Indian Nagara tradition. Early medieval centres such as Osian, Abhaneri, Jhalrapatan and Harshnath provide evidence for the growth of temple patronage, iconography and stone craftsmanship. The Kiradu group illustrates a later western Indian stylistic vocabulary, while the Dilwara Jain temples represent exceptionally refined marble carving and elaborate interior decoration. Ranakpur combines Jain ritual design with a vast marble-pillared space and complex patronage. Later and living temples—Eklingji, Jagdish, Shrinathji and others—show the continued relationship between worship, royal authority, sectarian traditions and local communities. The terms Maha-Maru and Maru-Gurjara can help structure the answer, but should not be used as rigid labels without reference to actual plans, sculpture, inscriptions and chronology.
