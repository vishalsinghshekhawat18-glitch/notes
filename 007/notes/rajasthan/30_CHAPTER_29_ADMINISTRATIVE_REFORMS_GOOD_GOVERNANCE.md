## 1. Good Governance (सुशासन): Concept and Framework

### 1.1 Meaning

Governance concerns how public authority is exercised, how decisions are made and implemented, and how public institutions interact with citizens. **Good governance** means that public power is exercised lawfully, transparently, responsively and effectively, with participation and accountability.

The World Bank’s 1992 report *Governance and Development* is associated with an influential definition of governance: the manner in which power is exercised in managing a country’s economic and social resources for development. It should **not** be cited as the source of the familiar “eight characteristics” list.

The widely used eight-characteristic framework is associated with the United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific (UNESCAP):

1. **Participation** — citizens and affected groups have meaningful opportunities to participate.
2. **Rule of law** — laws are fairly applied and basic rights are protected.
3. **Transparency** — decisions and relevant information are accessible and understandable.
4. **Responsiveness** — institutions respond to legitimate needs within a reasonable period.
5. **Consensus orientation** — competing interests are mediated towards workable decisions.
6. **Equity and inclusiveness** — all groups, especially vulnerable groups, have fair opportunities and access.
7. **Effectiveness and efficiency** — institutions achieve public objectives while using resources responsibly.
8. **Accountability** — decision-makers must explain and answer for their decisions and performance.

Some governance frameworks, including UNDP-oriented formulations, separately emphasize **strategic vision**. For RAS answers, state which framework is being used rather than treating all lists as identical.

### 1.2 Rajasthan examples: connect each principle to an institution

| Principle | Rajasthan / Indian example | What the example demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| Participation | Gram Sabha, social audit, public hearings | Citizens can scrutinize works and raise local issues. |
| Rule of law | Statutes, judicial review, anti-corruption mechanisms | Public decisions must be legally authorised and reviewable. |
| Transparency | RTI Act, 2005; Jan Soochna Portal | Public information can be accessed proactively or by request. |
| Responsiveness | Rajasthan Sampark / 181; Right to Hearing Act | A route exists for registering and pursuing grievances. |
| Consensus orientation | Consultation, mediation and deliberative processes | Conflicting interests can be heard and balanced. |
| Equity and inclusiveness | Reservations in local bodies; welfare schemes | Governance should include groups facing structural barriers. |
| Effectiveness and efficiency | e-Mitra, online applications, Jan Aadhaar-linked services | Digital and assisted channels can reduce transaction costs. |
| Accountability | Appellate mechanisms under service-delivery laws; social audit | An officer’s action or inaction can be challenged or scrutinised. |

**Constitutional connection:** “Good governance” is not a single, self-contained constitutional definition. Its values are reflected across constitutional guarantees and directives, including equality before law (Article 14), freedoms relevant to participation and expression (Article 19, subject to constitutional restrictions), life and personal liberty (Article 21), the Directive Principles, and constitutional provisions for local self-government. The Right to Information Act, 2005 and Rajasthan’s service/grievance laws provide statutory mechanisms supporting parts of this wider ideal.

### 1.3 Why good governance is more than e-governance

Digitisation can improve access, traceability and speed, but a portal alone does not guarantee a correct decision. Good governance also requires accurate records, accessible grievance channels, trained staff, reasons for rejection, protection of personal data, independent scrutiny and consequences for unjustified failure.

## 2. Citizen’s Charters (नागरिक चार्टर)

### 2.1 Origin and Indian adoption

- The **Citizen’s Charter initiative** was launched in the **United Kingdom in 1991** under Prime Minister **John Major** as a public-service quality and standards initiative.
- In India, the **Conference of Chief Ministers held in New Delhi on 24 May 1997**, presided over by Prime Minister **I. K. Gujral**, adopted an *Action Plan for Effective and Responsive Government*.
- One important decision was for Central and State Governments to formulate Citizen’s Charters, beginning with services having substantial public contact.

The chronology above is supported by the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances (DARPG) handbook on Citizen’s Charters.

### 2.2 Typical contents

A well-designed charter usually identifies:

1. the organisation’s vision, mission and principal services;
2. the users/beneficiaries and the service standards they can reasonably expect;
3. time limits and the documents/steps required from users;
4. the grievance-redressal channel and responsible officer;
5. the organisation’s commitments and the citizen’s responsibilities; and
6. review, feedback and improvement arrangements.

### 2.3 Limitation and relationship to statutory service guarantees

A Citizen’s Charter generally states an organisation’s service commitments. **The charter itself does not automatically create a statutory penalty or independent legal remedy** unless legislation, rules or another binding instrument gives it such effect. Statutory service-guarantee laws address this gap by specifying notified services, time limits, appeals and penalties.

**Exam distinction:** A charter states a service commitment; a service-guarantee statute creates a legally specified process for notified services. Do not claim that Rajasthan was India’s first state to enact a service-guarantee law: Madhya Pradesh’s Public Services Guarantee Act, 2010 preceded Rajasthan’s 2011 Act.

## 3. Rajasthan’s Rights-Based Accountability Timeline

| Date / period | Development | Safe interpretation for exams |
|---|---|---|
| 2000; brought into force in 2001 | Rajasthan Right to Information Act, 2000 (Act No. 13 of 2000) | An early state-level RTI enactment. Do not confuse it with the current central Right to Information Act, 2005 framework. |
| 2002 | LokMitra and JanMitra pilots | LokMitra was associated with Jaipur’s urban service needs; JanMitra was piloted in Jhalawar for rural service needs. They later informed the e-Mitra model. |
| 14 November 2011 | Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011 took effect | Statutory time-bound delivery of notified services; Madhya Pradesh’s 2010 law came first nationally. |
| 1 August 2012 | Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act, 2012 took effect | A statutory mechanism for a time-bound opportunity of hearing and disposal of covered public grievances. Frequently described in exam material as India’s first dedicated Right to Hearing Act; treat “first” as a comparative historical claim, not wording contained in the Act itself. |
| 2013 | Amendment to the 2011 service-guarantee law | Added a Commission-based revision mechanism to the statutory design. |
| 13 September 2019 | Jan Soochna Portal launched | Rajasthan’s public-information portal implements proactive disclosure principles under Section 4(2) of the RTI Act, 2005. |
| 18 December 2019 | Rajasthan Jan Aadhaar Yojana inaugurated | “One Number, One Card, One Identity” approach to resident-family data and scheme delivery. |
| 2020 | Rajasthan Jan Aadhaar Authority Act, 2020 | Statutory framework for the Jan Aadhaar Authority; the Act deems itself in force from 18 December 2019. |

**Core sequence to remember:** information access → time-bound service delivery → hearing and grievance redressal → proactive digital disclosure and integrated service channels. These mechanisms are complementary, not interchangeable.

## 4. Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011

**Official title:** Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011 (Act No. 23 of 2011).  
**Effective date used in official implementation material:** 14 November 2011.  
**Purpose:** To deliver specified services of public authorities to eligible persons within notified time limits, with appeal and accountability mechanisms.

### 4.1 The statutory architecture

The Act does **not** make every government service subject to one universal deadline. Under Section 3, the State Government notifies the services covered, the Designated Officer, the first appeal officer, the second appellate authority and the applicable time limit.

- **Designated Officer (DO):** the notified officer responsible for delivering the particular notified service.
- **Notified service:** a service brought under the Act through the relevant government notification.
- **Stipulated time limit:** the maximum period specified for delivering that service or deciding the first appeal, as applicable.
- **Acknowledgement:** the application must be acknowledged. The time period begins under the terms of Section 5 when the application is submitted to the designated officer or authorised receiving person.

The officer must either provide the service within the notified period or reject the application with reasons recorded in writing and communicated to the applicant.

### 4.2 Appeals

```text
Application for a notified service
            │
            ▼
Designated Officer (DO)
  • Provide service within the notified period; or
  • Reject with written reasons
            │
            ├── Service delivered in time → process ends
            │
            └── Rejection or failure to deliver in time
                         │
                         ▼
First Appeal Officer
  • Appeal normally filed within 30 days of rejection
    or expiry of the stipulated service period
                         │
                         ▼
Second Appellate Authority
  • Second appeal normally filed within 60 days
    of the first appellate decision
  • May order the service and consider penalty
                         │
                         ▼
Commission for Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services
  • Revision mechanism introduced by 2013 amendment;
    revision application normally within 60 days of
    the second appellate order
```

The Act allows late filing to be admitted where the relevant authority is satisfied that sufficient cause prevented filing within time. For the precise authority and deadline applicable to a particular service, consult the current notification rather than assuming that one officer handles every service.

### 4.3 Penalties: a critical distinction

Section 7 creates different penalties for different failures. These are **not automatic compensation**; the statutory authority must form the prescribed opinion, the officer must receive a reasonable opportunity to be heard, and the penalty is recoverable from salary.

| Failure established under the Act | Penalty under Section 7 |
|---|---|
| Designated Officer fails to provide a service without sufficient and reasonable cause | Lump-sum penalty of **₹500 to ₹5,000** |
| Designated Officer causes unjustified delay in providing a service | **₹250 per day**, capped at **₹5,000** for the delay |
| First appeal officer fails, without sufficient and reasonable cause, to decide the appeal within the stipulated time | **₹500 to ₹5,000** |
| Compensation to the appellant | The second appellate authority may direct that an amount be paid to the appellant **from the penalty imposed**, not exceeding the penalty amount |
| Persistent or unjustified non-performance | The authority may recommend disciplinary action under applicable service rules |

**Important wording:** the Act uses discretionary language such as “may impose”; it does not mean every delayed application automatically results in a fine. An officer also has to be given an opportunity to be heard before a penalty is imposed.

### 4.4 Why it matters

The law changes a notified service from an informal expectation into a time-bound statutory obligation. Its accountability chain includes service standards, written reasons for rejection, appeal rights and possible salary recovery. Effectiveness still depends on public awareness, correct service notifications, accessible filing, staffing, evidence and actual enforcement.

### 4.5 National comparison

Madhya Pradesh’s Public Services Guarantee Act, 2010 is widely identified by official Madhya Pradesh government material as the first law of its kind in India. Rajasthan’s law followed in 2011. The Rajasthan law’s notable features for revision include the **₹250/day delay penalty capped at ₹5,000**, the lump-sum non-delivery penalty, compensation from the penalty, and the later Commission-based revision mechanism.

## 5. Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act, 2012

**Official title:** Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act, 2012 (Act No. 22 of 2012).  
**Enforcement date:** 1 August 2012.  
**Purpose:** To provide an opportunity of hearing on covered public grievances within notified time limits, a decision on the complaint, and appeal/revision routes.

### 5.1 What type of issue does it address?

The Act addresses covered complaints connected with seeking benefits or relief under public policies, programmes or schemes, delays/failures relating to such benefits, and certain failures in public-authority functioning or violations of law, policy, order, programme or scheme.

The amended statutory definition does **not** turn every private dispute into a Right to Hearing complaint. It excludes, among other things, public-servant service matters; matters within a court or tribunal’s jurisdiction; and matters where an appeal is pending before a quasi-judicial authority. The law was amended in 2014 to address implementation problems in the scope of “complaint.” Check the current Act and notifications before applying the mechanism to a borderline case.

### 5.2 What the Public Hearing Officer must do

The notified Public Hearing Officer (PHO) must:

1. receive or arrange receipt of the complaint and ensure acknowledgement/registration;
2. provide an opportunity of hearing within the stipulated period;
3. decide the complaint by accepting it, referring it to an authority competent to grant the relief, suggesting an available alternative, or rejecting it with reasons recorded in writing; and
4. communicate the decision to the complainant.

The Act guarantees a **process and an opportunity to be heard**, not an automatic right to obtain the relief requested. A reasoned rejection or referral can be a lawful outcome if the statutory conditions are met.

### 5.3 Time limits: filing deadline is not the same as disposal deadline

The 1 June 2012 notifications under Section 3 set the following general periods. Under the Act, “days” means **working days**; the applicable rules exclude public holidays when calculating the stipulated time limit.

| Stage | Time limit / rule | Source meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing and disposal of original complaint | **15 days** from receipt | Time for the PHO to provide hearing and dispose of the complaint. |
| Communicating the decision | **7 days** | Notification specifies seven days for communication of the decision of a complaint or appeal. |
| First appeal: filing | Normally within **30 days** from expiry of the stipulated period or the PHO’s decision | This is the complainant’s filing deadline, subject to statutory condonation. |
| First appeal: disposal | **21 days** from filing of the appeal | Notified decision period for the first appellate authority. |
| Second appeal: filing | Normally within **30 days** of the first appellate authority’s decision | This is a filing deadline, not proof of a universal 30-day disposal deadline. |
| Revision | Normally within **60 days** of the second appellate order | The Act and the applicable notification identify the revision authority; the time period concerns filing. |

Do **not** state without qualification that the second appeal must always be decided within 30 days: the 2012 notification examined for this chapter specifies the original-complaint period, decision-communication period and first-appeal disposal period; it does not establish a blanket second-appeal disposal period in the same text.

### 5.4 Officer hierarchy: use the notification, not a made-up universal ladder

The Act authorises the State Government to notify PHOs, first appellate authorities and second appellate authorities. The 1 June 2012 notification assigns officials by the **subject and administrative level** of the complaint. For example:

- a Patwari-related revenue matter routes to the notified Tehsildar-level officer;
- certain Panchayat/Gram Sewak matters route to the notified Block Development Officer;
- Tehsil-level matters may route to the Sub-Divisional Officer; and
- district- and divisional-level matters have their own notified officers and appellate authorities, varying by department and subject.

The full notification includes several subject-specific arrangements, including separate routes for revenue, Panchayati Raj/rural development, municipal bodies and departmental matters. There is no safe universal rule that every complaint follows exactly “VDO/Sarpanch → BDO → SDO → Collector.” For factual questions, follow the current departmental notification for the precise subject.

### 5.5 Penalty: corrected statutory position

Under **Section 7**, the second appellate authority **may** impose a penalty on the Public Hearing Officer who, without sufficient and reasonable cause, failed to provide the opportunity of hearing within the stipulated time. The penalty is **not less than ₹500 and not more than ₹5,000**, and is recoverable from the officer’s salary after a reasonable opportunity of being heard.

The authority may also recommend disciplinary action against a PHO or first appellate authority for failure to discharge statutory duties without sufficient and reasonable cause. **The Right to Hearing Act does not establish the same citizen-compensation mechanism as Section 7 of the Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act.** Do not copy the compensation rule from one Act into the other.

### 5.6 Why it matters

The Act is designed to reduce repeated visits and “table-to-table” referrals by creating registration, a time-bound hearing opportunity, communicated decisions and appeals. Its limits should also be acknowledged: a complaint may be referred or rejected; a timeline is useful only if the responsible officer is correctly identified; and service quality depends on accessible centres, records, public awareness and follow-through.

## 6. Jan Soochna Portal (जन सूचना पोर्टल)

### 6.1 Launch and legal foundation

- **Launched:** 13 September 2019 by the Government of Rajasthan.
- **Purpose:** To place information about government schemes and services in one public-information portal and help citizens inspect eligibility, beneficiary and scheme-reach information.
- **Legal foundation:** Section 4(2) of the **Right to Information Act, 2005**, which requires public authorities to make continuing efforts to proactively disclose information through appropriate communication methods, including the internet, so citizens need to make fewer individual RTI requests.
- The Rajasthan government describes it as the first portal of its kind to bring information about schemes implemented in wards/panchayats together in one place. Treat this as the State’s description of the initiative, not as a statutory phrase.

### 6.2 Current dashboard snapshot

The official portal displayed **117 departments, 359 schemes and 758 “Information of Schemes” entries** in the dashboard snapshot observed on **16 October 2026**. These counts are dynamic and may change; they should be updated from the live dashboard before an exam if the question asks for current numbers.

Examples of data categories listed by the portal include MGNREGA worker information, ration-card/NFSA details, social-security pension information, sanitation beneficiaries, school education, Jan Aadhaar, mining, revenue records and other scheme-related information. The precise fields and availability vary by dataset.

### 6.3 Proactive disclosure versus a traditional RTI request

| Jan Soochna Portal | Individual RTI request |
|---|---|
| Information already published in a portal dataset can be consulted without first filing an RTI application. | A citizen asks a public authority for information not already available or not sufficiently disclosed. |
| Implements the principle of proactive disclosure under Section 4(2). | Governed by application, response and appeal provisions of the RTI Act, 2005. |
| Useful for public visibility, checking selected scheme/beneficiary records, and supporting social audit. | Useful for records or information that must be specifically requested from the relevant public authority. |
| Availability depends on what has been digitised and published, data quality and update frequency. | The statutory response period is generally 30 days, subject to statutory exceptions and special provisions; this is not inherently a “30-day delay.” |

**Exam trap:** The portal does not replace the RTI Act or remove the need for RTI in every case. Nor is every dataset necessarily live, exhaustive or searchable by Aadhaar/Jan Aadhaar. State only what the portal or the relevant dataset actually supports.

### 6.4 Governance value and limits

Jan Soochna can reduce the information gap between government and citizens, make selected scheme records easier to inspect, and support social accountability. Its impact depends on timeliness, completeness, usability, local-language access, accessibility for people with disabilities, privacy protections and the availability of a correction/grievance route when published records are wrong.

## 7. Rajasthan Sampark / CM Helpline 181

### 7.1 Purpose

**Rajasthan Sampark** is the State Government’s public grievance interface. The official portal offers grievance registration and status-checking functions and lists **181** as its toll-free number. Government information also describes an integrated call-centre and web-portal model that routes grievances to the concerned department or office.

Official portal: [sampark.rajasthan.gov.in](https://sampark.rajasthan.gov.in/)

### 7.2 Typical citizen workflow

1. **Register:** submit a grievance through the portal or an available assisted channel such as the helpline/e-Mitra, providing enough detail for identification and follow-up.
2. **Retain the reference number:** use the number for status tracking and future correspondence.
3. **Departmental action:** the grievance is routed to the concerned office for consideration and response.
4. **Check status and respond:** review the status; if the response does not resolve the issue, use the portal’s available follow-up/feedback options or the appropriate statutory remedy.

This is a practical summary, not a guarantee that every complaint follows an identical five-stage automation sequence. The live portal’s instructions and current process control.

### 7.3 What it is—and is not

- It is a **grievance-routing and follow-up channel**.
- It is not the same as a formal appeal under the Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act.
- It is not the correct substitute for an RTI application. The Sampark grievance form expressly directs RTI matters to the relevant department/Jan Soochna/RTI channel.
- A grievance number or “disposed” status does not by itself prove that the underlying problem has been substantively resolved.

**Mains evaluation:** Sampark can improve access and tracking, but disposal counts need to be assessed alongside the quality of redressal, recurrence of the same grievance, time taken, appeal/escalation outcomes and citizen feedback.

## 8. Jan Aadhaar: Identity and Benefit Delivery

### 8.1 Launch and legal framework

- The **Rajasthan Jan Aadhaar Yojana, 2019** was inaugurated on **18 December 2019**.
- The **Rajasthan Jan Aadhaar Authority Act, 2020** (Act No. 3 of 2020) provides the statutory framework for targeted delivery of public welfare benefits and services using Jan Aadhaar ID. The Act states that it is deemed to have come into force on 18 December 2019.
- Motto: **“One Number, One Card, One Identity” (एक नम्बर, एक कार्ड, एक पहचान).**

The official scheme describes Jan Aadhaar as a database of demographic and socio-economic information of resident families and as a channel supporting delivery of cash and non-cash benefits/services. It integrates with other departmental systems; it should not be portrayed as the only identifier or only payment route for every state/central benefit.

### 8.2 Identifier structure

| Identifier | Structure | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Family ID | **10 digits** | Identifies the enrolled family. |
| Individual member ID | **11 digits** | Identifies an individual member within the scheme’s system. |

These numbers are issued after the prescribed verification process. Aadhaar authentication and Jan Aadhaar enrolment are related but distinct: Jan Aadhaar uses its family/member identifiers, and its operational procedures include Aadhaar-based verification for eligible members.

### 8.3 Head of family

The official Jan Aadhaar FAQ lists the eligibility order for selecting a head of family as follows:

1. a female family member above 18 years of age;
2. a male member aged 21 years or more if there is no female member of 18 years or above; or
3. the eldest member if neither eligible category is present.

For exam answers, the key point is the scheme’s preference for a woman as head of family, while retaining defined alternatives. Avoid the absolute claim that an adult woman is invariably designated even when the family does not meet the eligibility conditions.

### 8.4 How it can improve governance

- reduces fragmentation of family/beneficiary information across linked services;
- supports verification and eligibility checks;
- can facilitate Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) where the relevant scheme is integrated and the beneficiary/bank details are valid;
- links citizens to services through digital and assisted access points such as e-Mitra; and
- can support women’s administrative visibility by recognising an eligible female family head.

### 8.5 Limits and safeguards

Jan Aadhaar does not make every welfare transfer automatically payable to the female head of family. **The beneficiary, eligibility conditions and payment account depend on the particular scheme’s rules and records.** Errors in family composition, bank details, authentication or linked databases can delay or obstruct a benefit. Any claim that the system is wholly “leakage-proof” is too strong; digitisation may reduce some risks, but it also creates data-quality, exclusion, authentication and privacy risks that require grievance correction and safeguards.

## 9. e-Mitra and e-Mitra Plus

### 9.1 Origin

Rajasthan’s e-Mitra model grew from two earlier initiatives launched in **2002**:

- **LokMitra:** urban-facing service/information pilot associated with Jaipur.
- **JanMitra:** rural-facing service/information pilot associated with Jhalawar.

Their integration informed the later e-Mitra service-delivery model. Government e-governance material describes e-Mitra as a multi-service platform using a public-private partnership (PPP) model and service counters supported by back-office processing.

### 9.2 Services and access channels

The official e-Mitra portal lists services such as applications and certificates, bill payments, transaction/status checks and Jan Aadhaar-related services. It supports a mixture of web/mobile channels, physical kiosks, e-Mitra Plus service ATMs and e-Mitra @Home services.

A current official e-Mitra description refers to approximately **80,000 physical kiosks** and around **15,000 e-Mitra Plus service ATMs**. These are operational figures, not permanent exam facts; the official dashboard and its update date should be checked before citing a precise number.

### 9.3 Governance value and risks

**Potential benefits:** one-stop access, fewer trips to offices, assisted access for people who cannot navigate online services independently, payment/application traceability and rural reach.

**Risks to manage:** variable service quality, kiosk charges, connectivity failure, data-entry mistakes, dependence on intermediaries and accessibility for citizens with limited digital literacy. A kiosk is an access point; it does not eliminate the need for a lawful decision by the competent department.

## 10. Social Audit and Civil Accountability

### 10.1 Meaning

A **social audit (सामाजिक अंकेक्षण)** is a participatory process through which citizens and affected communities examine whether public funds, public works, entitlements and programme rules match implementation on the ground. It is more than a financial audit: it can examine the accuracy of records, actual delivery, participation, quality of works and the response to identified irregularities.

### 10.2 Rajasthan’s contribution

The Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS), through public hearings (*jan sunwai*) and the verification of records against people’s lived experience, played a major role in popularising social-accountability practices in Rajasthan during the 1990s and in the wider Right to Information movement. It is safer to say “helped develop and popularise” than to claim that all social auditing was invented at one place or by one organisation.

### 10.3 Legal foundation and state institutions

- **MGNREGA, 2005, Section 17(2):** provides for social audit by the Gram Sabha of works undertaken under the scheme.
- **Audit of Schemes Rules, 2011:** form part of the national MGNREGA social-audit framework.
- Rajasthan established a state-level Social Audit Directorate and maintains the **Society for Social Audit, Accountability and Transparency (SSAAT)**. The State’s official social-audit website describes its work, schedules, reports, follow-up and capacity-building arrangements.
- Rajasthan’s official materials describe efforts to organise MGNREGA social audits twice a year at every Gram Panchayat. The exact calendar and implementation should be checked against the latest state audit calendar.
- The official SSAAT site also hosts guidance/reports for selected other programmes, including PMAY, sanitation and other schemes. This does **not** justify calling it “RSPAA,” nor does it establish that an identical audit is statutorily compulsory for every welfare scheme.

### 10.4 Social audit cycle

```text
Public records and expenditure information
                  ↓
Community verification of people, works and entitlements
                  ↓
Public hearing / discussion of findings
                  ↓
Recorded findings and responsibility for response
                  ↓
Action taken, recovery/correction where warranted,
and follow-up verification
```

### 10.5 Strengths and limitations

**Strengths:** local knowledge, visibility of ghost beneficiaries or incomplete works, public participation, accessible evidence and pressure for corrective action.

**Limitations:** poor access to records, social power imbalance, intimidation, weak follow-up, inadequate trained personnel and confusion between holding a public hearing and completing corrective action. A high-quality social audit requires both public disclosure and an enforceable response to findings.

## 11. Administrative-Reform Bodies and HCM RIPA

### 11.1 How to study reform committees

For every committee, learn five items: **year → chair/body → problem diagnosed → recommendation → implementation evidence**. A recommendation is not proof that the government accepted or implemented it.

### 11.2 Rajasthan Administrative Reforms Committee chaired by Harish Chandra Mathur (1963)

Rajasthan polity reference material identifies the 1963 Rajasthan Administrative Reforms Committee as the **Harish Chandra Mathur Committee**. Its reported Secretariat-reform themes included:

- keeping the Secretariat focused on important and policy-level matters;
- rationalising organisational structure and reducing unnecessary movement of files;
- increasing officers’ decision-making responsibility;
- improving delegation of financial powers while preserving the Finance Department’s scrutiny/advisory role; and
- giving weight to merit, tenure and field experience in staffing senior/administrative roles.

These are recommendations reported in secondary Rajasthan polity teaching material; for a question asking the exact wording or the full set of recommendations, use the standard textbook or the original report if available. Do not present the committee’s recommendations as proof that every proposed reform was implemented.

### 11.3 HCM Rajasthan State Institute of Public Administration (HCM RIPA)

Official institute history records that:

- the Officers’ Training School began in **Jodhpur in 1957**;
- it moved to Jaipur in **1963**;
- it was renamed **Harish Chandra Mathur State Institute of Public Administration** in **1969**; and
- “Rajasthan” was added to the name in **1983**.

HCM RIPA is the Government of Rajasthan’s apex-level civil-service training institution. It supports training and capacity building for civil servants. **Do not conflate its institutional timeline with the separate history or recommendations of the 1963 committee.**

### 11.4 Claims removed from the supplied draft

The supplied draft listed a “Sudhir Kumar State Administrative Reforms Committee (2000)” and characterised a “V.S. Vyas Committee (2005)” as an agricultural/rural-credit administrative-reform body. The material located during this audit did not substantiate the first entry. Available historical material instead identifies a V.S. Vyas-led committee on **integrated development and management of water resources**, with recommendations in 2005—an unrelated subject. These entries have therefore been removed rather than repeated as facts. ## 12. High-Yield Comparison Tables and Exam Traps

### 12.1 Service Guarantee Act vs Right to Hearing Act

| Feature | Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011 | Right to Hearing Act, 2012 |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Timely delivery of **notified services** | Hearing and reasoned disposal of **covered public grievances** |
| Main officer | Designated Officer (DO) | Public Hearing Officer (PHO) |
| Deadline | Varies by notified service | 15 days for original complaint hearing/disposal under the 2012 notification |
| First appeal | Normally within 30 days of rejection or expiry of service deadline | Normally within 30 days of stipulated-period expiry or PHO decision |
| First-appeal disposal | As notified for the applicable service/system | 21 days from filing under the 2012 notification |
| Second appeal filing | Normally within 60 days of first appellate decision | Normally within 30 days of first appellate decision |
| Penalty for DO/PHO | ₹500–₹5,000 lump sum for unjustified failure; ₹250/day for unjustified delay, capped at ₹5,000 | ₹500–₹5,000 for unjustified failure by PHO to provide opportunity of hearing within time |
| Salary recovery | Yes, subject to statutory procedure | Yes, subject to statutory procedure |
| Citizen compensation | Permitted from the penalty under the 2011 Act, up to the penalty imposed | Do not assume the same compensation provision applies |
| Further review | Commission-based revision mechanism introduced by the 2013 amendment | Revision mechanism under Section 8 and applicable notification |

### 12.2 Common exam traps

1. **Which law came first nationally for time-bound services?** Madhya Pradesh’s 2010 law preceded Rajasthan’s 2011 law.
2. **Rajasthan service-guarantee penalty:** unjustified delay = ₹250/day, maximum ₹5,000; unjustified failure to provide service = lump sum ₹500–₹5,000.
3. **Right to Hearing penalty:** ₹500–₹5,000, not ₹250–₹5,000; this is Section 7, not Section 9.
4. **Filing vs disposal:** the 30-day second-appeal period under the Right to Hearing Act is a filing deadline; do not convert it into a 30-day disposal guarantee without a supporting notification.
5. **Compensation:** the 2011 service-guarantee Act permits compensation from the penalty. Do not copy that provision into the 2012 hearing law.
6. **Jan Soochna:** a proactive disclosure channel under Section 4(2) of the central RTI Act, not a replacement for every RTI request.
7. **Jan Aadhaar ID:** family ID = 10 digits; member ID = 11 digits. A woman is normally prioritised as head of family under the scheme’s eligibility sequence, but that does not mean every benefit is paid into her account.
8. **e-Mitra origin:** the LokMitra–JanMitra pilots began in 2002; kiosk and service totals are dynamic.
9. **Social audit:** the verified institutional name is Society for Social Audit, Accountability and Transparency (SSAAT), not “RSPAA.” MGNREGA Section 17(2) is a key statutory anchor.
10. **Good-governance attributes:** the World Bank’s 1992 definition of governance and UNESCAP’s eight-characteristic list are related but not the same attribution.
11. **HCM RIPA:** Officers’ Training School began in 1957; HCM RIPA’s name was adopted in 1969. The institute’s chronology is distinct from the Mathur Committee’s chronology.
12. **Historical RTI law:** Rajasthan enacted its own RTI law in 2000, but the national RTI regime is now centred on the Right to Information Act, 2005.

## 13. Practice Questions and Model-Answer Frameworks

### 13.1 Two-mark questions

**Q1. What is the key difference between a Citizen’s Charter and a statutory service guarantee?**  
**Model answer:** A Citizen’s Charter publicly states an organisation’s service standards and grievance routes. A statutory service-guarantee law specifies covered services, time limits, appeal routes and possible penalties for unjustified failure.

**Q2. State the two main penalty forms under Section 7 of the Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011.**  
**Model answer:** For unjustified failure to provide a service, the penalty may be ₹500–₹5,000; for unjustified delay, it may be ₹250 per day up to ₹5,000. Penalty is not automatic and the officer must receive an opportunity to be heard.

**Q3. What is the penalty range under the Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act, 2012?**  
**Model answer:** Section 7 permits a penalty of ₹500–₹5,000 on a Public Hearing Officer for unjustified failure to provide an opportunity of hearing within the stipulated period, recoverable from salary.

**Q4. Which RTI provision underpins the Jan Soochna Portal?**  
**Model answer:** Section 4(2) of the Right to Information Act, 2005, which requires continuing efforts towards proactive disclosure so that citizens need less recourse to individual RTI applications.

**Q5. State the Jan Aadhaar identification format.**  
**Model answer:** A 10-digit family ID is assigned to an enrolled family and an 11-digit individual ID to each family member.

**Q6. Name Rajasthan’s state social-audit institution.**  
**Model answer:** The Society for Social Audit, Accountability and Transparency (SSAAT), supported by state social-audit institutional arrangements.

### 13.2 Five-mark questions

**Q1. Explain the appeal and penalty architecture under the Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011.**  
**Model answer framework:** Explain the notified-service model; application acknowledgement and written reasons for rejection; first appeal within 30 days and second appeal normally within 60 days; possible order to provide the service; separate penalties for unjustified failure and delay; salary recovery after an opportunity to be heard; possible compensation from the penalty; and the Commission-based revision mechanism added in 2013.

**Q2. Distinguish the Right to Hearing Act from the Service Guarantee Act.**  
**Model answer framework:** The 2011 Act focuses on a specific notified service, its service deadline and delivery appeal. The 2012 Act focuses on covered public grievances and a time-bound opportunity of hearing, with a reasoned decision and appeals. They have different primary officers, timelines and penalty provisions.

**Q3. How does the Jan Soochna Portal support transparency and social audit?**  
**Model answer framework:** Mention launch date (13 September 2019); Section 4(2) proactive-disclosure connection; consolidation of selected scheme/beneficiary/eligibility information; use by citizens and community groups to check public records; and limits such as data quality, update frequency, incomplete digitisation and the need for RTI or a grievance route where portal information is insufficient or incorrect.

**Q4. Describe the role of e-Mitra in citizen-centric governance.**  
**Model answer framework:** Explain the LokMitra/JanMitra origins, one-stop G2C/B2C services, physical and digital channels, reduced travel and assisted access, and the continuing need for lawful departmental decisions and oversight of fees, connectivity, errors and inclusion.

### 13.3 Ten-mark questions

**Q1. “Rajasthan’s public-service laws represent a shift from administrative discretion towards rights-based accountability.” Critically examine.**

Suggested structure:

- **Introduction:** Define rights-based administration and distinguish service delivery from grievance hearing.
- **2011 Act:** notified service standards; designated officer; written rejection reasons; appeals; penalties of ₹500–₹5,000 for unjustified failure and ₹250/day for delay up to ₹5,000; possible compensation from penalty; 2013 revision mechanism.
- **2012 Act:** covered grievance; registration and hearing within 15 days; decision communication; appeal rights; ₹500–₹5,000 penalty against a PHO for unjustified failure to provide hearing.
- **Digital complement:** Jan Soochna for proactive information, Sampark/181 for grievance routing and e-Mitra for assisted access.
- **Implementation challenges:** public awareness, staffing, records, correct routing, procedural disposal without substantive resolution, data errors and weak follow-up.
- **Way forward:** publish current service lists and appeal officers; monitor appeal outcomes and repeat grievances; improve accessible local-language assistance; protect privacy; report outcomes transparently; and close the loop between social-audit findings and corrective action.

**Q2. Evaluate the contribution of Jan Soochna, Jan Aadhaar, e-Mitra and social audit to good governance in Rajasthan.**

Suggested structure:

- **Information:** Jan Soochna puts selected scheme information in public view under the Section 4(2) principle.
- **Identity and delivery:** Jan Aadhaar provides family/member identifiers and supports service/benefit delivery where programmes are integrated.
- **Access:** e-Mitra provides assisted physical and digital routes for applications and services.
- **Participation and accountability:** social audit allows community verification and documented follow-up.
- **Synergy:** information → access to the service → beneficiary verification → community scrutiny → correction/grievance/appeal.
- **Critical evaluation:** inclusion and exclusion errors, privacy, authentication failures, uneven digital literacy, poor data updates and paper-compliance risk mean that technology must be backed by accountable institutions and independent verification.

### 13.4 Analytical synthesis model

For a Mains response on good governance, use the **I-A-D-R** framework:

- **Information:** Is information proactively published and accessible?
- **Access:** Can the citizen apply or obtain assistance through a usable channel?
- **Decision:** Is the responsible officer identified, time bound and required to give reasons?
- **Review:** Can the citizen appeal, complain, seek correction or participate in social audit?

This framework connects portals, kiosks, statutory procedures and community accountability without treating any single platform as a complete solution.

## 14. Rapid Revision Sheet

- **Good governance’s common eight characteristics:** participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, consensus orientation, equity/inclusiveness, effectiveness/efficiency and accountability.
- **Citizen’s Charter:** UK 1991; Indian Action Plan for Effective and Responsive Government adopted at the Conference of Chief Ministers on 24 May 1997.
- **Rajasthan RTI Act:** Act No. 13 of 2000; brought into force in 2001. The national RTI framework centres on the central RTI Act, 2005.
- **Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act:** Act No. 23 of 2011; in force from 14 November 2011 in official implementation material.
- **Service guarantee penalties:** ₹500–₹5,000 for unjustified failure; ₹250 per day for unjustified delay, maximum ₹5,000; first appeal officer can also face ₹500–₹5,000 for unjustified failure to decide in time.
- **Service guarantee appeals:** first appeal normally within 30 days; second appeal normally within 60 days; revision mechanism through the Commission was added by 2013 amendment.
- **Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act:** Act No. 22 of 2012; enforcement from 1 August 2012.
- **Right to Hearing timelines:** 15 days for original complaint hearing/disposal; 7 days to communicate the decision; 21 days for disposal of first appeal; appeals have their own filing deadlines.
- **Right to Hearing penalty:** ₹500–₹5,000 under Section 7; salary recovery; no automatic compensation mechanism identical to the 2011 Act.
- **Jan Soochna:** launched 13 September 2019; Section 4(2), RTI Act 2005. Dashboard snapshot seen 16 October 2026: 117 departments, 359 schemes, 758 information entries; figures change.
- **Rajasthan Sampark:** grievance portal; toll-free 181; not a replacement for statutory appeals or RTI applications.
- **Jan Aadhaar:** launched 18 December 2019; Family ID 10 digits; individual member ID 11 digits; Act No. 3 of 2020 deems the Act in force from 18 December 2019.
- **e-Mitra:** based on LokMitra (Jaipur) and JanMitra (Jhalawar) pilots launched in 2002; precise kiosk/service counts are dynamic.
- **Social audit:** MGNREGA Section 17(2); Rajasthan has a Social Audit Directorate and SSAAT; official state material describes twice-yearly Gram Panchayat audits under the state programme.
- **HCM RIPA:** OTS in Jodhpur 1957; moved to Jaipur 1963; named HCM State Institute of Public Administration in 1969; “Rajasthan” added in 1983.
