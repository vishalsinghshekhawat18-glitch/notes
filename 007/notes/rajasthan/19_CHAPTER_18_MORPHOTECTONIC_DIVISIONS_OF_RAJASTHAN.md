# Chapter 18: Morphotectonic Divisions of Rajasthan — Revised Master Chapter

**Audit status:** Substantially revised after source review  
**Primary use:** RPSC RAS and related Rajasthan geography examinations  
**Evidence rule:** Standard exam classifications are retained where useful, but they are labelled as conventions when definitions vary. Claims about geological origin, precise percentages, peak elevations and ancient drainage are not treated as certain merely because they appear in coaching notes.

---

## 1. How to Understand Rajasthan’s Physical Geography

Rajasthan’s relief is the result of several interacting processes: ancient crustal evolution, folding and faulting, sedimentation, river erosion and deposition, weathering, and wind action. Its landscapes range from the old Aravalli mountain belt to the Thar’s aeolian dunes, the alluvial plains of the east, Chambal ravines and the mixed sedimentary-volcanic terrain of Hadoti.

### 1.1 Four commonly taught physiographic divisions

For RPSC/RAS preparation, a widely used fourfold scheme is:

1. **Western Sandy Plain / north-western desert region** — Thar Desert, aeolian landforms, inland drainage and saline depressions.
2. **Aravalli Range and hilly region** — old, strongly denuded mountain belts and associated uplands.
3. **Eastern Plains** — riverine and alluvial plains, including the Banas–Banganga and Chambal systems.
4. **South-eastern Plateau / Hadoti Plateau** — Vindhyan sedimentary scarps and areas influenced by Deccan Trap basalt, dissected by the Chambal and its tributaries.

The fourfold scheme is a practical physical-geography classification, not a single universally binding geological taxonomy. Rajasthan Foundation gives the Eastern Plains as about 23.3% of the state and Hadoti as about 9.6%; many exam-preparation tables use approximately 61%, 9%, 23% and 7% for the four divisions. The difference reflects differing boundary conventions and rounding. Do not present all percentages as precision measurements without naming the scheme. [1]

| Common exam division | Approximate share often used in exam notes | Main characteristics |
|---|---:|---|
| Western Sandy Plain | ~61% | Sandy terrain, dunes, arid/semi-arid climate, inland drainage in several tracts |
| Aravalli Range and hills | ~9% | Ancient folded/metamorphic rocks, ridges, valleys, mineral resources |
| Eastern Plains | ~23% | River and alluvial plains, comparatively more extensive agriculture and settlement |
| South-eastern Plateau / Hadoti | ~7% | Vindhyan sandstone and Deccan Trap basalt in different sub-regions; ravines and plateau surfaces |

**Caution:** Population shares such as 40/10/39/11 are reproduced in some coaching tables, but should not be treated as a current official population classification unless the source defines the boundaries and census calculation. Physical regions do not map neatly onto administrative districts.

### 1.2 Geographic regionalisation schemes

Different geographers classify Rajasthan for different purposes: physiography, rainfall, agriculture, drainage, or regional planning. The fourfold division above is the simplest exam framework. More detailed regionalisations split the state into arid, semi-arid, canal-irrigated, Aravalli, eastern agricultural, south-eastern agricultural and ravine regions, or use macro-, meso- and micro-regions.

Avoid memorising a claim that one scholar definitively invented the “first” classification unless the prescribed textbook gives a precise citation. A useful answer can say that classifications vary with the criteria used, then state the criteria: relief, lithology, drainage, rainfall or land use.

### 1.3 Geological framework: correct time scales and relationships

- **Aravalli–Delhi belt:** A very old Precambrian mountain/fold belt, with complex metamorphic and igneous rocks. It is commonly described as one of the world’s older mountain systems, but the phrase “the oldest fold chain on Earth” is too absolute.
- **Vindhyan Basin:** Dominated by sedimentary rocks, notably sandstone, shale and limestone in different formations. It is not a basalt plateau.
- **Deccan Traps:** Extensive basaltic lava flows associated with Late Cretaceous volcanism. Parts of south-eastern Rajasthan have Deccan Trap basalt.
- **Western desert deposits:** Quaternary wind-blown sand, older and younger dune systems, alluvium and other sediments over a complex geological basement. Some western Rajasthan rocks record former marine conditions, but the entire modern Thar Desert is not simply a direct “remnant of the Tethys Sea.”
- **Eastern plains:** Principally a product of river systems, alluvial deposition, erosion and local geological structure. Their alluvium should not be described as a direct Tethys Sea deposit.
- **Hadoti:** A mixed physiographic province. Vindhyan sedimentary scarps and Deccan basalt occur in different parts; the phrase “Vindhyan basalt” is incorrect.

### 1.4 Great Boundary Fault (GBF)

The **Great Boundary Fault** is an important structural lineament in south-eastern Rajasthan, near the contact between older basement/fold-belt rocks and the Vindhyan sedimentary succession. Geological interpretations have varied: studies have discussed normal-fault origins, later reactivation and reverse/thrust geometry. Therefore, it is misleading to state as an uncontested fact that it is an 800-km “suture zone.” Geological literature generally describes it as several hundred kilometres long; approximately 400 km is common in Rajasthan geography material, but the exact mapped trace depends on the segment and interpretation. [8]

**Exam-safe description:** “A major fault zone in south-eastern Rajasthan associated with the structural boundary between older rocks of the Aravalli/Bhilwara region and the Vindhyan succession; it is important for understanding the geology and relief of the Hadoti margin.”

Its surface trace is not a single continuous vertical sandstone wall, and a simple route through every district listed in older notes should not be treated as a surveyed universal alignment.

## 2. Division I — Western Sandy Plain / Thar Desert

The Western Sandy Plain is Rajasthan’s largest physiographic division. It contains mobile and stabilised dunes, sandy plains, rocky and gravelly tracts, inland drainage basins, salt lakes and alluvial surfaces. The Thar also extends beyond Rajasthan into neighbouring Indian states and Pakistan. FAO describes the Thar as the world’s most densely populated desert, quoting a density of about 83 persons per square kilometre; this is a desert-wide estimate, not a current census figure for the Rajasthan physiographic division alone. [4]

### 2.1 Rainfall and sub-regional framework

Exam texts often use the **25 cm** and **50 cm** annual-rainfall isohyets as convenient guides:

- **Marusthali / more arid western tract:** generally below about 25 cm rainfall.
- **Rajasthan Bagar / semi-arid transition:** often presented as the 25–50 cm belt east of the more arid tract.

These are climatological guide lines, not immovable administrative boundaries. Rainfall changes from year to year, and a single isohyet does not demarcate every sub-region exactly.

A common teaching scheme divides the arid sector into **dune-covered terrain** and **dune-free or relatively dune-poor terrain**. CAZRI literature describes sand dunes as covering roughly 58% of the Rajasthan desert; 58.5% and 41.5% are often used as a paired exam-note split. Treat the decimal values as a conventional classification, not a timeless land-cover measurement. [5]

### 2.2 Desert surface terms

| Term | Meaning | Exam distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Erg** | An extensive sandy desert or sand sea | Dune-dominated terrain; not a single dune type |
| **Hamada** | A rocky desert surface or plateau, often with little loose sand | Rock-dominated desert pavement |
| **Reg** | A gravelly or stony desert surface | Coarse fragments and gravel rather than deep mobile sand |
| **Dune / Tibba / Dhora** | A mound or ridge of wind-deposited sand | General landform term; regional names vary |
| **Playa / inland saline depression** | A basin where water temporarily collects and evaporates | Can leave salts; not necessarily a surviving sea |

### 2.3 Sand dunes: form, wind and stability

Sand dunes form when wind transports and deposits sand. Shape depends on wind direction and variability, sand supply, vegetation, surface moisture and obstacles. No single type is “the most dangerous” in all circumstances; mobile dunes can create local hazards, while vegetated dunes may be relatively stable.

| Dune type | Basic shape / formation | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Barchan / Barkhan** | Crescent-shaped; horns point downwind under a dominant wind direction and limited sand supply | Typically mobile; movement rate varies by sand supply, wind and size |
| **Transverse** | Ridges broadly perpendicular to the dominant wind | Common where sand supply is abundant and winds are reasonably persistent |
| **Longitudinal / linear / seif** | Long ridges broadly aligned with the resultant wind direction | Common in parts of western and southern Thar; formation can involve complex wind regimes |
| **Parabolic** | U-shaped dunes commonly anchored by vegetation; arms point upwind | Stabilised/coalesced forms are widespread in the Rajasthan desert literature |
| **Star** | Several arms formed under multidirectional wind regimes | Localised; not a universal indicator of one particular locality |
| **Coppice / nebkha** | Small mounds of sand accumulating around shrubs or other obstacles | Closely tied to vegetation and local sediment supply |
| **Compound / complex dunes** | Multiple forms joined into a larger dune field | A single field may combine more than one basic form |

CAZRI and desert geomorphology literature describes parabolic/coalesced parabolic dunes as prominent and widespread, while barchans and barchanoids are often more mobile. Do not memorise “parabolic dunes occur in every district” or “barchans have the highest desertification hazard” as scientific universals. [5]

### 2.4 Dune-free and rocky tracts; Akal Wood Fossil Park

Parts of the desert between and around Pokhran, Ramgarh and Phalodi have fewer extensive dune fields and more rocky, gravelly or sedimentary surfaces. “Dune-free” is a relative geomorphic label; it does not mean that the whole tract is a single bare-rock hamada.

**Akal Wood Fossil Park**, near Jaisalmer, preserves fossilised tree trunks dating to roughly **180 million years ago**, in the Jurassic. The Rajasthan Tourism and Jaisalmer district pages describe the site as evidence of ancient vegetation and a markedly different environment from today’s desert. Fossilised wood supports the existence of a past forested landscape; it does not by itself prove that the area was an “equatorial rainforest.” Distances are commonly reported as roughly 15–17 km from Jaisalmer, so avoid insisting on 21 km as an exact figure. [2, 3]

### 2.5 Lathi Formation / aquifer and Chandan well

The **Lathi Formation** is an important local geological and groundwater unit in Jaisalmer district. Central Ground Water Board material describes relatively fresh groundwater in the Lathi aquifer, although water quality varies by location and aquifer. The popular description of Chandan tube well as the “Thar ka Ghada” is a regional nickname, not proof of an inexhaustible supply. [6]

The association of some aquifers with buried channels is a hydrogeological interpretation. Linking the entire Lathi aquifer conclusively to a specific “mythical Saraswati river” channel goes beyond what should be stated as established fact in a static geography source.

**Sewan grass** (*Lasiurus scindicus*) is an important desert grass and grazing resource. The Great Indian Bustard (Godawan) is critically endangered and has important habitat in the western desert, especially the Desert National Park landscape; avoid calling the grassland a “prime nesting sanctuary” without qualifying the specific locality and evidence. [7]

### 2.6 Semi-arid transition: main sub-regions

#### A. Luni Basin / Godwar

- The Luni rises in the Aravalli region near Ajmer and flows generally southwest through western Rajasthan before dispersing into the Rann of Kachchh region; the lower course is often seasonal and the water becomes saline in sections.
- Tributaries include the Sukri, Jawai, Bandi and others; river names and basin coverage are not identical to current district boundaries.
- The Jalore region contains granites and other igneous/metamorphic formations. **Chhappan ki Pahadiyan** refers in Rajasthan exam tradition to a cluster of rounded hills around the Siwana area (now Balotra district). It must not be confused with **Chhappan ka Maidan** in southern Rajasthan.

#### B. Nagauri Upland and the Kobar/Banka Patti belt

The Nagaur–Didwana–Ajmer belt is associated in local geography and public-health discussions with groundwater fluoride problems. Chronic excess fluoride exposure can produce dental and, in severe cases, skeletal fluorosis. “Kobar Patti” or “Banka Patti” is a regional teaching term, not a formally defined geological formation; do not explain it as a single underground rock layer or claim that it uniformly causes visible spinal deformity across its population.

#### C. Shekhawati internal drainage

Parts of Shekhawati are characterised by internal drainage, seasonal streams and sandy terrain. The **Kantli** is an example of a seasonal river that does not reach the sea. Some depressions and channels terminate within sandy or inland basins.

- **Johad:** A traditional rainwater-harvesting pond or earthen embankment reservoir, generally designed to hold runoff and improve infiltration. It is not properly defined as a “raw rainwater well drilled down to limestone.”
- **Beed:** A community or village grazing reserve/pasture. Management practices vary locally; “fenced and rotational” is not an essential universal definition.

#### D. Ghaggar Plain / Nali

The Ghaggar is an ephemeral river system crossing the northern part of Rajasthan. Its channel continues into the Hakra area of present-day Pakistan in descriptions of the broader Ghaggar–Hakra system. The Nali tract is commonly associated with the low-lying Ghaggar floodplain in the Hanumangarh region.

Ancient identifications of Ghaggar–Hakra with the Vedic Saraswati and Drishadvati systems remain a matter of archaeological, geomorphological and historical debate. State the present-day river system and its seasonality as fact; describe the Saraswati link as a hypothesis or traditional identification, not as settled proof.

## 3. Division II — Aravalli Range and Hilly Region

The Aravalli Range trends broadly **south-west to north-east**, from the Mount Abu region toward the Delhi–Haryana uplands. The Rajasthan Foundation lists an approximate total range length of **692 km**. Textbooks commonly quote about **550 km within Rajasthan**, but the exact length depends on how the broken continuation and endpoints are defined. Guru Shikhar, at **1,722 m**, is the highest point of the range and of Rajasthan. [1, 2]

### 3.1 Geomorphic and ecological significance

1. **Old mountain belt:** The Aravallis are a deeply denuded Precambrian fold/mountain system, not a young, continuously high range.
2. **Drainage divide:** The range and its associated uplands influence drainage toward the Arabian Sea and toward the Bay of Bengal systems, but local watersheds do not everywhere follow a simple straight crest line.
3. **Rainfall contrast:** The range lies broadly parallel to the south-west monsoon flow from the Arabian Sea, so it does not create a continuous, high orographic barrier across the state. Mount Abu is a notable high-rainfall exception; the aridity of western Rajasthan has multiple causes.
4. **Minerals and habitat:** Aravalli and associated Proterozoic rocks are important for mineral resources and support forest and tribal landscapes. Mining and fragmentation can create ecological pressure.

### 3.2 Regional subdivisions: one useful exam scheme

Some teaching sources divide the Aravallis into **northern, central and southern** zones; other sources distinguish four belts or separate the central range into Shekhawati and Merwara sections. The boundaries are schematic rather than a universally fixed morphotectonic map.

| Region | Broad area | Key relief associations often used in exams |
|---|---|---|
| Northern Aravalli / Shekhawati hills | Sikar, Jhunjhunu, Jaipur–Alwar uplands and adjoining areas | Raghunathgarh and other broken ridges; quartzite, phyllite and other old rocks |
| Central Aravalli / Merwara hills | Ajmer–Beawar and neighbouring hill country | Narrower and more dissected belts, ghats and passes; Taragarh and Todgarh-area highlands |
| Southern Aravalli | Udaipur, Rajsamand and Sirohi region | Highest and more continuous mountain massifs; Mount Abu and Guru Shikhar |

**Administrative caution:** District names changed in the 2020s. Official Rajasthan pages list 41 districts as of October 2026, including Balotra, Beawar, Didwana-Kuchaman, Phalodi, Salumbar and others. Older geography books may use their parent districts. Physical regions do not change when a district boundary changes. [9]

### 3.3 Important peaks and elevations

The following are widely taught approximate elevations; sources can differ slightly because of measurement points, map datums or whether a temple/platform at the summit is included.

| Peak / hill | Approximate elevation commonly quoted | Association |
|---|---:|---|
| **Guru Shikhar** | **1,722 m** | Highest point of Rajasthan and the Aravallis; Mount Abu, Sirohi |
| **Ser** | ~1,597 m | Southern Aravalli, Sirohi area |
| **Dilwara** | ~1,442 m | Mount Abu area |
| **Jarga** | ~1,431 m | Mewar / southern Aravalli |
| **Achalgarh** | ~1,380 m | Mount Abu region |
| **Raghunathgarh** | ~1,055 m | Commonly taught as the highest peak of the northern Aravalli scheme |
| **Kho** | ~920 m | Northern Aravalli/Jaipur hills in many lists |
| **Taragarh** | ~870 m (some sources differ) | Ajmer region |

Do not treat a “top 15” list as definitive without a reliable map or survey source. In the original source, some peaks were omitted (for example, Malkhet and Lohargal from several commonly used northern peak lists), some elevations varied, and districts were mixed across changing administrative boundaries. Guru Shikhar should be memorised as **1,722 m** according to Rajasthan Tourism, while older books may cite 1,727 m for a summit structure or a different point. [2, 10]

### 3.4 Plateaus, valleys and local landforms

- **Oriya/Udia Plateau:** Common Rajasthan exam material lists it at around 1,360 m in the Mount Abu area and often calls it the state’s highest plateau. Since precise “plateau” definitions and elevations are not consistently documented across authoritative sources, use that as a textbook convention rather than an unqualified survey fact.
- **Abu Plateau:** A high granitic upland in the Mount Abu region.
- **Bhorat Plateau:** Commonly placed between the Kumbhalgarh uplands and Gogunda region, often quoted at about 1,225 m; it belongs to the high southern Aravalli uplands and is associated with drainage divides.
- **Uparmal Plateau:** Rocky tableland commonly described between Bijolia and Bhainsrorgarh, with important mining and historical landscapes.
- **Lashadiya/Lasadia Plateau:** A dissected, badland-like plateau east of Jaisamand Lake; descriptions often locate it in the Salumbar–Udaipur region. Use both the traditional and current administrative context when comparing older sources.
- **Desh-Haro:** A local name used for a greener valley area between Jarga and Raga hills in the Udaipur region; exact boundaries vary in regional references.
- **Bhakar:** Term used for steep, rugged, sharp-edged hill country, particularly in Sirohi and the western Aravalli fringe.
- **Girwa:** The ring of hills surrounding the Udaipur basin/city; “girdle of hills” is a useful descriptive gloss.

### 3.5 Mountain passes / Naal

A **naal** or **ghat** is a low or narrow passage through a hill range, allowing movement between valleys. Important passes commonly named in Rajasthan geography include:

- **Desuri ki Naal:** Links the Pali-side route with the Kumbhalgarh/Charbhuja area toward Mewar.
- **Hathiguda Naal:** Associated with approaches to Kumbhalgarh and the surrounding southern Aravalli.
- **Someshwar Naal:** A rugged pass/gorge in the southern-central Aravalli region.
- **Paglya/Jhilwa Naal, Bar Naal and other local passes:** Names and locations vary among texts and administrative descriptions, so use a map-linked source for exact district or highway placement.

Avoid describing every named pass as a direct Marwar–Mewar military route without historical or map evidence.

## 4. Division III — Eastern Plains

The Eastern Plains occupy the northeast, east and southeast of the Aravalli system. Rajasthan Foundation lists this division at about **23.3% of the state’s area**. It contains river plains, older alluvial surfaces and locally undulating terrain rather than a uniform flat surface. [1]

### 4.1 Principal drainage/landform groupings

#### A. Chambal Basin: ravines and badlands

The Chambal and its tributaries have produced deeply dissected ravines through gully erosion in unconsolidated materials in parts of Dholpur, Karauli and Sawai Madhopur, extending beyond Rajasthan into Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh.

- **Beehad / ravines:** Narrow, branching gullies and steep-sided erosional channels.
- **Utkhat Bhumi / badland topography:** A common regional term for heavily dissected terrain; it describes a landform, not a formally bounded single plateau.
- **Dang:** A regional name used for rough, ravine-affected or scrubby country in parts of eastern Rajasthan, especially around Karauli and Dholpur. “Dang ki Rani” is an epithet found in exam notes, not a geological classification.

The original claim that one badland unit covers an exact 4,500 sq km or is unambiguously India’s largest should not be memorised without a defined map and study.

#### B. Banas–Banganga / Mewar plain and eastern alluvial plains

The Banas system drains much of south-eastern Rajasthan and eventually joins the Chambal. The Berach, Kothari and Khari form important parts of its tributary network. The Banganga drains eastward toward the Yamuna system through the state’s eastern plains. Alluvial deposits are important, but plains also include older surfaces, bedrock outcrops and local hills.

Some regional schemes distinguish a **Mewar Plain** in the west from a **Malpura–Karauli plain** in the east. Treat these as regional divisions in a particular classification, not as two universally defined sedimentary basins.

#### C. Mahi Basin, Chhappan Plain and Kanthal

- **Chhappan ka Maidan:** A southern Rajasthan regional plain associated with Banswara and Pratapgarh and the Mahi drainage network. “Chhappan” is commonly explained through 56 streams and/or 56 villages in local accounts. Retain the association for exams but label the etymological explanation as traditional rather than a surveyed hydrological count.
- **Kanthal:** A local regional name associated with the Mahi river corridor in Pratapgarh. It is not a “coastal” plain in the marine-geography sense; in this inland context, the word is used for the riverbank/edge region.

### 4.2 Why the eastern plains are agriculturally important

Compared with the driest western desert, much of the eastern plain has more reliable rainfall, extensive river/alluvial soils, irrigation access and greater agricultural settlement. Wheat, mustard, gram and other crops are important, but crop distribution depends on irrigation, soil, climate and local cropping decisions—not the physiographic label alone.

## 5. Division IV — South-eastern Plateau / Hadoti

Hadoti includes the Kota–Bundi–Baran–Jhalawar region in the conventional scheme and connects toward the Malwa plateau. The physiographic province is geologically mixed. It contains **Vindhyan sedimentary scarps** as well as areas with **Deccan Trap basalt** and associated black soils. Rajasthan Foundation’s current overview gives the south-eastern plains/Hadoti region as about 9.6% of the state; many exam charts round it to around 7%. Treat those numbers as scheme-dependent, not physically exact boundaries. [1]

### 5.1 Vindhyan scarpland and Chambal-facing relief

The Vindhyan region is known for sandstone and other sedimentary strata, which can produce prominent scarps, mesas, ridges and stepped relief. Chambal erosion has dissected parts of the landscape into ravines. “Vindhyan” names a rock succession/geological region; it does not imply that all plateau land is basalt.

### 5.2 Deccan lava and black soils

Parts of south-eastern Rajasthan expose basalt from the Deccan volcanic province. Weathering of basalt can form clay-rich black soils (Regur/Vertisols) where climate and drainage permit. However, not every Hadoti soil is black soil, and not every black-soil site is on a single uniform lava plateau.

### 5.3 Mukundara Hills and Shahbad Upland

- **Mukundara Hills:** A dissected hill belt between the Kota–Jhalawar region and surrounding plateau country. Some exam sources quote **Chandwadi** at about 517 m as a high point; treat the specific highest-peak claim as a textbook association unless confirmed against a topographic map.
- **Shahbad Upland:** A forested/highland tract in Baran district, adjoining the Vindhyan landscape.
- **Dag–Gangdhar Upland:** A low rolling basaltic upland commonly associated with south-western Jhalawar.

### 5.4 Ramgarh Crater — current legal status and science

**Ramgarh Crater** is located near Ramgarh village in **Kishanganj tehsil, Baran district**. Rajasthan Government Notification **S.O. 579 of 16 March 2024**, published in the Rajasthan Gazette on 19 March 2024, declares it a **National Geo-Heritage Site** in consultation with the Geological Survey of India. The notification describes it as approximately **3.2 km in diameter** and estimates its age at about **165 million years**. [11]

Exam corrections:

- The official state notification is the strongest source for the designation and location.
- The often-quoted **75,000-year** age is inconsistent with the 2024 notification and should be discarded for this chapter.
- “106th national geological monument” and “India’s third impact crater” are not stable identifiers supported by the notification. Use the verified designation **National Geo-Heritage Site** and cite the gazette.
- The crater contains the Pushkar Talab wetland complex and forms part of a landscape with ecological and cultural value. The state notification also describes the Bhand Deva Temple in relation to the crater.

## 7. High-Yield Prelims Traps — Corrected

1. **The four divisions:** Western Sandy Plain, Aravalli Range and hills, Eastern Plains, South-eastern Plateau/Hadoti.
2. **Area shares:** Common exam shorthand is roughly 61/9/23/7%; Rajasthan Foundation reports Eastern Plains ~23.3% and Hadoti ~9.6%. Do not mix tables from differing classifications without noting the source.
3. **Tectonic/geological origin:** The Aravallis are an ancient Precambrian mountain belt; they are not best memorised simply as “Gondwanaland remnants.”
4. **Tethys Sea:** Former marine conditions are recorded in some regional rocks, but neither all western sandy terrain nor the Eastern Plains can be explained as a direct Tethys remnant.
5. **Hadoti rocks:** Vindhyan sandstone/sediments and Deccan Trap basalt occur in different parts. “Vindhyan basalt” is incorrect.
6. **Great Boundary Fault:** A major fault zone in south-eastern Rajasthan; several-hundred-kilometre length and tectonic interpretation vary in the literature. Avoid the unqualified 800-km suture-zone claim.
7. **50 cm / 25 cm isohyets:** Common teaching guides for western aridity and the semi-arid transition; not permanent administrative borders.
8. **Dune-covered share:** CAZRI literature describes dunes as covering about 58% of the desert; 58.5/41.5 is a common exam-note split.
9. **Barchan:** Crescent-shaped, horns point downwind; usually more mobile under favourable wind/sand conditions.
10. **Parabolic dune:** U-shaped, often vegetation-stabilised; parabolic/coalesced parabolic dunes are prominent in Rajasthan desert literature. Avoid “present in every district” as an absolute.
11. **Akal Wood Fossil Park:** Near Jaisalmer; fossils commonly dated to approximately 180 million years (Jurassic). Fossilised forest evidence does not prove a specific “equatorial rainforest” claim.
12. **Lathi Formation:** Important groundwater unit in Jaisalmer; water quality varies. Saraswati paleo-channel links should be framed cautiously.
13. **Johad:** Rainwater-harvesting pond/reservoir, not a drilled well.
14. **Ghaggar–Hakra:** Modern ephemeral river system; proposed identification with Saraswati/Drishadvati is debated.
15. **Aravalli trend:** Broadly south-west to north-east.
16. **Aravalli length:** Rajasthan Foundation says about 692 km for the range; ~550 km within Rajasthan is common textbook shorthand and depends on endpoints.
17. **Highest peak:** Guru Shikhar, 1,722 m. Some older sources quote 1,727 m using a different summit reference.
18. **Northern Aravalli:** Raghunathgarh (~1,055 m) is commonly taught as its highest peak; other sources also highlight Malkhet and Lohargal.
19. **Plateau terminology:** Oriya/Udia and Bhorat elevations are common exam conventions; exact boundaries/heights need map-specific support.
20. **Chhappan terms:** Chhappan ka Maidan is in southern Rajasthan in the Mahi basin; Chhappan ki Pahadiyan are associated with Siwana in western Rajasthan.
21. **Kobar/Banka Patti:** Regional name linked to fluoride-affected groundwater, not a formally defined geological layer.
22. **Eastern Plains:** Principally riverine/alluvial but not a uniform flat sediment blanket.
23. **Chambal badlands:** Result from gully/ravine erosion; avoid unsupported claims of a uniquely exact 4,500 sq km extent.
24. **Ramgarh Crater:** Baran district, Kishanganj tehsil; state-declared National Geo-Heritage Site in 2024; estimated 165 Ma, ~3.2 km diameter per Gazette.
25. **Current district names:** Rajasthan’s state portal and 2026 government pages list 41 districts. Older sources may refer to previous parent districts; regional geography itself does not change with administrative reorganisation.

## 8. Practice MCQs

### Q1. Which statement about the fourfold physiographic division is most accurate?

A. It is a universally binding geological taxonomy with fixed boundaries.  
B. It is a commonly used Rajasthan geography scheme, and area shares may vary by classification.  
C. It divides Rajasthan by linguistic dialect alone.  
D. It is identical to the current district map.

**Answer: B.** It is useful for exams, but the official Rajasthan Foundation overview and coaching tables do not give identical area shares for every division. [1]

### Q2. Which geological pairing is incorrect?

A. Aravalli belt — ancient Precambrian rocks  
B. Vindhyan succession — sedimentary formations  
C. Deccan Traps — basaltic lava flows  
D. Vindhyan Supergroup — basaltic lava plateau throughout Hadoti

**Answer: D.** Vindhyan formations are chiefly sedimentary; Deccan Trap basalt occurs in parts of south-eastern Rajasthan.

### Q3. The Great Boundary Fault is best described as:

## 9. RPSC RAS Mains Answer Frameworks

### Q1. Describe the four major physiographic divisions of Rajasthan. (5 marks)

Rajasthan is commonly divided into the Western Sandy Plain, Aravalli Range and hilly region, Eastern Plains and south-eastern Plateau/Hadoti. The west is dominated by arid to semi-arid conditions, aeolian dunes and inland drainage. The Aravallis are an ancient, deeply denuded mountain belt and influence drainage. Eastern plains contain riverine/alluvial surfaces and Chambal ravines. Hadoti is a mixed plateau region with Vindhyan sedimentary scarps and areas of Deccan basalt. Area percentages vary by the classification used.

### Q2. Explain the origin and distribution of sand dunes in the Thar. (5 marks)

Wind transports and deposits sand, creating barchan, transverse, longitudinal, parabolic, star and shrub-coppice dunes. Their form depends on wind direction, sand supply, vegetation and stability. Barchans are crescent-shaped with downwind-pointing horns and can be mobile. Longitudinal dunes form elongated ridges; transverse dunes are broadly perpendicular to dominant winds. Parabolic dunes are commonly vegetation-anchored and prominent in Rajasthan desert studies. Dune mobility and desertification risk vary by site and land management.

### Q3. Discuss the Great Boundary Fault and its regional significance. (5 marks)

The Great Boundary Fault is a major structural lineament in south-eastern Rajasthan near the contact of older basement/fold-belt rocks and Vindhyan sedimentary rocks. It is important for reconstructing the tectonic evolution of the region and is associated with complex fault rocks and reactivation. Geological interpretations of its original movement and present geometry vary, so it should not be described uncritically as an 800-km suture zone. Its geomorphic expression contributes to the relief contrast near the Hadoti margin.

### Q4. Explain the ecological and geographical significance of the Aravalli Range. (10 marks)

The Aravallis are an ancient Precambrian mountain belt trending broadly southwest–northeast across Rajasthan. They create a major topographic contrast between the arid northwest and the more diversified east/southeast; influence the drainage divide between west-flowing and east-flowing systems; support mineral resources, forests and tribal livelihoods; and include the state’s highest peak, Guru Shikhar (1,722 m). The range’s alignment broadly parallel to Arabian Sea monsoon flow means it does not form a complete orographic barrier, so regional rainfall is controlled by several factors. Unregulated mining, fragmentation and degradation threaten biodiversity, slopes and watersheds.

### Q5. Describe the Western Sandy Plain and explain its major sub-regions. (10 marks)

The Western Sandy Plain is Rajasthan’s largest physical division. Its landforms include dunes, sandy sheets, gravelly and rocky surfaces, inland drainage basins and saline depressions. A common teaching framework distinguishes the more arid Marusthali, often associated with rainfall below about 25 cm, from a semi-arid Bagar transition of roughly 25–50 cm, but isohyets are climatic guides rather than fixed lines. The Luni/Godwar tract drains southwest; Shekhawati includes internal drainage; and the northern Ghaggar plain is seasonally influenced by the Ghaggar–Hakra system. Groundwater units such as the Lathi Formation are important but local in extent and water quality.

### Q6. Compare the Eastern Plains with the Hadoti Plateau. (10 marks)

The Eastern Plains are shaped substantially by rivers and alluvial deposition and include the Banas–Banganga and Chambal systems. The Chambal basin contains deeply dissected ravines produced by gully erosion. Hadoti is a south-eastern plateau region with both Vindhyan sedimentary scarps and Deccan Trap basalt in different localities. Basaltic weathering can support black soils, while sandstone landscapes produce scarps and stepped relief. The two divisions differ in geology, soils, relief and drainage, but both are internally varied. Current administrative boundaries should not be mistaken for physiographic boundaries.
