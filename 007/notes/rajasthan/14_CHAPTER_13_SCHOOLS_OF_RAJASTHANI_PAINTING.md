# Chapter 13 — Schools of Rajasthani Painting

**For:** RPSC RAS Prelims and Mains and Rajasthan state examinations  
**Purpose:** A reliable, revision-friendly core source with distinctions between documented works, standard exam conventions and disputed attribution.

> **How to use this chapter:** For a painting, learn the centre/school, approximate date, patron or context, artist if documented, subject and one or two visual features. Do not turn every familiar exam phrase into a universal art-historical rule. “First”, “only”, “exclusive”, “world’s finest” and precise artist attributions require stronger evidence than a general style description.

## 1. What do we mean by “Rajasthani painting”?

Rajasthani painting is not a single, uniform school. It is a group of related court, devotional, manuscript and mural traditions associated with princely states, thikanas, religious centres and merchant towns across present-day Rajasthan and neighbouring regions. It developed through interaction among Western Indian/Jain manuscript traditions, local drawing and colour practices, Mughal ateliers, Bhakti movements, court patronage and regional exchanges.

In 1916, Ananda K. Coomaraswamy published *Rajput Painting*, an influential early framework for discussing these paintings. “Rajput painting” was a broad art-historical label, not a precise synonym for all painting made within modern Rajasthan. It could include painting from Rajasthan as well as Pahari and Malwa traditions. Later scholarship increasingly used regional or stylistic names, such as Mewar, Marwar/Jodhpur, Bikaner, Kishangarh, Jaipur, Bundi and Kota. The labels overlap: some teaching sources group styles into broad regional families, while museum catalogues treat several court traditions as distinct schools.

**Important classification rule:** The four-part grouping—Mewar, Marwar, Dhundhar and Hadoti—is a useful RAS revision framework, but it is not the only scholarly taxonomy. The NCERT chapter *The Rajasthani Schools of Painting* lists several centres separately, including Mewar, Bundi, Kota, Jaipur, Bikaner, Kishangarh, Jodhpur/Marwar, Malwa and Sirohi. Do not assume that Kishangarh or Bikaner is always classified only as a subordinate branch of Marwar, or that every regional centre fits neatly into one of four boxes. See [NCERT, *An Introduction to Indian Art—Part II*, “The Rajasthani Schools of Painting”](https://www.ncertbooks.net/textbook/pdf/lefa102.pdf).

## 2. Mewar painting: early manuscripts, Chavand and Udaipur

Mewar is one of the most important early centres in the history of Rajasthani painting. Its surviving record includes illustrated Jain manuscripts, the Chavand Ragamala of 1605, and major 17th-century Udaipur court series. Early surviving works are not a complete record of all painting once produced; the loss of manuscripts and court material means claims about the absolute beginning of the tradition should be cautious.

### 2.1 Early illustrated manuscripts

**Shravaka Pratikramana Sutra Churni** (also transliterated in scholarship as *Savaga Padikamana Satta Chunni*) is dated **1260 CE** and associated with Aghata/Ahar in the Mewar region during the rule of Guhila king Tejasimha. It is an important early dated illustrated Jain manuscript and often appears in Rajasthan art-history notes as the earliest dated example of the regional tradition. The precise modern phrase “earliest Rajasthani painting” is a historiographical shorthand: it means an early surviving dated illustrated manuscript, not proof that no painting existed earlier.

A second important manuscript is the **Supasanahachariyam**, dated **Vikrama Samvat 1479–1480 / 1422–23 CE** in its colophon and associated with the Medapata region during the reign of Rana Mokal. It is valuable for understanding the continuation and transformation of Western Indian Jain manuscript conventions.

The Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts’ survey, [“Art Gallery: Mewar School of Painting”](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/36456.pdf), discusses both manuscripts and the Chavand Ragamala, and is a useful starting point for exam revision. Where artist names or manuscript locations differ across secondary sources, prefer the colophon or a museum/catalogue record over a repeated coaching-note assertion.

### 2.2 Chavand Ragamala, 1605

The **Chavand Ragamala** is a key dated landmark in early 17th-century Mewar painting. A set of works dated **1605** is associated with Chavand and the reign of Maharana Amar Singh I. The Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art catalogues its *Shri Raga, from the Chawand Ragamala* as painted by **Nasiruddin**, at Chawand, in 1605, during Amar Singh’s reign. See the [Smithsonian collection entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:fsg_F1991.1).

Some exam notes give a precise completion date of **10 April 1605** and alternate spellings such as *Nisardi*, *Nisaruddin* or *Nasiruddin*. The robust, directly catalogued core is: **Chavand; 1605; Nasiruddin; Mewar court; reign of Amar Singh I.** Use a day-and-month date only when the question follows a specific state textbook that supplies it.

Common stylistic descriptions include bold colour, relatively direct composition, clearly separated planes, and a strong relation to earlier indigenous manuscript idioms. Avoid the absolute claim that it is completely “pre-Mughal”: Mewar’s visual culture evolved through multiple contacts, and one dated set cannot represent every work made at the court.

### 2.3 Udaipur court painting and Sahibdin

Painting at Udaipur reached a celebrated phase under **Maharana Jagat Singh I (1628–1652)**. A royal painting workshop is associated in Rajasthani art-history literature with the name **Chiteron ki Ovari** (also written *Chiteron ri Ovari*), with *Tasveeraan ro Karkhano* appearing as a popular explanatory phrase. Treat these as workshop/court terminology, not necessarily a modern, formally constituted department with a single founding document.

**Sahibdin** is a leading Mewar painter associated with significant illustrated religious and literary series, including Ragamala, *Gita Govinda*, *Rasikapriya* and Ramayana material. **Manohar** is another name associated with Mewar’s 17th-century painting. For study purposes, connect Sahibdin primarily with Mewar/Udaipur, not with Jaipur’s Sahibram.

**Features commonly associated with Mewar painting** include strong reds and yellows, clear contours, compact figures, emphatic eyes, landscape elements such as hills and trees, and narrative scenes from epics, devotional poetry and courtly life. These features are tendencies, not a checklist that every work must satisfy.

### 2.4 Nathdwara, Shrinathji and Pichhwai

The establishment of Shrinathji’s shrine at **Nathdwara** in the late 17th century, conventionally dated **1672**, supported a distinctive devotional painting culture associated with **Pushtimarg**. The name *Nathdwara* is linked to the shrine and its religious community; the earlier installation site is often given as Sihad/Sinhad.

A **Pichhwai** is a painted textile—commonly cotton cloth—hung behind or around the image of Shrinathji in the shrine environment. Pichhwai images are not merely decorative backdrops: they form part of the visual and ritual programme of festivals, seasons and devotional services. Subjects may include Shrinathji raising Govardhan, cows, lotus ponds, trees, gopis, seasonal offerings and festivals such as Annakut or Sharad Purnima.

Nathdwara painting draws on the wider Mewar visual tradition but developed its own workshop practices and devotional iconography. Art historian Tryna Lyons’ study, *The Artists of Nathadwara: The Practice of Painting in Rajasthan* (Indiana University Press, 2004), documents artist families, sketchbooks and women’s participation. **Kamala and Ilaichi**, sisters in the Nathdwara painting world, are important examples of women artists whose work challenges the assumption that traditional Indian painting was always anonymous or exclusively male. Avoid claiming that every Pichhwai was made by them or that the field was exclusively female or male.

### 2.5 Devgarh, Shahpura and Phad

The Devgarh court tradition is associated with a thikana founded by **Rawat Dwarkadas Chundawat** in the late 17th century. It is frequently described as an artistic meeting point for Mewar, Marwar and Jaipur/Dhundhar elements. **Bagta** and **Chokha** are important artist names in discussions of Devgarh painting; spellings and the list of associated artists vary among publications. Art historian **Shridhar Andhare** played an important role in drawing scholarly attention to the Devgarh material.

Do not confuse **Shahpura court painting** with **Phad painting** simply because both are associated with places in the same broad region. **Phad** is a long painted cloth scroll used to narrate the stories of folk deities, notably Pabuji and Devnarayan. The performance involves specialist singer-priests (*bhopas* and, in the Devnarayan tradition, *bhopis*) and music. The Joshi painters of Shahpura are particularly associated with the modern practice and transmission of Phad painting. Artist **Shree Lal Joshi** is a major 20th-century figure; other family artists include later generations of practitioners.

## 3. Jodhpur/Marwar, Kishangarh and Bikaner

The term **Marwar** can refer to a broad region and dynastic-cultural world, while **Jodhpur painting** is a more specific court tradition. Kishangarh and Bikaner share regional relationships with Marwar but have sufficiently distinctive visual histories to be taught as separate schools in many art-history frameworks.

### 3.1 Jodhpur / Marwar

Jodhpur painting includes court portraits, historical and religious narratives, illustrated texts and works connected with the Nath tradition. Under Maharaja **Man Singh (r. 1803–1843)**, the court’s association with the Nath yogi **Ayas Devnath** supported a substantial body of manuscript painting. Works associated in standard Rajasthan notes with this cultural milieu include *Nath Charitra*, *Shiva Purana*, *Durgacharit* and *Siddha Siddhanta Paddhati*.

Commonly described visual features include forceful colour, red and yellow ochres, energetic lines, turban and dress details, courtly portraiture and landscape/celestial effects. The labels “almond-shaped eyes,” “high pointed turban” or “spiralling clouds” are helpful shorthand, but should be treated as recurring tendencies rather than reliable standalone attribution tests.

### 3.2 Kishangarh and Bani Thani

**Kishangarh** should be learned as a distinctive style, not merely as a generic Jodhpur sub-style. Its courtly painting flourished in the 18th century, especially under **Savant Singh** (also written Sawant Singh), who wrote devotional poetry under the name **Nagari Das**. His devotional world was strongly shaped by Radha-Krishna imagery and Vaishnava bhakti.

**Nihal Chand** is the painter most closely associated in standard art history with Kishangarh’s idealized physiognomy. Its common visual markers include elongated crescent or lotus-like eyes, arched brows, a long pointed nose, a delicate chin, slender bodies, gauzy veils and lyrical, expansive landscapes in soft whites, pinks, blues and greens. The figures of Sawant Singh and Bani Thani are frequently interpreted as visual counterparts of Krishna and Radha.

**Bani Thani** is a celebrated portrait and cultural figure associated with Kishangarh. Standard Indian art textbooks credit Nihal Chand with shaping the iconic image, and the NCERT chapter explains its relationship to Sawant Singh’s poetry and idealized Radha-Krishna imagery. There is, however, a useful catalogue-level nuance: the [National Museum, New Delhi record](https://museumsofindia.gov.in/repository/record/nat_del-63-812-4917) for its Bani Thani painting describes it as an 18th-century Kishangarh work but lists the **main artist as “not known.”** The safest formulation is: **Bani Thani is conventionally attributed to or associated with Nihal Chand; a particular National Museum object record does not assign a named artist.**

The popular comparison **“Mona Lisa of India”** is widely attached to Bani Thani. Because the exact origin of this epithet is less secure than the object’s museum record and stylistic attribution, treat it as a popular label rather than a documented quote unless the exam asks for the conventional association. The 20-paise stamp usually dated **1973** is likewise a standard exam fact; use it as textbook convention unless you have verified the particular postal issue catalogue.

The work *Chandni Raat ki Sangeet Goshthi* is often attributed in Rajasthan exam material to **Amarchand**. It may be useful as a conventional artist-work pairing, but the claim should not be extended without a collection record identifying the exact painting.

### 3.3 Bikaner: Mughal and Deccani influences

Bikaner painting developed through local court patronage, close links with Mughal imperial culture and the service of Bikaner rulers in the Deccan. Its art can be unusually refined in its drawing, textiles, portraiture, gold accents and atmospheric landscapes. The court under **Maharaja Anup Singh** is an important phase, but date ranges for his reign and accession vary among summaries; a safe examination association is **Anup Singh’s late-17th-century patronage**.

Bikaner is particularly useful for understanding artists as named professionals. A Smithsonian catalogue example, *Raja Sujan Singh of Bikaner smoking a hookah*, credits **Ustad Ahmad**, identifies Bikaner as the school, and records a reverse inscription giving the work as a likeness of the ruler and naming the artist and his father. See the [Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art record](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:fsg_S2018.1.84). This proves that signed/inscribed works exist and are historically valuable; it does **not** prove that every Bikaner painting is signed or that all artists always recorded complete genealogies and dates.

Two craft traditions often taught in connection with Bikaner are:

- **Usta art:** Embossed and painted decoration, including gold and coloured work on treated camel hide and other supports. The [Government of Rajasthan’s Handmade in Rajasthan page on Usta Kala](https://handmadeinrajasthan.rajasthan.gov.in/content/industries/handmadeinrajasthandepartment/artandcraft/leather/ustakala.html) describes its leather and camel-hide practice.
- **Mathairan / Matheirana painting:** A local painting tradition associated with Bikaner and artist families. Exam sources list artists such as Chandulal, Munnalal, Mukund and related practitioners. The precise community label and medium vary in secondary notes, so do not repeat the source chapter’s unsupported statement that it was simply “religious painting practised by the local Jain community” as a universal fact. Check a specialised Bikaner art history if that exact community affiliation is asked.

Bikaner painting displays **Mughal and Deccani influence**, not a lack of regional identity. The Smithsonian’s description of the Sujan Singh portrait explicitly links its colour palette and landscape atmosphere with Deccani court art.

### 3.4 Nagaur and Jaisalmer: local centres, not equally documented “schools”

Exam books sometimes describe **Nagaur** as having muted or chalky colour palettes and **Jaisalmer** as retaining strong folk conventions, including the romance of **Moomal**. Such descriptions can assist recognition, but the surviving and published corpus is less uniform than for Mewar, Kishangarh, Bikaner, Bundi or Kota. Avoid treating every painting in a district as one stable style or assigning an individual work solely from a palette.

## 4. Dhundhar: Amer, Jaipur, Alwar, Shekhawati and Uniara

Dhundhar is a useful broad regional label for Jaipur/Amer and nearby centres. Mughal contact, court administration, ateliers and royal patronage helped shape portraiture and manuscript painting, but the mural tradition of Shekhawati and local traditions such as Uniara need to be distinguished from the Jaipur court atelier.

### 4.1 Amer and Jaipur

**Amer** was a major Kachhwaha court before Jaipur became the capital in 1727. Its painting history reflects courtly contact with the Mughal world, illustrated manuscripts and palace murals. The original chapter’s specific claim that imperial artists illustrated the **Razmnama at Amer in 1588** and that the surviving manuscript is held by the City Palace Museum needs direct catalogue evidence; it has therefore been removed from the memorisation core.

**Jaipur** developed a sophisticated court painting tradition after the capital was founded by **Sawai Jai Singh II (1727)**. Its royal workshop is associated with the **Suratkhana**, which produced court portraits, religious illustrations, Ragamala paintings and manuscripts. Under rulers including **Sawai Pratap Singh (r. 1778–1803)**, the arts received significant patronage.

**Sahibram** (not Sahibdin) is a key name associated with Jaipur/Dhundhar painting and life-size or near-life-size royal portraiture (*aadmakad chitra*). Keep this distinction fixed for exams:

- **Sahibdin** → Mewar/Udaipur, 17th century, illustrated devotional and literary series.
- **Sahibram** → Jaipur/Dhundhar, 18th century, court portraiture including large-scale images.

The famous four gateways of Pritam Niwas Chowk at Jaipur City Palace are primarily architectural subjects; they should not be mixed into a painting-school answer unless the question explicitly links visual culture and palace design.

### 4.2 Alwar

The Alwar school developed after the establishment of Alwar as an independent state in the late 18th century. It is associated with court manuscripts, fine portraiture and paintings on ivory. The illustrated **Gulistan** of Saadi, associated with **Baldev** and **Ghulam Ali** under Maharaja **Vinay Singh**, is a common exam reference.

Ivory miniature painting was practised more widely than Alwar alone, so it is unsafe to call it an exclusive Alwar invention. Alwar’s court collection includes portraits and courtly subjects, including depictions of performers and courtesans. The phrase “the only Rajput court to commission courtesan portraits” is too absolute without a comparative study across collections.

### 4.3 Shekhawati mural tradition

Shekhawati’s painted merchant havelis are a regional mural and architectural tradition tied to merchant patronage, trading routes and townscapes. Their paintings range from religious themes and folk stories to rulers, patrons, daily life and—especially in late 19th- and early 20th-century work—railways, motor cars, gramophones and other contemporary technologies.

The region’s painted buildings are often called an **“open-air art gallery,”** a useful popular epithet rather than an official ranking. The UNESCO World Heritage Centre added **The Painted Haveli Towns of Shekhawati** to India’s **Tentative List on 1 June 2026**. Tentative List status is not the same as inscription on the World Heritage List. The official entry describes a serial nomination of historic ensembles in 14 towns and discusses mural techniques, mercantile patronage, urban form and water infrastructure. See [UNESCO Tentative List entry 7028](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/7028/).

Shekhawati murals used more than one technique. In **fresco buono**, pigments are applied to suitable fresh lime plaster and become integrated as the plaster carbonates; in **fresco secco**, paint is applied to a dry surface and requires a different binding relationship. Actual historic wall paintings may use complex and changing mixtures, including imported pigments; a simple list of mineral pigments should not be treated as a laboratory analysis of a specific haveli.

### 4.4 Uniara

Uniara is commonly discussed as a local court centre whose painting combines elements associated with Dhundhar and Hadoti. Names such as **Dheema**, **Mir Baksh**, Kashi and Ramlakhan appear in regional exam literature. Because spellings and attributions vary, treat these as conventional names unless a particular work or inscription is cited.

## 5. Hadoti: Bundi and Kota

The Hadoti region’s painting traditions are especially recognised for natural settings, dense vegetation, wildlife, rain and courtly hunting. **Bundi** and **Kota** are close historically but should be distinguished by their strongest recurring subjects.

### 5.1 Bundi: landscape, flora, fauna and monsoon

Bundi painting often uses lush trees, plantain and banyan foliage, lotus-filled water, birds, animals, hills and dramatic monsoon skies. Devotional and courtly subjects—especially Krishna-related themes, court processions and romantic episodes—also appear. “School of Birds and Animals” is a familiar exam epithet, but Bundi is not limited to animal pictures.

**Chitrashala / Rang Vilas** in the Garh Palace complex is celebrated for its wall paintings. Standard Rajasthan notes associate the gallery’s major phase with **Maharao Ummed Singh** in the 18th century. Exact construction and completion phases should not be compressed into a single date unless supported by a building record. When asked to identify Bundi, think of intricate natural settings, bright colour, fine decorative detail, romance and devotional scenes.

### 5.2 Kota: royal hunting and animal movement

Kota painting is especially associated with dramatic **shikar** scenes and the depiction of animals in rugged, textured landscapes. Paintings of **Maharao Umed Singh** hunting tigers are important examples. The Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art catalogues *Maharao Umed Singh hunting tigers* as a Kota-school painting by **Hans Raj Joshi**, dated **1787**, with a dated inscription on the reverse. The catalogue notes that Kota artists produced extraordinary hunting scenes from around 1660 and highlights animal movement, textured landscapes and the changing treatment of colour. See the [Smithsonian catalogue entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:fsg_S2018.1.39).

The claim that **royal women hunting big game is the exclusive hallmark of Kota painting** is too strong. Hunting is a major diagnostic subject, and women may be represented in active roles in particular works, but an “exclusive” claim needs a wide comparative corpus. For exams, memorise **Kota = royal hunt / shikar, dynamic animals and Chambal landscape**, not an absolute rule about women.

**Dalu** is frequently paired in Rajasthani exam literature with a Kota Ragamala dated 1768. Keep the pairing as a textbook convention if it appears in an official syllabus source, but verify the exact series and attribution against a museum catalogue before using it as an archival claim.

## 6. Materials, pigments and technical vocabulary

Traditional Indian miniature and mural painting required prepared supports, finely ground pigments, brush control and multiple surface-finishing steps. Exact recipes differed by period, workshop and intended use.

### 6.1 Supports and techniques

- **Vasli:** Layered paper support made by pasting sheets together, then pressing and polishing the surface for painting.
- **Palm leaf:** Used for early Jain manuscript illustration; its narrow format influenced layout, text and figure placement.
- **Paper:** Common for later miniature paintings, often with opaque watercolour and gold.
- **Cloth:** Used for Pichhwai paintings and Phad scrolls, but the two traditions have different ritual and performance functions.
- **Ivory:** Used for small-scale portrait miniatures in some court and commercial settings. Its use was not unique to Alwar.
- **Wall plaster:** Shekhawati and palace murals used lime-based plaster systems and different fresco or secco approaches.
- **Camel hide and other surfaces:** Used in Usta craft, in which embossing, painting and gilding create a dimensional decorative effect.

### 6.2 Pigments and colour language

Rajasthani painting uses a range of colours whose visual appearance depends on the pigment, binder, support and preservation condition. Traditional technical terms commonly found in notes include:

| Term | Common association | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Hinglu** | Vermilion/cinnabar-like red | Historic recipe and actual composition may differ; cinnabar contains mercury. |
| **Geru** | Red ochre/earth red | Mineral earth pigment, not the same substance as cinnabar. |
| **Hartal** | Orpiment/yellow arsenic sulphide in many technical glossaries | Toxic; do not treat pigment names as safe handling instructions. |
| **Lajward** | Lapis lazuli blue | Cost and availability varied; some works use other blues. |
| **Neel** | Indigo | Plant-derived blue, with properties unlike mineral ultramarine. |
| **Dana-farang / malachite-related greens** | Green mineral pigment in some glossaries | Transliteration and regional use vary; identify a specific pigment only from technical analysis. |
| **Kajol** | Lampblack/soot black | Common black pigment association. |
| **Safeda / Khadia** | White pigment or chalk terminology in regional craft accounts | The exact material depends on the source and period. |
| **Varq / gold leaf** | Applied metal leaf | Use of gold does not mean every gold-looking area is actual gold. |
| **Halkari** | Often used in Rajasthan art notes for a liquid gold effect or gold wash | Definition and spelling need local technical context. |

**Safety and factual note:** Historic pigments may include highly toxic compounds such as mercury-, arsenic- or lead-based materials. This table is a vocabulary guide, not a conservation recipe.

### 6.3 Common subject genres

- **Ragamala:** A visual interpretation of ragas and raginis, linking musical modes with human figures, moods, seasons, times and poetic situations. Numbering schemes vary across traditions.
- **Barahmasa:** A twelve-month cycle linking seasonal change with human emotion, often separation and longing.
- **Nayika-bheda:** Classifications of heroines according to romantic situation and emotional state.
- **Bhagavata Purana / Krishna Lila:** Devotional narratives, especially important in Vaishnava court and temple settings.
- **Ramayana / Mahabharata:** Large illustrated series, sometimes produced over years by workshops with multiple hands.
- **Portraiture:** Images of rulers, nobles, courtiers, ascetics and performers.
- **Shikar:** Hunting scenes that can simultaneously document courtly spectacle, royal identity, landscape, animal behaviour and political power.
- **Pichhwai / Phad:** Painted cloth forms with different ritual and narrative roles; do not confuse these with miniature albums.

## 8. High-yield examination traps

1. **Sahibdin vs Sahibram:** Sahibdin is associated with Mewar/Udaipur; Sahibram with Jaipur/Dhundhar portraiture.
2. **Ragamala at Chavand:** Remember **1605, Nasiruddin, Chavand, reign of Amar Singh I**. Avoid claiming an exact day unless your exam text specifies it.
3. **Early illustrated manuscript:** *Shravaka Pratikramana Sutra Churni* — 1260 CE, Ahar/Aghata, reign of Tejasimha.
4. **Supasanahachariyam:** 1422–23 CE / VS 1479–80; associated with Medapata during Rana Mokal’s reign.
5. **Pichhwai:** A painted cloth hung behind/around Shrinathji’s image; it is not the same as Phad.
6. **Nathdwara women artists:** Kamala and Ilaichi are documented in scholarship on Nathdwara painting, but individual attributions require evidence.
7. **Bani Thani:** Associated conventionally with Nihal Chand and Kishangarh; the National Museum object record lists the artist as unknown.
8. **Kishangarh classification:** Often treated as a distinct school; do not automatically subsume it under Jodhpur/Marwar.
9. **Bikaner inscriptions:** Some works give artist names, parentage or dates; “every Bikaner artist signed every work” is false as a universal statement.
10. **Bikaner external influence:** Both Mughal and Deccani interactions matter; the style cannot be reduced to one influence.
11. **Usta art:** Associated with Bikaner, including painted and embossed camel-hide work; not synonymous with miniature painting as a whole.
12. **Mathairan painting:** Avoid unsupported assertions about its single community identity or exclusive medium without a specialised source.
13. **Alwar ivory:** Ivory miniature painting is associated with Alwar in exam literature but was not exclusive to Alwar.
14. **Shekhawati:** Painted-haveli tradition is primarily a mural and townscape tradition, not simply a court-miniature sub-school.
15. **UNESCO status:** The Painted Haveli Towns of Shekhawati joined the Tentative List on **1 June 2026**; this is not World Heritage inscription.
16. **Bundi vs Kota:** Bundi is strongly associated with dense natural scenery, birds, animals and monsoon; Kota with dynamic royal hunting and animal movement.
17. **Women hunting:** Some Kota paintings may depict women hunting, but do not claim this motif is exclusive to Kota without comparative evidence.
18. **Coomaraswamy:** Published *Rajput Painting* in 1916; this is a key historiographical milestone, not necessarily the first systematic study of every branch of Indian painting.
19. **Four-school scheme:** Mewar, Marwar, Dhundhar and Hadoti is a helpful summary, not a universal classification. Other schools and centres should be learned separately where the syllabus demands them.
20. **Pigment terminology:** Regional pigment terms are useful vocabulary, not proof of the chemical composition of any specific painting.

## 10. Mains answer frameworks

### Q1. Explain the significance of the Chavand Ragamala in the history of Rajasthani painting. (2 marks)

The Chavand Ragamala is a key early dated Mewar painting set associated with **Chavand, 1605 CE, painter Nasiruddin, and the reign of Maharana Amar Singh I**. Its inscription and identifiable court context make it important for reconstructing early Rajasthani painting.

### Q2. Compare the principal features of Mewar and Kishangarh painting. (5 marks)

Mewar painting is noted for early Jain manuscript evidence, narrative works, expressive colour and the major Udaipur and Chavand traditions. Kishangarh developed a distinct 18th-century idiom under Savant Singh/Nagari Das and associated painters, especially Nihal Chand. Its characteristic elongated physiognomy and lyrical Radha-Krishna imagery are prominent. Kishangarh is often treated as a separate school rather than merely a subdivision of Marwar.

### Q3. Distinguish Pichhwai, Phad and Shekhawati mural painting. (5 marks)

- **Pichhwai:** Painted textile associated with Shrinathji worship at Nathdwara; its subjects and display relate to devotional seasons and festivals.
- **Phad:** Narrative cloth scroll associated with folk-deity traditions such as Pabuji and Devnarayan, used by *bhopas* in sung performance.
- **Shekhawati mural painting:** Frescoes and painted surfaces on merchant havelis and other buildings, recording religious, social, commercial and colonial-era imagery in a broader townscape.

They differ in patronage, support, function and modes of viewing; all should not be grouped indiscriminately as “miniature painting.”

### Q4. Discuss the role of regional patronage and cultural interaction in the development of Rajasthani painting. (10 marks)

Rajasthani painting developed through overlapping court, religious and mercantile environments rather than from a single uniform school. Mewar’s dated Jain manuscripts and Chavand Ragamala are major early records; Udaipur’s 17th-century court supported illustrated devotional series. Kishangarh developed idealized Radha-Krishna imagery linked to Savant Singh/Nagari Das and Nihal Chand. Bikaner’s painting reflects both Mughal and Deccani interactions, while Jaipur’s atelier supported court portraiture and manuscripts. Nathdwara’s Pichhwai tradition emerged from shrine practice; Phad retained a narrative-performance role; Shekhawati’s merchant havelis translated mercantile wealth into mural townscapes. Bundi and Kota used landscape differently, with nature and monsoon imagery prominent in Bundi and royal hunts prominent in Kota. These cases show regional identities shaped by geography, patronage, religion, workshop practice and exchange.

### Q5. Why must stylistic labels and artist attributions be used cautiously? (10 marks)

School names are useful for organising a large and diverse visual record, but they are not rigid biological categories. A painter might work across media or under different patrons; workshops involved multiple hands; artistic influence moved through courts and religious institutions; surviving collections are incomplete. Popular attributions may differ from modern museum catalogue entries—as with the National Museum’s Bani Thani record listing the artist as unknown despite the conventional link to Nihal Chand. Likewise, Bikaner has inscribed works, but this cannot prove that every painting is signed. A sound answer therefore separates a dated or inscribed object, a museum attribution, a conventional exam pairing and a popular epithet. This prevents false certainty without discarding useful revision conventions.
