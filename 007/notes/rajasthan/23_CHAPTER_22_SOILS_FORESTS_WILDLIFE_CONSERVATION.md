## 1. Soils of Rajasthan: Formation, Types and Management

Rajasthan's soils reflect differences in parent rock, rainfall, temperature, relief, drainage and land use. The broad pattern is a transition from sandy and calcareous soils in the arid west to varied alluvial, red/yellow, brown and clay-rich soils in the east, south and south-east. These are regional patterns, not sharp boundaries: soil properties can change substantially within a district or even a field.

### A. Regional soil groups used in Rajasthan geography

| Soil group | Broad distribution | Main characteristics | Agricultural / management significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Desert and sandy soils** (*मरुस्थलीय / रेतीली*) | Western and north-western Rajasthan; dunes and sandy plains | Coarse texture in many locations, low organic carbon and limited water-holding capacity; calcareous layers and salinity may occur locally | Bajra, moth bean, guar, fodder and other drought-adapted crops; windbreaks, residue retention and moisture conservation are important |
| **Brown and grey-brown soils** | Large parts of the semi-arid central and eastern tracts | Highly variable sandy-loam to loam textures; fertility depends on depth, organic matter, carbonate and moisture regime | Common mixed farming zone; balanced nutrients and erosion control are important |
| **Red and yellow / red-loam soils** | Parts of the southern and south-eastern uplands, including the Mewar–Vagad belt | Colour often reflects iron oxides; generally variable in depth and organic matter, especially on slopes | Maize and pulses are important in many rainfed areas; contour measures and organic matter management can reduce erosion and improve moisture retention |
| **Black and clay-rich soils** | Hadoti and adjoining south-eastern areas, including Kota, Bundi, Baran and Jhalawar | Fine texture; some profiles show strong shrink–swell behaviour, cracks when dry and stickiness when wet | Can retain substantial moisture but drain slowly; timely tillage, drainage and careful irrigation scheduling matter. Soybean, wheat, coriander and other locally suited crops occur in the region |
| **Alluvial soils** | River valleys, floodplains and eastern plains, including parts of the Banas, Chambal and Banganga systems | Deposited by rivers and local runoff; texture, depth, pH and fertility vary from site to site | Often agriculturally productive where water and drainage are suitable; do not assume all alluvium is neutral or uniformly fertile |
| **Saline, sodic and saline-sodic soils** | Localised inland depressions, arid plains and some poorly drained or canal-irrigated areas | Accumulation of soluble salts and/or excess exchangeable sodium; affected by groundwater quality, evaporation, drainage and irrigation practice | Management depends on the diagnosis: salt leaching and drainage for saline soils; calcium amendment such as gypsum plus leaching for sodic soils when recommended by soil testing |
| **Shallow, gravelly and hill soils** | Aravalli slopes, rocky uplands and parts of southern Rajasthan | Limited soil depth, stones and high erosion risk on steep or sparsely vegetated slopes | Grazing management, contour works, vegetative cover and watershed treatment are central to protection |

**Area-share caution:** “Sandy soil covers around 60% of Rajasthan” is a commonly repeated textbook approximation. Use it only when a question explicitly follows that convention; do not present it as a precise statewide soil survey statistic without naming the survey and its classification.

### B. Soil taxonomy: scientific orders versus regional labels

Regional names such as *desert soil*, *red-loam soil*, *black soil* and *alluvial soil* describe landscape or field properties. USDA Soil Taxonomy orders such as **Entisols, Aridisols, Inceptisols, Alfisols and Vertisols** are defined through diagnostic horizons, moisture regimes and other profile properties. The two schemes are **not interchangeable** and a whole district should not be assigned one order as if every soil profile were identical.

- **Entisols:** weakly developed soils with little or no diagnostic subsurface horizon; they can occur on young alluvium, dunes and other recently formed or repeatedly reworked surfaces.
- **Aridisols:** soils of arid moisture regimes, often with carbonate, gypsum, salt or other accumulations depending on local conditions. Arid Rajasthan contains both Aridisols and Entisols; neither term should be used as a synonym for all desert soils.
- **Inceptisols:** soils with some subsurface development but without the stronger diagnostic horizons required for more developed orders. They may occur on diverse landscapes, not only one fixed Aravalli belt.
- **Alfisols:** soils meeting the order's diagnostic horizon and base-status requirements. It is unsafe to label all eastern alluvial soils Alfisols or all Alfisols the state's “most fertile” soil.
- **Vertisols:** clay-rich soils with pronounced shrink–swell behaviour and characteristic cracks or slickensides. They are associated with parts of south-eastern Rajasthan, but their presence is determined by the soil profile, not district boundaries alone.

A published study of the **hot-arid region** reported substantial representation of both Entisols and Aridisols; this is a regional result, not a whole-state order-by-order census. For RAS, learn the regional pattern first and the diagnostic properties second.

### C. Saline versus sodic soils: a high-value distinction

| Problem | What it means | Typical management logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Saline soil** | Excess soluble salts raise electrical conductivity; sodium may or may not dominate the exchange complex | Where feasible, leach salts with suitable-quality water and provide drainage; amendment choice depends on the actual chemistry |
| **Sodic / alkali soil** | Excess exchangeable sodium damages soil structure; pH is often high | A calcium source such as **gypsum** may replace exchangeable sodium, followed by leaching and drainage; apply based on laboratory recommendation |
| **Saline-sodic soil** | High soluble salts and high exchangeable sodium coexist | Requires a site-specific sequence of amendment, leaching and drainage; treating it as ordinary saline soil can make the structure problem worse |
| **Acid soil** | Soil reaction is too acidic for the crop and local conditions | Lime may be used to raise pH, with the dose based on soil testing and crop requirements |

**Do not memorize “iron pyrites or rock phosphate treats all saline–alkaline soils.”** These are not universal substitutes for soil diagnosis, drainage, leaching and a properly calculated amendment.

### D. Soil erosion and degradation

1. **Wind erosion:** prominent in arid western districts where exposed, dry topsoil is moved by strong winds. It can bury fields, damage seedlings and contribute to dune movement.
2. **Sheet and rill erosion:** runoff removes thin layers of topsoil or cuts small channels on exposed slopes and cultivated land.
3. **Gully erosion and ravines:** concentrated flow incises soft material, most visibly in the Chambal basin. The resulting *beehad* / badland terrain reduces cultivable land and complicates settlement and transport.
4. **Salinisation and sodification:** may develop through saline groundwater, inadequate drainage, excessive irrigation or capillary rise in susceptible soils.
5. **Loss of organic matter and fertility:** repeated removal of crop residues, inadequate nutrient replacement and overgrazing can lower soil quality.

**Control measures:** shelterbelts and field windbreaks; dune stabilisation with suitable native vegetation; contour bunds, trenches and vegetative barriers; check dams and watershed treatment; controlled grazing; crop-residue and organic-matter management; drainage and scientifically managed irrigation.

### E. Soil Health Card Scheme

The national Soil Health Card Scheme was introduced by the Prime Minister at **Suratgarh, Rajasthan, on 19 February 2015**. The card reports soil nutrient status and provides recommendations for fertiliser application.

It includes **12 parameters**:

- **Macronutrients:** nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), sulphur (S).
- **Micronutrients:** zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), boron (B).
- **Other soil properties:** pH, electrical conductivity (EC), and organic carbon (OC).

The last three are not “physical parameters” in the same sense as soil texture or bulk density. EC helps assess salinity; pH indicates acidity/alkalinity; OC is a key indicator of soil organic matter status.

## 2. Forest Resources: Reading the Statistics Correctly

### A. Three measures that must not be conflated

- **Recorded Forest Area (RFA):** land recorded/notified as forest in government records. It is a legal/administrative measure; not every hectare necessarily carries dense tree cover.
- **Forest cover:** land of at least one hectare with tree canopy density at or above the FSI threshold, irrespective of ownership or legal status. It is a remote-sensing measure.
- **Tree cover:** tree patches outside the forest-cover definition, including smaller patches and scattered trees. It is not the same as forest cover.

**Latest FSI benchmark located in this audit: ISFR 2023.** The detailed state chapter reports the following figures for Rajasthan:

| Measure / canopy class | ISFR 2023 area (km²) | Approx. share of state geographical area | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Very Dense Forest (VDF; canopy ≥70%) | 223.20 | 0.07% | Smallest canopy-density class |
| Moderately Dense Forest (MDF; 40% to <70%) | 4,237.41 | 1.24% | Intermediate canopy density |
| Open Forest (OF; 10% to <40%) | 12,087.60 | 3.53% | Largest forest-cover class by area |
| **Total forest cover** | **16,548.21** | **4.84%** | FSI forest-cover measure |
| Scrub (below forest-cover canopy threshold) | 5,476.75 | 1.60% | Mapped separately; not included in total forest cover |
| **Tree cover outside forest cover** | **10,841.12** | **3.17%** | Separate FSI category |
| **Forest cover + tree cover** | **27,389.33** | **about 8.00%** | Combined green-cover measure used in ISFR state table |

The earlier frequently quoted combination of **16,655 km² forest cover + 8,733 km² tree cover = 25,388 km²** belongs to an older assessment, not ISFR 2023. Do not mix figures from different ISFR editions in a single table.

### B. Legal forest area: latest state administrative table

| Legal category | Area (km²) | Share of tabulated total |
|---|---:|---:|

**Legal nuance:** reserved, protected and unclassed are legal/administrative categories under forest law and notifications. Do not reduce them to the unsupported absolutes “all activity is banned in every reserved forest” or “grazing is automatically permitted in every protected forest.” Rights, restrictions and permitted uses depend on the relevant legal status, settlement and applicable orders.

### C. Policy framework

- **National Forest Policy, 1988:** states a national policy goal of at least **one-third of the country's total land area under forest or tree cover**, and two-thirds in hill and mountainous regions. This is a policy objective, not a simple statutory order that automatically converts land to forest.
- **Rajasthan Forest Policy, 2023:** sets the policy aim of increasing vegetal cover to **20% of the state's geographical area by 2040**. This is a target, not a statement of present cover.
- Forest conservation, wildlife protection, afforestation, agroforestry, community participation, ecological restoration and climate adaptation are related but distinct policy tools; plantation count alone is not a sufficient measure of ecosystem recovery.

### D. District-wise statistics: use the report geography

The ISFR 2023 Rajasthan table gives **Udaipur** the largest district forest-cover area in its published calculation (**2,766.30 km²**) and the highest listed district share (**23.60%**). Among the legacy district entries, **Churu** has the smallest forest-cover area (**62.73 km²**) and the lowest share (**0.45%**).

These rankings are tied to the **district and calculated-area framework used by ISFR 2023**. Rajasthan's district map has since been reorganised; new districts such as Balotra, Phalodi, Deeg and others should not be assigned old figures without a boundary-matched calculation. For current district administration questions, use the current district names; for ISFR data questions, quote the report year and its geographic basis.

## 3. Natural Vegetation of Rajasthan

Vegetation follows a broad aridity and relief gradient: thorn scrub and grassland dominate much of the arid west, dry deciduous forests occur across the Aravalli hills and parts of the east and south-east, and teak and more moisture-demanding mixed vegetation are concentrated in the southern/south-eastern wetter tracts. Exact boundaries are gradual, affected by rainfall, soil, slope, disturbance and past land use.

### A. Major vegetation groups and representative species

| Broad group / habitat | Distribution pattern | Representative species and exam relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Tropical thorn / arid scrub** | Western and north-western arid and semi-arid plains | Khejri (*Prosopis cineraria*), Rohida (*Tecomella undulata*), Kair (*Capparis decidua*), Ber (*Ziziphus* spp.), Phog (*Calligonum polygonoides*), Babool; Sewan and other native grasses matter to desert pastoralism |
| **Tropical dry deciduous and Dhok-dominated forest** | Aravalli slopes/foothills and parts of eastern and south-eastern Rajasthan | Dhok (*Anogeissus pendula*) is a characteristic and widespread species; its distribution should not be converted into an unsupported exact percentage of the entire forest area |
| **Mixed dry deciduous forest** | Southern Aravallis, Mewar–Vagad and adjoining uplands | Salar/Salai (*Boswellia serrata*), Dhak/Palash (*Butea monosperma*), Khair (*Senegalia catechu*, often given in older sources as *Acacia catechu*), Tendu (*Diospyros melanoxylon*), Mahua (*Madhuca longifolia*; older sources may use *Madhuca indica*) and bamboo in suitable tracts |
| **Dry teak forest** | Wetter pockets of southern and south-eastern Rajasthan, especially parts of Banswara and Dungarpur and adjoining areas | Teak (*Tectona grandis*) occurs where rainfall, soil and local conditions permit; it is not a uniform district-wide cover |
| **Mount Abu montane vegetation** | Higher, locally wetter elevations of the Mount Abu massif | Distinct montane and broad-leaved elements occur due to altitude and orographic rainfall. Avoid “only forest type in all Rajasthan at exactly X metres” claims without a defined classification |
| **Grasslands and open habitats** | Western desert and semi-arid grazing landscapes; saline flats and seasonal wetlands in particular sites | Sewan grassland and other native grasses support livestock and wildlife. Open habitat is itself a conservation value and should not automatically be labelled degraded forest |

### B. Species facts: correct the common confusions

- **Dhok — *Anogeissus pendula*:** characteristic dry deciduous tree of rocky, dry hills; common in the Aravalli–eastern/south-eastern forest belt.
- **Khejri — *Prosopis cineraria*:** a key tree of arid agroforestry, valued for shade, fodder, soil protection and edible pods (*sangri*). It is Rajasthan's state tree.
- **Rohida — *Tecomella undulata*:** dry-region timber tree known as *Marushobha* / the “Teak of Marwar” in popular usage; Rajasthan's state flower is referred to as Rohida's flower.
- **Dhak/Palash — *Butea monosperma*:** the “Flame of the Forest” due to its striking flowers.
- **Khair — *Senegalia catechu* / older name *Acacia catechu*:** heartwood is used to prepare catechu (*kattha*).
- **Salar/Salai — *Boswellia serrata*:** source of salai resin. **Guggal is a different plant**, *Commiphora wightii*; do not identify the resin of *Boswellia serrata* as guggal.
- **Tendu — *Diospyros melanoxylon*:** its leaves are used for bidi wrapping; local vernacular naming varies. Avoid memorizing a nationalisation date unless supported by a specific official source.
- **Teak — *Tectona grandis*:** occurs in selected wetter parts of southern Rajasthan, not uniformly across all districts of the southern region.

### C. Forests, woodland, grassland and scrub are not synonyms

ISFR's canopy classes are a mapping system; legal forest status is a different system; ecological vegetation types are a third way of describing the landscape. A naturally open desert grassland can be ecologically valuable despite not meeting the FSI forest-cover threshold. Similarly, a plantation can add tree cover without replacing the biodiversity and functions of a native forest.

## 4. State Symbols and Conservation Heritage

| Symbol / status | Name | Key significance |
|---|---|---|
| State tree | **Khejri** (*Prosopis cineraria*) | Arid-zone agroforestry, fodder, shade and *sangri*; also associated with the Bishnoi conservation tradition |
| State flower | **Rohida** (*Tecomella undulata*) | Characteristic dry-region flowering tree; popularly called *Marushobha* or “Teak of Marwar” |
| State bird | **Godawan / Great Indian Bustard** (*Ardeotis nigriceps*) | Critically Endangered; a flagship species of open arid grassland and desert habitat |
| State wild animal | **Chinkara** (*Gazella bennettii*) | Indian gazelle adapted to arid and semi-arid habitats |
| State domestic / heritage animal | **Camel** (*Camelus dromedarius*) | Important to desert livelihood, transport, pastoral culture and indigenous breeds; official wording may distinguish “heritage animal” from exam-book shorthand “domestic state animal” |

### A. Khejarli sacrifice (1730)

The Khejarli event is a major example of community-led tree protection. In 1730, **363 Bishnois in total**, including **Amrita Devi Bishnoi and her daughters**, are traditionally commemorated as having died while resisting the felling of Khejri trees near Khejarli. Count **363 as the total**, not 363 people in addition to Amrita Devi and her daughters. The story is central to the Bishnoi conservation ethos and later Indian environmental history.

The Government of India has an **Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award** for exceptional community-based wildlife protection. Avoid unverified superlatives such as “the world's only tree fair” or unsupported claims about the first recipient.

### B. Godawan / Great Indian Bustard

- Scientific name: *Ardeotis nigriceps*.
- IUCN status: **Critically Endangered**.
- Rajasthan's major habitat and conservation landscapes include the Desert National Park/Sanctuary landscape around Jaisalmer–Barmer and conservation sites such as Sorsan; site records can change with surveys and seasons.
- A major threat is collision with overhead power lines, alongside habitat disturbance, fragmentation and other pressures on an open-ground nesting bird.
- The Rajasthan Forest Department and Wildlife Institute of India have operated a conservation-breeding programme. A temporary facility was established at Sam in 2019; the long-term Ramdevra facility became functional in 2022. The first captive-bred chick hatched in 2023 at Sam according to WII's project report.

**Avoid stale population totals:** estimates of the wild population vary by survey date and method. Do not treat “under 150 globally” or “90% in Rajasthan” as a timeless fixed count unless the question supplies a specific report year.

### C. Camel and Chinkara

The camel was designated Rajasthan's state heritage animal in 2014; the Rajasthan Camel (Prohibition of Slaughter and Regulation of Temporary Migration or Export) Act, 2015 is relevant legislation. The National Research Centre on Camel is at Bikaner. Breed names and uses can vary across local and technical sources, so do not make unsupported claims that one breed is exclusively “the fastest” or “the only” breed for a specific use.

The Chinkara is the state wild animal. It is a gazelle, not a member of the cattle family; both sexes may show horns in some descriptions, but the length and prominence differ. Claims that every individual can survive indefinitely without drinking water are too categorical.

The Rajasthan Forest Department's current protected-area list available for this audit records:

- **3 National Parks**
- **26 wildlife sanctuaries**
- **37 conservation reserves**
- **5 tiger reserves**

These categories overlap spatially and must **not** be summed to calculate a unique protected-area total: tiger reserves include core and buffer areas that may overlap national parks, sanctuaries and other protected lands.

### E. Ramsar wetlands of Rajasthan: updated to five

As of 17 October 2026, five Rajasthan sites appear on the Ramsar List. The two-site count in older notes is obsolete.

| Ramsar site | Broad location | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Keoladeo National Park** | Bharatpur | Managed freshwater wetland and internationally important bird habitat; designated in 1981; UNESCO World Heritage Site; associated with the Montreux Record since 1990 because of ecological change and water-related concerns |
| **Sambhar Lake** | Inland saline wetland around the Jaipur–Ajmer–Nagaur region in legacy descriptions | India's major inland saline lake; important for flamingos and other waterbirds; designated in 1990 |

Ramsar designation is an international wetland-recognition status, not the same as national-park or wildlife-sanctuary notification. **Keoladeo is the Rajasthan site historically associated with the Montreux Record.** Its Siberian-crane association is historical; do not describe the species as a currently regular winter visitor without recent survey evidence.

### F. Protected-area categories: keep the legal distinction clear

- **National Park / Wildlife Sanctuary:** statutory protected-area categories under the wildlife law, with different legal regimes and degrees of restriction; exact permitted activities depend on the law and applicable notifications.
- **Conservation Reserve:** government-owned area declared for protecting landscape, flora, fauna or habitat, often adjacent to or linking existing protected areas; local community consultation is part of the legal framework.
- **Community Reserve:** can be declared on private or community land where owners/community agree to conserve habitat; the Rajasthan Forest Department's page consulted for this audit stated none had then been declared in the state.
- **Tiger Reserve:** a management landscape with core/critical tiger habitat and buffer; it can include more than one statutory protected area and should not be treated as a wholly separate non-overlapping land category.
- **Ramsar site:** wetland of international importance under the Ramsar Convention; it can overlap another protected-area category but does not automatically become a national park.

## 6. Twenty-Five High-Value Examination Traps

1. **Forest-cover baseline:** ISFR 2023 Rajasthan forest cover is **16,548.21 km²**, not 16,655 km² (the latter is an older report figure).
2. **Tree cover:** ISFR 2023 gives **10,841.12 km²**; it is not the same measure as forest cover.
3. **Combined green cover:** forest + tree cover is **27,389.33 km²**, about **8.00%** of the state area in ISFR 2023.
4. **Recorded/legal forest area:** Rajasthan's 2024–25 state legal-status table totals **33,020.32 km²**; this is not a direct substitute for satellite-mapped forest cover.
5. **Legal forest categories:** protected forests are the largest category in the state 2024–25 table, about 56.55%; legal categories do not automatically define actual canopy density.
6. **National Forest Policy:** the 1988 policy goal is one-third of national land under forest or tree cover; the two-thirds objective applies to hill/mountain regions, not a “60% mountain / 20% plains” rule.
7. **Rajasthan forest policy:** the 2023 policy aims for 20% vegetal cover by 2040; this is a target, not current cover.
8. **Forest-cover threshold:** FSI forest cover counts tree canopy at/above 10% on qualifying land parcels; scrub below that threshold is mapped separately.
9. **District rankings:** ISFR 2023 rankings use that report's district/calculated-area framework; don't attach these numbers to post-reorganisation districts without recalculation.
10. **Entisols vs Aridisols:** both occur in hot-arid Rajasthan; neither is a universal synonym for all desert soil.
11. **Regional soil type vs soil order:** “black soil” is not automatically equivalent to Vertisol everywhere; diagnostic profile properties matter.
12. **Alluvial fertility:** alluvial soil is variable; it is not uniformly neutral or highest-fertility statewide.
13. **Saline vs sodic soils:** saline soils require attention to salt leaching and drainage; gypsum is especially relevant to sodicity when soil testing recommends it.
14. **Soil Health Card:** launched at Suratgarh on 19 February 2015; its 12 indicators are N, P, K, S; Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B; pH, EC and OC.
15. **Salai vs guggal:** *Boswellia serrata* produces salai resin; guggal is *Commiphora wightii*.
16. **Khejarli count:** 363 Bishnois is the traditional total, including Amrita Devi and her daughters—not 363 plus them.
17. **Current national parks:** the Forest Department lists three: Ranthambhore, Keoladeo and Mukundara Hills.
18. **Desert National Park's legal status:** common name notwithstanding, the Forest Department's current table labels it a sanctuary and lists national-park notification under process.
19. **Tiger reserves:** Rajasthan has five in the current NTCA list.
20. **Tiger-reserve dates:** 1973/1978 may refer to Project Tiger / historical chronology; NTCA formal notifications for Ranthambhore and Sariska are shown as 2007.
21. **National numbering:** Ramgarh Vishdhari was India's 52nd tiger reserve; Dholpur–Karauli was India's 55th, not 54th.
22. **Sariska reintroduction:** it was an Indian tiger-reintroduction landmark after local extirpation; “world's first helicopter translocation” is not a safe fact.
23. **Godawan:** critically endangered; overhead power-line collision is a major threat; population estimates must be dated.
24. **Ramsar count:** Rajasthan has five Ramsar sites as of October 2026, including Menar, Khichan and Siliserh added in 2025.
25. **Ramsar vs protected-area status:** Ramsar designation does not itself make a wetland a national park or wildlife sanctuary.

## 7. Model Examination Answers

### Q1. Distinguish recorded forest area from forest cover. (2 marks)

**Model answer:** Recorded forest area is the land recorded/notified as forest in government records; forest cover is a satellite-mapped measure based on qualifying tree-canopy density, regardless of ownership or legal status. Their totals differ because legal classification and actual canopy are different measures.

### Q2. Name the 12 Soil Health Card parameters. (2 marks)

**Model answer:** N, P, K, S; Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B; and pH, electrical conductivity (EC), and organic carbon (OC). The scheme was introduced at Suratgarh on 19 February 2015.

### Q3. Why is gypsum not the universal treatment for all saline soils? (3 marks)

**Model answer:** Saline soils have excessive soluble salts, while sodic soils have excess exchangeable sodium that damages structure. Saline soils may need suitable-water leaching and drainage. Gypsum can supply calcium to replace exchangeable sodium in sodic or saline-sodic soil when soil analysis recommends it. Treatment must follow diagnosis.

### Q4. State the current protected-area counts of Rajasthan. (2 marks)

**Model answer:** The Rajasthan Forest Department's current list records 3 national parks, 26 wildlife sanctuaries, 37 conservation reserves and 5 tiger reserves. These categories overlap, so their areas should not be added as if they were mutually exclusive.

### Q5. Explain the ecological significance of the Great Indian Bustard and the main conservation challenge. (5 marks)

**Model answer:** The Great Indian Bustard (*Ardeotis nigriceps*), Rajasthan's state bird, is Critically Endangered and depends on open arid grasslands and desert habitats. A major threat is collision with overhead power lines, alongside habitat fragmentation and disturbance. Rajasthan and the Wildlife Institute of India have developed conservation-breeding facilities, including the long-term centre at Ramdevra. Habitat protection, safer power infrastructure, prey and nesting-ground protection, monitoring and local participation are complementary measures.

### Q6. Assess forest cover in Rajasthan and explain why the statistics need careful interpretation. (5 marks)

**Model answer:** ISFR 2023 recorded forest cover of 16,548.21 km² (4.84%) and tree cover of 10,841.12 km²; combined forest-plus-tree cover was 27,389.33 km², around 8.00%. The Rajasthan Forest Department's 2024–25 legal-status table reported 33,020.32 km² under reserved, protected and unclassed categories. These totals differ because legal forest records are not the same as remotely sensed canopy cover. Data year, mapping method and district boundaries must be stated when comparing values.

### Q7. Critically discuss biodiversity conservation in Rajasthan. (10 marks)

**Answer framework:**

1. **Landscape diversity:** arid grassland and scrub, Aravalli hills, dry deciduous forest, river ravines and managed wetlands support distinct communities.
2. **Protected-area network:** three listed national parks, 26 sanctuaries, 37 conservation reserves and five tiger reserves; five Ramsar sites add international wetland recognition.
3. **Flagship species:** tiger, Great Indian Bustard, chinkara, gharial and migratory waterbirds represent different habitat and management needs.
4. **Principal pressures:** habitat loss and fragmentation, linear infrastructure, unsustainable grazing or resource extraction, invasive species, water stress and human–wildlife conflict.
5. **Conservation responses:** protect corridors and open habitats, retrofit dangerous infrastructure, use science-based monitoring, restore degraded catchments, maintain ecological water requirements, involve communities and evaluate outcomes rather than counting plantations alone.
6. **Conclusion:** an effective conservation strategy must connect legal protection with landscape planning, ecosystem restoration and local livelihoods.
