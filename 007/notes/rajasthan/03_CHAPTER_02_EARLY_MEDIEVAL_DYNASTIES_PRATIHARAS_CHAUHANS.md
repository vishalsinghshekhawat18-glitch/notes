# CHAPTER 02: EARLY MEDIEVAL DYNASTIES OF RAJASTHAN
## Gurjara-Pratiharas and the Chahamanas (Chauhans)

> **Purpose:** A reliable, revision-oriented source for the Rajasthan-history portion of RPSC RAS Prelims and Mains.
>
> **How to read claims:**  
> **[Secure]** well supported by inscriptions, contemporary accounts, or multiple reliable sources.  
> **[Qualified]** broadly accepted but exact dates, extent, or interpretation remain uncertain.  
> **[Tradition]** appears in later literary, bardic, or local accounts and should not be repeated as uncontested fact.  
> **[Disputed]** historians do not agree or the evidence is inadequate.
>
> **Syllabus connection:** RPSC's Rajasthan-history syllabus includes major dynasties, their administrative/revenue systems, socio-cultural issues, architecture and monuments, and important works of Rajasthani literature. This chapter therefore covers political history alongside source criticism, economy, society, religion, literature and architecture—not just battles and ruler lists.

---

## 2.0 The historical frame: what changed in early-medieval Rajasthan?

Between roughly the 6th and early 14th centuries, Rajasthan's political landscape was shaped by multiple regional ruling houses rather than one continuous state. The Gurjara-Pratiharas became a major north Indian power during the 8th–10th centuries. The Chahamanas—commonly called Chauhans—developed several regional branches, including Shakambhari/Ajmer, Naddula (Nadol), Ranastambhapura (Ranthambore), and Jalore (Songara).

Four processes help connect the chapter:

1. **Regional state formation:** Local ruling lineages expanded from strongholds and regional centres into wider territories.
2. **Imperial competition:** The Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas competed for control of Kannauj, an important symbol and strategic centre of north Indian power.
3. **Temple and literary patronage:** Rulers used religious institutions, temples, inscriptions, coins and court literature to express authority and legitimacy.
4. **Changing political balance in the 12th–14th centuries:** Ghurid and Delhi Sultanate campaigns defeated several north-western Indian ruling houses, but regional resistance and political adaptation continued after individual forts fell.

**Do not treat “Rajasthan” as one unified medieval kingdom.** The modern state did not exist in this period; territories, frontiers and capitals changed across time.

## 2.1 Reading the evidence: a method for avoiding historical traps

| Evidence type | What it can tell us | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Inscriptions / prasastis** | Rulers, genealogies, dates, grants, titles, temples, victories and religious patronage | Often eulogise the patron; a claimed victory is not always independently corroborated |
| **Coins** | Titles, political authority, iconography, monetary circulation and sometimes changes in sovereignty | A coin's circulation or inscription alone may not prove the full political story attached to it |
| **Contemporary or near-contemporary Persian/Arabic narratives** | Campaigns, diplomatic encounters and how the Sultanate or Arab world described Indian rulers | Written from particular political, religious and literary perspectives; figures and speeches require caution |
| **Sanskrit court poetry** | Political ideology, genealogy, royal self-presentation and cultural patronage | Usually selective and encomiastic; omissions can be meaningful |
| **Later Jain, Rajasthani and bardic texts** | Regional memory, ethical ideals, traditions of resistance and cultural identity | Composed later than the events; may reshape history to fit patronage or literary aims |
| **Modern historical reconstruction** | Compares different sources and separates probability from legend | Should make uncertainty visible rather than replace one unverified certainty with another |

### The source hierarchy used in this chapter

- Use dated inscriptions and contemporary records first for chronology and political claims.
- Use later texts as important evidence for **how events were remembered**, while separately testing whether their event-details are corroborated.
- If an exact troop number, quotation, private motive, romantic episode or heroic death scene comes from a later narrative alone, do not label it a proven fact.
- Where an exam guide uses an established conventional answer, retain it only with a note where the underlying evidence is uncertain.

# PART I — THE GURJARA-PRATIHARAS

## 2.2 The Gurjara-Pratiharas: rise, imperial power and regional legacy

### 2.2.1 Identity and origin: what can and cannot be stated

The Gurjara-Pratiharas emerged as a major political power in western and northern India from the 8th century. Their history is reconstructed from inscriptions, coins, literary sources and accounts of foreign visitors.

- **“Pratihara” and the Lakshmana tradition:** Later dynastic accounts connect the name with Lakshmana, brother of Rama, serving as Rama's *pratihara* (doorkeeper/guardian). This is a dynastic origin tradition, not a literal historical record of the dynasty's foundation.
- **“Gurjara”:** The term appears in early-medieval sources as an ethnic, political or territorial label; its meaning and relationship to the ruling lineage have been debated. Do not reduce it to one indisputable modern ethnic or geographical definition.
- **Bhillamala/Bhinmal:** The Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang's 7th-century account describes a Gurjara kingdom and a capital commonly identified with Bhillamala (modern Bhinmal). This evidence is important for the earlier Gurjara political landscape, but it does **not by itself prove an unbroken dynastic line** from that 7th-century kingdom to every later Pratihara branch.
- **Mandore and the imperial Pratiharas:** An important Pratihara line ruled from Mandavyapura (Mandore, near Jodhpur). Another line rose to imperial power and came to dominate Kannauj. Their precise genealogical relationship should not be stated more confidently than the evidence permits.

**Exam distinction:** The Pratihara name has a legendary connection with Lakshmana; the historical dynasty is reconstructed from early-medieval records. Legend and political history are not the same type of evidence.

### 2.2.2 The two major lines: keep the distinction clear

| Line | Centre and evidence | Secure examination points |
|---|---|---|
| **Pratiharas of Mandavyapura/Mandore** | Mandore inscriptions, including Bauka's inscription (commonly dated 837 CE) and the Ghatiyala inscriptions of Kakkuka (commonly dated 861 CE) | Harishchandra appears in the line's origin tradition; Rajjila is associated with Mandore's fortification; Bauka and Kakkuka are key inscriptional rulers |
| **Imperial Gurjara-Pratiharas** | Wider inscriptions, especially the Gwalior *prasasti* of Mihira Bhoja, alongside other records and literary evidence | Nagabhata I, Vatsaraja, Nagabhata II, Mihira Bhoja, Mahendrapala I and later rulers; Kannauj became the central imperial prize |

Avoid an oversimplified route-map that asserts, without qualification, that one line moved neatly from Bhinmal to Ujjain to Kannauj. Bhinmal is important in the earlier Gurjara context, while the imperial Pratihara base and its expansion shifted over time.

### 2.2.3 The Pratiharas of Mandore

#### Harishchandra and the foundation tradition

The Mandore line's origin account presents **Harishchandra** as a learned and capable figure. The genealogical tradition assigns him a Brahmana wife and a Kshatriya wife, with descendants from the latter becoming rulers. This narrative is preserved through inscriptions and should be read as a record of how the lineage represented its origins, not as a modern census of social identity.

#### Rajjila

Rajjila is associated with the fortification of Mandore. The Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) notes that the Pratihara ruler Rajilla built the fort rampart around the 6th century; the surviving Brahmanical temple at the site has a later architectural history and restorations.

#### Bauka and the Mandore inscription

Bauka's inscription, usually dated **837 CE**, is an important source for the dynasty's genealogy and religious/cultural context. It is more valuable as an inscriptional record than as proof that every element of a dynastic origin story is literally historical.

#### Kakkuka and the Ghatiyala inscriptions

Kakkuka's inscriptions, usually dated **861 CE**, are among the most useful records for the Mandore Pratiharas. They associate Kakkuka with action against Abhira disturbances and with the development of Ghatiyala as an inhabited and commercial centre. The inscriptions also record religious patronage, including a Jain temple.

**Memory pair**
- **Bauka — Mandore — c. 837 CE — genealogy**
- **Kakkuka — Ghatiyala — c. 861 CE — Abhira disturbances, settlement/commercial life, Jain patronage**

**Correction to the original draft:** Do not state as a secure fact that Kakkuka created “free shops,” built a specific victory pillar at multiple locations, or personally authored every inscription unless the relevant inscriptional edition supports the exact wording. Retain the core points that can be tied to the record.

## 2.3 The imperial Gurjara-Pratiharas

### 2.3.1 Nagabhata I (c. 8th century)

Nagabhata I is conventionally treated as the founder of the imperial Pratihara power. He rose in western/central India during a period of competition among regional rulers and Arab forces operating from Sindh.

- The **Gwalior *prasasti*** of his descendant Mihira Bhoja states that Nagabhata defeated a powerful *mleccha* army.
- Historians commonly connect this reference with Arab military expansion, but the inscription does not give a modern military dispatch naming every commander, troop formation or exact date.
- It is therefore reasonable to remember Nagabhata I for checking an Arab advance, while avoiding unsupported details about a precise battlefield, a fully documented confederacy, or an exact victory date.
- **Capital/territorial sequence:** Nagabhata's territorial base expanded through western and central India. Avoid representing the capital sequence as an entirely settled chain without noting the uncertainty in the early geography of the dynasty.

**Prelims anchor:** Nagabhata I — early imperial rise; *mleccha* army in the Gwalior *prasasti*; commonly associated with resistance to Arab expansion.

### 2.3.2 Vatsaraja (late 8th century)

Vatsaraja expanded Pratihara power eastward and became involved in the contest for Kannauj.

- Vatsaraja's conflict with the Pala ruler Dharmapala and his defeat by the Rashtrakuta ruler Dhruva form part of the wider political competition often called the **Tripartite Struggle**.
- The contest was not a single continuous three-sided battle. It consisted of successive campaigns, changing alliances and attempts to control Kannauj.
- Some accounts describe royal umbrellas captured from the Palas; this is an example of the symbolic language of sovereignty used in royal narratives.
- The Prakrit work **Kuvalayamala**, composed by the Jain author Udyotana Suri and commonly dated to 779 CE, is associated with Jalore and provides evidence for contemporary cultural and linguistic life. Precise patronage claims should be handled cautiously.
- **Do not assign the *Harivamsa Purana* of Jinasena to Vatsaraja's patronage as a secure fact.** That attribution is not reliable enough to retain in a single-source exam text.

### 2.3.3 Nagabhata II (late 8th–early 9th century)

Nagabhata II rebuilt and enlarged Pratihara power after the setbacks suffered under Vatsaraja.

- He fought both the Palas and the Rashtrakutas and is associated with the Pratihara acquisition of Kannauj.
- Pratihara and Rashtrakuta inscriptions present competing claims to victory; a sound account should report the sequence as a struggle rather than imply that one ruler enjoyed uninterrupted supremacy.
- Nagabhata II's reign belongs to the period when Kannauj became the central strategic and symbolic centre of north Indian imperial competition.

**Remove as unverified:** the claim that Nagabhata II rebuilt Somnath after Arab sea-raider desecration, and the claim that he certainly ended his life by *jala-samadhi* in 833 CE. Such details need a directly checked source before they belong in a high-trust static source.

### 2.3.4 Mihira Bhoja (c. 836–885 CE): the high point of Pratihara power

Mihira Bhoja is widely regarded as one of the most powerful Pratihara rulers.

**Titles and evidence**
- **Adivaraha** is the best-known title associated with Bhoja, also reflected in his coinage and the Vaishnava political imagery of his reign.
- The **Gwalior *prasasti*** is a major source for Pratihara genealogy and claims of royal achievement.
- Medieval Arabic geographical writing describes the Gurjara ruler as militarily powerful, with a notably strong cavalry. The report attributed to **Sulaiman** (commonly placed in the mid-9th century) presents the Gurjara ruler as hostile to Arab power. It is safer to say that the account **reports** this evaluation than to assume Sulaiman personally visited every place described.
- Such descriptions are valuable evidence of the Pratiharas' military reputation and external relations, but should not be converted into exact force totals or a complete picture of the empire's internal administration.

**Avoid these overclaims**
- “Bhoja crushed Devapala and re-established undisputed authority over Bengal and Bihar.”
- “The Pratiharas halted every Arab/Islamic advance for three uninterrupted centuries.”
- Treating a translated Arabic passage as a verbatim modern quotation without identifying the translation and context.

These go beyond what the cited evidence can safely sustain.

### 2.3.5 Mahendrapala I (c. late 9th–early 10th century) and Rajasekhara

Mahendrapala I continued the Pratihara imperial tradition. His court is strongly associated with the Sanskrit poet and literary theorist **Rajasekhara**, who is also linked with Mahipala.

Important works attributed to Rajasekhara include:

| Work | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Karpuramanjari** | A drama written in Prakrit; useful for the history of literary language and court culture |
| **Kavyamimamsa** | A work on poetics and the role of the poet |
| **Viddhasalabhanjika** | Sanskrit drama |
| **Balaramayana** | Dramatic work based on the Rama story |
| **Balabharata / Prachandapandava** | Work associated with the Mahabharata tradition |

Rajasekhara's wife Avantisundari is associated with the literary setting of *Karpuramanjari*. It should not be asserted that every work listed in later literary catalogues was written under one ruler's patronage without separate evidence.

**Exam trap:** *Karpuramanjari* is associated with Rajasekhara and is composed in Prakrit—not classical Sanskrit.

### 2.3.6 Mahipala I and imperial decline

The Pratihara empire gradually weakened as powerful feudatories and regional houses asserted greater autonomy and rivals challenged its core territories.

- Al-Masudi's account is associated with his visit to India in the early 10th century and describes a powerful Gurjara ruler often identified with Mahipala.
- The Rashtrakuta ruler **Indra III** invaded north India and attacked Kannauj around 916 CE, damaging Pratihara prestige.
- Mahmud of Ghazni's campaign against Kannauj in 1018 CE occurred when Pratihara authority was much diminished. The ruler Rajyapala is associated with this episode in the historical tradition.
- The Pratiharas' imperial power fragmented over the 10th and early 11th centuries; local powers increasingly controlled former imperial territories.

**Causal chain for Mains**
1. Recurrent conflict with the Palas and Rashtrakutas placed pressure on the empire.
2. Maintaining a wide territory depended on subordinate rulers and regional elites.
3. As central authority weakened, regional lineages became more independent.
4. Attacks on Kannauj exposed the reduced ability of the imperial centre to protect itself.
5. Political power shifted toward regional kingdoms, including the expanding Chauhan branches.

Do not compress this complex process into a claim that a single battle instantly destroyed the dynasty.

## 2.4 Pratihara-period architecture and cultural life in Rajasthan

### 2.4.1 The term “Maha-Maru”

“Maha-Maru” is used in discussions of western Indian temple architecture, but it should not be treated as a single rigid architectural code invented by one dynasty. Temple forms evolved over time, and regional idioms interacted. Art historians distinguish Pratihara-period temples of Rajasthan from related temple groups elsewhere in north India.

### 2.4.2 Key sites

| Site | Exam-relevant association | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Mandore** (Jodhpur) | Pratihara political centre; Rajjila's fortification; Kakkuka's Jain patronage at Ghatiyala/Mandore context | The surviving structures represent more than one building phase |
| **Osian** (Jodhpur region) | Major temple cluster with Hindu and Jain traditions; important for early-medieval western Indian architecture | Do not assign every temple to a single ruler or a single year |
| **Harshat Mata Temple, Abhaneri** (Dausa) | Early temple architecture and sculpture; ASI assigns the temple to approximately the 8th century | The present shrine and local goddess identification reflect later history as well |
| **Kiradu** (Barmer region) | Temple complex that illustrates the later development of western Rajasthan temple architecture | Avoid labelling the whole complex a direct product of one Pratihara king |
| **Ajmer — Saraswati Kanthabharana institution** | Associated with Chauhan ruler Vigraharaja IV; literary/inscriptional evidence connects the site with Sanskrit learning and the *Harkeli* play | Its later conversion into a mosque took place in a different political period; exact origin of the name “Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra” is not settled by a single story |

### 2.4.3 Architecture as political and social evidence

Temples were not merely religious buildings. Depending on the case, they could:
- display royal patronage and legitimacy;
- support ritual specialists, artisans and local settlements;
- serve as focal points for donations and endowments;
- preserve inscriptions that identify rulers, officials, donors, titles and religious affiliation;
- connect regional courts to wider Sanskrit, Jain and Hindu cultural networks.

**Mains connection:** Explain a temple through its *patronage, style, inscription, religious use and regional setting*, rather than using “magnificent temple” as the only description.

# PART II — THE CHAHAMANAS (CHAUHANS)

## 2.5 Chauhan origins and branches

The Sanskrit form of the dynastic name is **Chahamana**; “Chauhan” is the familiar later form. The best-known line is the Chahamanas of Shakambhari/Ajmer, but several related branches ruled other parts of Rajasthan.

### 2.5.1 Origin traditions: separate record from legend

| Tradition or evidence | What it says | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Vatsa-gotra statement in the Bijolia inscription (1170 CE)** | Gives a genealogical account linking the Chahamana line to Vatsa-gotra | An important inscriptional claim, but it is a later record relative to the dynasty's earliest origins |
| **Solar lineage claim** | Royal literary and inscriptional traditions connect the Chahamanas with the solar dynasty | Present as a claim of royal legitimacy |
| **Agnikula/fire-pit account** | Later narratives describe warrior lineages emerging from a fire ritual at Mount Abu | Important for Rajput origin tradition and cultural memory; not a literal contemporary record of the dynasty's formation |
| **Foreign-origin theories** | Colonial-era writers sometimes proposed Central Asian origins for Rajput groups | Historiographical theories, not an established account for the Chahamanas |

The **Bijolia inscription of 1170 CE** is central to the Shakambhari Chahamana genealogy. The tradition that Vasudeva founded Shakambhari in 551 CE is known from later literature; the date should not be treated as a contemporary, independently corroborated starting date.

### 2.5.2 The principal Chauhan branches

| Branch | Main centre | Notable rulers / endpoint |
|---|---|---|
| **Shakambhari–Ajmer** | Shakambhari (Sambhar), later Ajayameru (Ajmer) | Vasudeva in tradition; Simharaja; Ajayaraja II; Arnoraja; Vigraharaja IV; Prithviraja III |
| **Naddula / Nadol** | Nadol, in present-day Pali region | Important branch from which later lineages, including the Songara rulers of Jalore, emerged |
| **Ranthambhapura / Ranthambore** | Ranthambore | Hammira/Hammiradeva; defeat and fall of Ranthambore in 1301 |
| **Jalore / Songara** | Jalore (Suvarnagiri) | Kirtipala and later Kanhadadeva; Jalore fell to Alauddin Khalji's forces in 1311 |
| **Chandravati–Abu / Deora** | Chandravati and the Abu–Sirohi region | Later Deora/Devda Chauhan lineages; distinct from the earlier Shakambhari line |

A branch relationship does not mean every branch shared the same capital, chronology or immediate political allegiance.

## 2.6 The Chahamanas of Shakambhari and Ajmer

### 2.6.1 Early rulers and the rise to independence

**Vasudeva** is traditionally named as the early founder associated with Shakambhari. The often-cited date **551 CE** derives from a later account and should be remembered as a conventional tradition, not an inscriptionally secure accession date.

Shakambhari (Sambhar) was important because of its location and salt-lake economy. The Chauhans are often described as Pratihara feudatories before their later independence. By the 10th century, **Simharaja** is associated with the assertion of sovereign status and the title *Maharajadhiraja*.

**Exam point:** The political rise of the Chahamanas is a process—from a regional centre and relationships with stronger powers toward a more independent kingdom—not a sudden foundation in a securely documented year.

### 2.6.2 Ajayaraja II and Ajayameru (Ajmer)

Ajayaraja II is associated with the development of Ajayameru, modern Ajmer, as a political centre in the early 12th century.

- Use **Ajayameru** as the historical name associated with Ajmer.
- The widely repeated foundation date **1113 CE** is a conventional chronology; do not treat every detail about the founding of Taragarh fortress as proven by the same evidence.
- Coins are important material evidence for the Chauhan kingdom; claims about the precise identity and independent issues of Queen Somaladevi should be retained only where a reliable numismatic catalogue or published study supports them.

### 2.6.3 Arnoraja (Ana Ji)

Arnoraja ruled in the 12th century and is associated with **Ana Sagar**, the major lake at Ajmer.

- The construction of Ana Sagar is connected with Arnoraja in local and historical tradition.
- The story that the lake was built to purify land stained by the blood of invading Turks is a remembered explanation, not a neutral engineering report.
- Arnoraja is also associated in the tradition with religious patronage at Pushkar.
- He was killed by his son Jagaddeva, an episode reported in dynastic history; avoid making an unsupported claim that this was categorically the “first patricide in Chauhan history.”

**Context link:** Ana Sagar illustrates how medieval rulers could combine water management, urban development, political prestige and religious memory.

### 2.6.4 Vigraharaja IV (Visaladeva/Bisaldeo), c. mid-12th century

Vigraharaja IV is one of the most important Chauhan rulers of Ajmer.

**Political history**
- He expanded Chauhan power and asserted influence in the Delhi region, where the Tomaras had been a major ruling house.
- His claims of conquest are reflected in inscriptions and literary traditions. The precise territorial extent of Chauhan control should not be inflated into permanent rule over all Punjab.

**Literary and educational patronage**
- He is associated with **Saraswati Kanthabharana**, a Sanskrit learning institution in Ajmer.
- The Sanskrit drama **Harkeli / Harakeli** is associated with him; fragments are known from the fabric of the later Ajmer mosque complex.
- **Somadeva** is associated with the court play *Lalita Vigraharaja*.
- *Kavi Bandhava* (“friend of poets”) is a title associated with Vigraharaja in the literary tradition.

**Architecture and water works**
- Bisalsar is associated with the ruler's building activity in regional accounts. Individual claims about the foundation of settlements, reservoirs or every surviving structure must be matched to specific evidence.

**Mains value:** Vigraharaja IV connects territorial expansion, Sanskrit learning, royal authorship, court literature and monumental reuse in Ajmer.

### 2.6.5 Prithviraja III (Prithviraj Chauhan), c. late 12th century

Prithviraja III was the best-known ruler of the Shakambhari–Ajmer line and fought Muhammad of Ghur in the battles of Tarain.

- His exact birth details and the story of a formal regency under his mother Karpuri Devi are not equally secure across sources. Avoid stating them with more certainty than the cited record permits.
- Conflicts involving the Chandelas of Bundelkhand are remembered in later accounts of the Mahoba campaign. The heroic narratives of Alha and Udal belong to a wider oral and literary tradition; separate the historical conflict from every detail of the epic.
- The rivalry with Jayachandra, ruler of the Gahadavalas of Kannauj, is often dramatized through the Sanyogita episode. **The famous abduction-at-swayamvara story is not securely established by contemporary evidence** and should be labelled as literary tradition.

## 2.7 The First and Second Battles of Tarain

Tarain (also rendered Taraori) lies in present-day Haryana. The two battles are central to the political history of north India.

| Dimension | First Battle | Second Battle |
|---|---|---|
| Date | **1191 CE** | **1192 CE** |
| Main belligerents | Prithviraja III and Muhammad of Ghur | Prithviraja III and Muhammad of Ghur |
| Immediate context | Ghurid capture of Tabarhind/Tarain-region frontier stronghold and Chauhan counter-campaign | Renewed Ghurid campaign to defeat Chauhan power |
| Broad outcome | Chauhan victory; Muhammad of Ghur withdrew wounded according to the historical narrative | Ghurid victory; Prithviraja's position collapsed |
| Wider significance | Did not eliminate the Ghurid threat | Opened the way for expanded Ghurid political-military control in north India |

### 2.7.1 What can be said confidently?

- The first battle took place in **1191** and ended in a Chauhan victory.
- The second battle took place in **1192** and ended in Prithviraja's defeat.
- The second defeat weakened the Shakambhari–Ajmer Chauhan kingdom and helped create the conditions for wider Ghurid control.
- The Delhi Sultanate is conventionally dated from **1206**, following the death of Muhammad of Ghur and the independent rule of Qutb-ud-din Aibak. It is inaccurate to say it was formally founded on the battlefield in 1192, though the battle was a major step in the process.

### 2.7.2 What needs qualification?

- Very exact army sizes, elaborate multi-squadron counts, the truce-and-pre-dawn attack story, and the claim that the Chauhan army was caught during morning ablutions should not be used as certain facts without a verifiable source.
- The claim that Prithviraja was blinded and killed Muhammad of Ghur with a *shabd-bhedi baan* belongs to later heroic legend; Muhammad of Ghur died in 1206, years after the battles of Tarain.
- Accounts differ on the exact fate of Prithviraja after defeat. A single-source chapter should state what the Persian and Sanskrit narratives report, not fuse them into one seamless narrative.
- The “last Hindu emperor” label is historically misleading: it projects a later communal category onto a complex political landscape and should be removed from analytical answers.

### 2.7.3 The sources for Prithviraja: a useful comparison

| Source | Time / orientation | How it should be used |
|---|---|---|
| **Prithviraja Vijaya** (Jayanaka) | Sanskrit court poem from Prithviraja's period; celebrates the ruler | Important for court ideology and some political events; still a royal eulogy |
| **Persian chronicles**, including Hasan Nizami's *Taj-ul-Ma'asir* | Written under the early Delhi Sultanate | Important for the Ghurid victory and political aftermath; read as a court narrative |
| **Prithviraj Raso** | Surviving versions developed over time; attributed traditionally to Chand Bardai | Major source of popular memory, but not a straightforward contemporary eyewitness account for every episode |
| Later regional and bardic traditions | Varied dates and local contexts | Valuable for memory and identity; event-details require corroboration |

**Revision principle:** “Prithviraja defeated Muhammad of Ghur in 1191 and was defeated in 1192” is high-confidence core knowledge. Sanyogita's swayamvara and the *shabd-bhedi baan* are literary traditions and belong in a different confidence category.

## 2.8 Chauhans of Ranthambore and Hammiradeva

### 2.8.1 Formation of the Ranthambore branch

After the defeat of the Shakambhari–Ajmer line, a Chauhan branch established itself at Ranthambore. Govindaraja is traditionally identified as an early ruler of this branch. Its political history involved relations with the Delhi Sultanate, changes in control of the fort and repeated attempts to maintain local independence.

### 2.8.2 Hammiradeva (Hammira), late 13th century–1301

Hammiradeva is remembered as the best-known ruler of the Ranthambore Chauhans.

- Later accounts associate Hammira with campaigns against neighbouring powers and with a policy of protecting those who sought refuge at his court.
- The conflict with Alauddin Khalji culminated in the fall of Ranthambore in **1301 CE**.
- One commonly given explanation for the conflict is Hammira's sheltering of Mongol rebels who had served the Delhi Sultanate. The details and motives are presented through different narrative traditions and should not be reduced to a single uncontested cause.
- **Hammira Mahakavya**, by Nayachandra Suri, is a major later literary source for Hammira's story. It was written in the 15th century, more than a century after Hammira's death; it is invaluable for historical memory, but not every speech or dramatic episode can be accepted literally.
- Amir Khusrau's account is valuable as a near-contemporary Sultanate-side narrative of Alauddin's campaigns. Read the two traditions comparatively rather than treating either as an impartial, complete transcript.

### 2.8.3 Siege of Ranthambore: secure core and later memory

**Secure core**
- Alauddin Khalji's forces captured Ranthambore in **1301 CE** after a major siege.
- Hammiradeva's defeat marks the end of this major Chauhan kingdom.
- The event was remembered in later Rajput and Jain literary traditions through themes of loyalty, resistance, sacrifice and betrayal.

**Claims that require a “later tradition” label**
- The exact roles and motives of each alleged betrayer, including Ranmal and Ratipala.
- The specific sequence of poisoning water stores with cow carcasses.
- The exact date **11 July 1301** for the jauhar.
- The names assigned to all women who participated, and the claim that the event was definitively the “first Jauhar of Rajasthan.”
- The story of **water-jauhar** in Padmala pond as a fully established factual detail.

A careful exam answer may say that later traditions associate the siege with jauhar and saka, but should not present the “first” ranking, exact date, or every named episode as uncontested contemporary fact.

**Terminology**
- **Jauhar:** a traditional term for collective self-immolation of women in the context of imminent defeat/capture.
- **Saka:** the associated final sortie or last combat by warriors in the narrative tradition.
- These terms are essential for understanding Rajput historical memory, but source date and context matter.

### 2.8.4 Hammira literature

- **Hammira Mahakavya** — Nayachandra Suri (15th century).
- Later *Hammira Raso* and other works are attributed to different authors in literary histories; dates and authorship should be checked against a critical edition before being memorised as fixed facts.

## 2.9 The Songara Chauhans of Jalore and the siege of Siwana

### 2.9.1 From Nadol to Jalore

The Songara Chahamanas of Jalore developed from the wider Chahamana political network, with **Kirtipala** commonly identified as the founder of the Jalore line in the late 12th century. The fort is associated with **Suvarnagiri** (“golden mountain”), from which the name Songara is commonly explained.

Use this safe formulation: Kirtipala, a son of Alhana of Nadol, founded the Jalore branch; the exact chronology given in regional summaries should be treated as conventional where not corroborated by a dated inscription.

### 2.9.2 Siwana (1308 CE)

Siwana was a strategically important fort west of Jalore. The campaign of **1308 CE** is associated with Alauddin Khalji's expansion into western Rajasthan.

- Sataldeva/Sataldeo is remembered as a defender of Siwana.
- The fort's fall is also linked in later traditions with jauhar, saka and betrayal.
- **Khairabad** is the name associated with Siwana after its conquest in some historical accounts.

Details about Bhayla Pawar, the contamination of the Bhandelao pond, named participants in jauhar, or exact battle dialogue should be included only as attributed regional tradition, not as fully independent fact.

### 2.9.3 Kanhadadeva and the fall of Jalore (1311 CE)

Kanhadadeva was the best-known Songara Chauhan ruler of Jalore. The fort fell to Alauddin Khalji's forces in **1311 CE**.

**Kanhadade Prabandha**
- Composed by **Padmanabha**, generally dated to about **1455 CE**.
- Written more than a century after the fall of Jalore.
- Describes Alauddin's Gujarat campaign, the conflict involving Jalore, resistance by Kanhadadeva and the memory of Somnath.
- Modern scholarship shows that the text reshapes the conquest through the concerns and political culture of its own time. It is a major work of historical memory, not a neutral contemporary campaign diary.

### 2.9.4 Legend versus evidence in the Jalore story

| Episode in regional/literary tradition | Responsible treatment |
|---|---|
| Kanhadadeva refused passage to Alauddin's forces in 1299 | Attribute details to *Kanhadade Prabandha* unless independently corroborated |
| Recovery and re-consecration of fragments associated with the Somnath linga | Important regional memory; do not present every detail as independently verified |
| Alauddin's daughter Firoza loved Kanhadadeva's son Viramadeva | Literary episode; not a secure political fact |
| Bika Dahiya betrayed Jalore by revealing a weak point in the fortification | A prominent later tradition; details and motives require corroboration |
| Hira Devi killed Bika Dahiya | Heroic regional tradition, not a verified contemporary record |
| The “mustard price overnight” proverb explained the discovery of a weak wall | Folkloric explanation; not a reliable military-engineering report |

**Prelims anchor:** Jalore — Kanhadadeva — Padmanabha's *Kanhadade Prabandha* — conquest by Alauddin Khalji in 1311 CE.

## 2.11 Politics, administration, economy and society: Mains-ready connections

### 2.11.1 Political structure

Early-medieval power was often layered. A ruler might exercise direct control around a capital while depending on subordinate chiefs, feudatories, local lineages and military allies farther away. The terminology and exact organisation varied by kingdom and period.

For the Pratiharas and Chahamanas, useful analytical points include:

- **Royal titles:** Titles such as *Maharajadhiraja* expressed claims to sovereignty; a title alone does not prove uniform control over every region.
- **Feudatory relationships:** Regional chiefs could serve stronger rulers and later assert greater autonomy when imperial power weakened.
- **Fortified centres:** Mandore, Ajmer, Ranthambore and Jalore connected military defence with control of routes and surrounding territories.
- **Genealogy and patronage:** Inscriptions and temple building helped legitimise dynastic claims and bind rulers to religious institutions.
- **Succession and conflict:** Dynastic rivalries could weaken regional coalitions, but should not be used as a single-cause explanation for every defeat.

### 2.11.2 Revenue and resource base

The chapter's original text focused on rulers and battles and gave too little attention to the syllabus reference to administrative and revenue systems. The evidence available here does not justify an overly detailed, kingdom-wide revenue manual, but the following broad points are useful:

- **Agrarian resources:** Control of cultivated land and agrarian surplus underpinned state power; local arrangements varied by region.
- **Land grants and endowments:** Inscriptions are important for understanding donations to temples, religious specialists and institutions. Such grants can illuminate rights, obligations and local authority, but each inscription must be read individually.
- **Trade and routes:** The Ghatiyala record's account of order and commercial settlement illustrates the importance attached to merchants and market activity. Forts and centres in Marwar also had strategic importance because they were located near overland routes.
- **Salt:** The Sambhar region's salt resources formed part of its economic importance; it helps explain why Shakambhari/Sambhar mattered beyond dynastic tradition.
- **Coinage:** Coins provide evidence for political titles, exchange and regional circulation; they should be interpreted with their findspot and type, not simply as proof that a ruler controlled all places where a coin is found.

**Important limitation:** Do not memorise a supposedly uniform list of Pratihara or Chauhan tax rates, offices and revenue shares unless you have a specific inscriptional or textual source for it.

### 2.11.3 Religion and cultural patronage

The Pratihara and Chauhan records reveal a varied religious landscape:
- Vaishnava imagery and temples associated with royal patronage;
- Shaiva traditions and temples;
- Jain temples and Jain literary scholarship;
- royal and courtly support for Sanskrit and Prakrit literature;
- religious institutions as important centres of landholding, learning, donation and local identity.

This does not mean that all rulers had the same religious policy or that every temple was royal in origin. Patronage differed by place and time.

### 2.11.4 Literary culture

| Work / source | Author or association | Historical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Gwalior *prasasti*** | Associated with Mihira Bhoja's reign | Genealogy, royal legitimacy and remembered military achievements |

### 2.11.5 Four useful cross-chapter connections

1. **From inscriptions to political geography:** Bauka and Kakkuka help establish the Mandore line; later Guhila/Sisodia records will be useful for tracing political change in Mewar.
2. **From forts to state formation:** Mandore, Ajmer, Ranthambore and Jalore show how control of fortified seats supported regional kingdoms.
3. **From water structures to legitimacy:** Ana Sagar and temple-linked reservoirs connect kingship with urban development and local religious memory.
4. **From court literature to historical memory:** *Prithviraja Vijaya*, *Hammira Mahakavya* and *Kanhadade Prabandha* demonstrate that later heroic memory must be distinguished from contemporary documentation.

## 2.12 Common examination traps — corrected

1. **Mandore Pratiharas are not interchangeable with the imperial Pratiharas.** Distinguish the two lines, and qualify claims about their exact relationship.
2. **Bauka and Kakkuka:** Bauka is associated with Mandore (c. 837); Kakkuka with Ghatiyala (c. 861).
3. **Nagabhata I and Nagabhata II are different rulers.** Nagabhata I is linked with the defeat of a *mleccha* army; Nagabhata II with the later expansion and Kannauj struggle.
4. **The Tripartite Struggle was a process**, involving the Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas over Kannauj—not a single battle.
5. **Mihira Bhoja's title:** Adivaraha.
6. **Sulaiman and Al-Masudi:** separate accounts and periods; do not casually say both personally toured every Pratihara territory.
7. **Rajasekhara:** *Karpuramanjari* is a Prakrit drama; *Kavyamimamsa* deals with poetics.
8. **Vasudeva's 551 CE date:** traditional and late-attested, not the same as a contemporary inscriptional date.
9. **Bijolia inscription (1170 CE):** a key Chahamana genealogical source; the claim it records is not identical to proof of the dynasty's literal origins.
10. **Agnikula:** a later origin tradition, not a contemporary record of a fire-created historical dynasty.
11. **Ajayameru:** historical name associated with Ajmer; avoid overconfident details about every structure's foundation.
12. **Ana Sagar:** associated with Arnoraja; the story of blood purification is a traditional explanation.
13. **Vigraharaja IV:** associated with Ajmer's Sanskrit learning institution and *Harkeli*; claims of permanent control over all Punjab are overstated.
14. **Tarain I (1191):** Chauhan victory.
15. **Tarain II (1192):** Ghurid victory; the Delhi Sultanate is conventionally dated from 1206, not founded instantly in 1192.
16. **Sanyogita and the swayamvara:** famous tradition, not secure contemporary history.
17. **Shabd-bhedi arrow:** legend; Muhammad of Ghur died in 1206.
18. **Hammira Mahakavya:** written by Nayachandra Suri in the 15th century, not in Hammira's own lifetime.
19. **Ranthambore (1301):** the fall is historical; “first Jauhar of Rajasthan,” exact date, water-jauhar details and every named participant require source qualification.
20. **Siwana (1308) and Jalore (1311):** distinct campaigns; do not interchange their defenders or traditions.
21. **Kanhadade Prabandha:** by Padmanabha, generally dated c. 1455; a later narrative of the conquest of Jalore.
22. **Firoza–Viramdeva episode:** literary tradition, not secure dynastic diplomacy.
23. **Bika Dahiya and the mustard-price story:** regional narrative, not a reliable technical report of how the fort fell.
24. **Chittorgarh 1303 and Padmini:** the siege belongs to the Sultanate's campaigns; the famous Padmini story is linked to Jayasi's 16th-century *Padmavat*, not a contemporary account.
25. **“Last Hindu emperor”:** avoid as an objective historical label.

## 2.13 Prelims practice: statement-based questions

### Q1. Consider the following statements about the Gurjara-Pratiharas:
1. The Gwalior *prasasti* connects Nagabhata I with the defeat of a powerful *mleccha* army.
2. The Mandore and imperial Pratihara lines must be treated as one fully documented and uninterrupted line.

Which are correct?

A. 1 only  
B. 1 and 3 only  
C. 2 and 3 only  
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: B.** Statement 2 overstates the certainty of the relationship between the Mandore line and imperial Pratiharas.

| Source / work | Association |
|---|---|
| 1. Bauka inscription | a. Mandore, c. 837 CE |
| 2. Ghatiyala inscriptions | b. Kakkuka, c. 861 CE |
| 3. *Karpuramanjari* | c. Rajasekhara |

C. 1-a, 2-c, 3-b, 4-d  
D. 1-d, 2-b, 3-a, 4-c

**Answer: A.**

### Q3. Which statement about the battles of Tarain is correct?

A. Prithviraja defeated Muhammad of Ghur in both battles.  
B. The First Battle occurred in 1191 and the Second in 1192.  
C. The Delhi Sultanate was formally founded in 1192 on the battlefield.  
D. The *Prithviraj Raso* is an unproblematic contemporary eyewitness chronicle.

**Answer: B.** The Sultanate is conventionally dated from 1206; *Raso* must be used with attention to its textual history.

### Q4. Which of the following are later literary sources rather than contemporary battle reports?
1. *Hammira Mahakavya*
2. *Kanhadade Prabandha*
3. *Prithviraja Vijaya*

A. 1 and 2 only  
B. 2 and 3 only  
C. 1 and 3 only  
D. 1, 2 and 3

**Answer: A.** *Hammira Mahakavya* and *Kanhadade Prabandha* were written well after their principal events. *Prithviraja Vijaya* is a near-contemporary court poem.

### Q5. Which pair is correctly matched?

A. Mihira Bhoja — *Kanhadade Prabandha*  
B. Vigraharaja IV — *Harkeli* association

D. Nayachandra Suri — *Prithviraja Vijaya*

**Answer: B.**

### Q6. Which claim should be treated primarily as a later heroic tradition?

A. The Second Battle of Tarain was fought in 1192.  
B. The fall of Jalore occurred in 1311.  
C. Prithviraja killed Muhammad of Ghur with a sound-aimed arrow after being blinded.  
D. The Ghatiyala inscriptions are associated with Kakkuka.

**Answer: C.**

### Q7. The Tripartite Struggle involved which three powers?

A. Chahamanas, Guhilas and Chandelas  
B. Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas  
C. Cholas, Palas and Chahamanas  
D. Tomaras, Chauhans and Gahadavalas

**Answer: B.**

### Q8. Which of the following is the best method for using the *Kanhadade Prabandha*?

A. Treat every description as a contemporary official record of 1311.  
B. Discard it because it was composed after the events.  
C. Use it as an important source for later regional memory and test event-details against independent evidence.

**Answer: C.**

## 2.14 Mains answer-building templates

### 2-marker: Explain the significance of the Ghatiyala inscriptions.

**Model answer:** The Ghatiyala inscriptions of Kakkuka (c. 861 CE) illuminate the Mandore Pratihara line, local order, settlement and Jain patronage.

### 5-marker: Explain the historical significance of Mihira Bhoja.

**Model answer framework:**
1. One of the most powerful rulers of the imperial Gurjara-Pratiharas.
2. Associated with the title *Adivaraha*.
3. The Gwalior *prasasti* is an important source for his dynasty and royal achievements.
4. Arabic geographical accounts reflect the Pratihara ruler's military reputation.
5. His reign represents a high point before later imperial fragmentation.

### 5-marker: Explain the cultural importance of Vigraharaja IV.

**Model answer framework:**
1. Expanded Chauhan influence in the Delhi region.
2. Associated with the Saraswati Kanthabharana institution at Ajmer.
3. Linked with the Sanskrit drama *Harkeli*.
4. Court literature includes Somadeva's *Lalita Vigraharaja*.
5. His reign illustrates the relationship between political power, Sanskrit learning and monumental patronage.

### 10-marker: Compare contemporary evidence and later memory in the history of Prithviraja III.

**Suggested structure:**
- **Introduction:** Prithviraja's history is reconstructed from inscriptions, Sanskrit court poetry, Persian chronicles and later bardic texts.
- **Contemporary/near-contemporary evidence:** Explain the value and limits of *Prithviraja Vijaya* and Persian narratives.
- **Later memory:** Discuss how *Prithviraj Raso* and oral traditions expanded episodes such as the Sanyogita story and the *shabd-bhedi baan*.
- **Political core:** Establish the battles of Tarain in 1191 and 1192 and the consequences of the second defeat.
- **Evaluation:** Separate verifiable chronology from later narratives while recognising the cultural importance of the latter.
- **Conclusion:** Historians must compare sources by date, purpose and corroboration rather than choosing between “legend” and “fact” wholesale.

### 10-marker: Assess the political and cultural significance of the Chauhan branches in medieval Rajasthan.

**Suggested structure:**
- Outline the Shakambhari–Ajmer, Naddula, Ranthambore and Jalore branches.
- Explain the rise from regional centres and changing relations with the Pratiharas.
- Discuss forts, military routes, water works and urban centres.
- Use Vigraharaja IV for Sanskrit learning and Hammira/Kanhadadeva for the later literary memory of resistance.
- Conclude that Chauhan history demonstrates both regional state formation and the need to separate contemporary evidence from later hero narratives.
