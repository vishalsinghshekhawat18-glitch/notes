## 7.1 The Particle Shift Engine: Why Phrasal Verbs Baffle Test-Takers

A **Phrasal Verb** consists of a core lexical verb combined with one or two relational particles (prepositions or spatial adverbs). The defining feature of a phrasal verb is **non-compositionality**: the semantic meaning of the combined unit cannot be deduced by simply adding the meaning of the verb to the meaning of the particle.

When an author writes *"The committee **called off** the strike"*, the action has nothing to do with calling with the voice or being physically located off the ground. The particle transforms the semantic identity of the verb:

```
[ CORE BASE VERB ]       +       [ RELATIONAL PARTICLE ]       ──►       [ NEW IDIOMATIC CONCEPT ]
Example: Call (speak loudly)     +       Off (departure/separation)    ──►       CANCEL / TERMINATE
Example: Look (use eyes)         +       Into (penetrate)              ──►       INVESTIGATE FORENSICALLY
Example: Put (place physically)  +       Off (away)                    ──►       POSTPONE / DELAY
```

### The Spatial Logic of Particles
While phrasal verbs appear arbitrary, particles retain systematic metaphorical trajectories:
* **UP**: Completion, intensification, total consumption (*wind up, use up, clean up, speed up*).
* **DOWN**: Suppression, reduction, recording on paper (*crack down, scale down, write down, step down*).
* **OFF**: Departure, cancellation, severance (*call off, put off, write off, ward off*).
* **OUT**: Exhaustion, disappearance, discovery, resolution (*run out, iron out, figure out, burn out*).
* **INTO**: Investigation, sudden impact, involuntary collision (*look into, run into, bump into*).
* **AWAY**: Permanent elimination, disposal, gradual fading (*do away with, pass away, throw away*).

### 2. The BRING Family

* **Bring about**: To cause something to happen (*Macroeconomic reforms brought about industrial revival*).
* **Bring out**: To publish a book/report; to highlight or make an inherent quality conspicuous (*The audit brought out serious internal control failures*).
* **Bring up**: 
  1. To rear, educate, and care for a child (*brought up in an austere environment*).
  2. To introduce a sensitive subject for parliamentary or board discussion (*The director brought up the issue of executive compensation*).
* **Bring down**: To reduce prices/inflation; to overthrow an established government (*The scandal brought down the coalition government*).
* **Bring round / around**:
  1. To revive someone from unconsciousness or fainting.
  2. To persuade someone to change their opinion and agree (*After prolonged negotiations, the Governor brought the board round to his perspective*).
* **Bring forward**: To propose a motion or schedule an event for an earlier date (*The monetary policy review was brought forward to Monday*).

---

### 3. The CALL Family

* **Call off**: To cancel an event, arrangement, or industrial strike that was already scheduled (*The labor union called off the national strike after wage guarantees*).
* **Call on / upon**:
  1. To pay a short formal or professional visit (*The ambassador called on the Prime Minister*).
  2. (*Followed by an infinitive*): To formally appeal to, summon, or demand an action (*The central bank called upon commercial lenders to bolster capital buffers*).
* **Call for**: To publicly demand or require as necessary (*The systemic banking collapse called for immediate regulatory intervention*).
* **Call in**: To summon a professional or outside expert to assist (*The hospital called in a team of specialized epidemiological consultants*).
* **Call up**: To recollect a memory; to summon someone for mandatory military or jury service.
* **Call out**: To challenge, expose, or publicly criticize someone for dishonest behavior (*The investigative journalist called out the firm's greenwashing*).

---

### 4. The COME Family

* **Come across**: To meet or discover unexpectedly by chance (*While auditing old ledgers, the clerk came across an uncredited promissory note*). (⚠️ *Never say "suddenly came across"*—a tautology!).
* **Come by**: To acquire or obtain something, especially something rare or difficult (*Integrity of that caliber is exceptionally hard to come by*).
* **Come of**: To originate from or be the inevitable natural result of (*Nothing constructive came of the bilateral discussions*).
* **Come off**:
  1. To take place successfully as planned (*The corporate acquisition came off without regulatory friction*).
  2. To become detached from something (*The seal came off the vault door*).
* **Come round / around**: To regain consciousness; to accept an opinion previously opposed.
* **Come down on**: To punish, reprimand, or discipline with severe severity (*The Securities Board came down heavily on insider trading cartels*).
* **Come into**: To inherit money, property, or title (*Upon his grandfather's demise, he came into a vast agricultural estate*).

---

### 5. The GET Family

* **Get along / on with**: To maintain a friendly, cooperative relationship with someone (*The executive director gets along well with employee unions*).
* **Get away with**: To escape penalty, blame, or detection for a crime or misdemeanor (*Rogue financial traders must not be allowed to get away with regulatory non-compliance*).
* **Get through**:
  1. To pass or succeed in a rigorous competitive examination (*He got through the Civil Services Examination on his first attempt*).
  2. To complete an arduous task or endure an ordeal (*The economy got through the liquidity crunch*).
  3. To successfully establish telephone or electronic contact (*After three attempts, the trader finally got through to the treasury desk*).
* **Get over**: To overcome or recover from an emotional shock, illness, or financial loss (*The corporation took two years to get over the reputational fallout*).
* **Get at**: To reach or access; to imply or suggest obliquely (*What fiscal point is the member getting at?*).

---

### 6. The GIVE Family

* **Give in**: To cease resistance; to surrender or yield to pressure (*The central bank refused to give in to political demands for artificial rate cuts*).
* **Give up**: To abandon hope, resign a position, or stop an ingrained habit (*The debtor gave up all claims to the collateral*).
* **Give away**:
  1. To distribute prizes, awards, or gifts (*The Governor gave away the national productivity trophies*).
  2. To unintentionally betray or reveal a secret or identity (*His nervous demeanor gave away his complicity*).
* **Give off**: To emit or discharge gas, heat, light, or an odor (*The chemical effluent gave off noxious sulfur dioxide fumes*).
* **Give out**:
  1. To distribute notices, circulars, or information to a crowd.
  2. To become exhausted or fail mechanically (*His physical stamina gave out near the mountain peak*).

---

### 7. The LOOK Family

* **Look into**: To examine, investigate, and analyze forensic evidence (*A special audit team was constituted to look into the non-performing loan diversion*).
* **Look after**: To care for, protect, and attend to the needs of someone (*The welfare department looks after distressed migrant families*).
* **Look down on / upon**: To view someone with disdain, superiority, or arrogant contempt (*Privileged elites must not look down upon informal gig workers*).
* **Look for**: To search for or seek out a lost object or opportunity.
* **Look forward to**: To anticipate a future event with eager pleasure (**MANDATORY GERUND**: *We look forward to **receiving** your inspection report*).
* **Look up to**: To respect, admire, and hold someone in high esteem as a role model (*Young civil servants look up to the upright cabinet secretary*).
* **Look out**: To be vigilant, alert, and watchful for impending danger (*Investors must look out for hidden charges in unregulated digital loan apps*).

---

### 8. The MAKE Family

* **Make out**:
  1. To discern, decipher, or distinguish through visual or auditory observation (*Through the dense fog, the maritime pilot could barely make out the lighthouse*).
  2. To understand the underlying meaning or character of something (*Economists could not make out the logic behind the sudden tariff hike*).
* **Make up**:
  1. To invent, fabricate, or concoct a false narrative (*The cashier made up an elaborate pretext for the ledger shortage*).
  2. To compensate or make amends for a deficiency, loss, or delay (*The airline increased flight frequencies to make up for lost time*).
  3. To reconcile and settle differences following an argument.
* **Make off with**: To steal something valuable and make a rapid escape (*The rogue promoters made off with hundred crores in bank borrowings*).
* **Make for**: To move in the direction of; to be a contributing factor towards (*Transparency and predictability make for robust market governance*).

---

### 9. The PUT Family

* **Put off**:
  1. To postpone or defer to a later date (*The annual general meeting was put off until statutory compliance audits were finalized*).
  2. To cause someone to feel disgust, aversion, or disinterest (*The exorbitant processing fees put off prospective mortgage borrowers*).
* **Put out**: To extinguish a fire, flame, or cigarette (*The emergency fire response crew put out the industrial blaze within two hours*).
* **Put up with**: To endure, tolerate, or bear an unpleasant situation without complaint (*The regulatory ombudsman will not put up with deceptive banking advertisements*).
* **Put down**:
  1. To suppress a rebellion, mutiny, or violent riot by police or military force (*The sovereign administration put down the border insurrection*).
  2. To write down, record in a register (*The clerk put down the account numbers*).
* **Put on**: To don clothing; to feign, simulate, or assume an insincere persona (*He put on an air of professional indifference*).
* **Put forward**: To submit or propose a theory, candidate, or policy recommendation for formal consideration (*The Ministry put forward an ambitious infrastructure bond roadmap*).

---

### 10. The RUN Family

* **Run into**:
  1. To encounter someone unexpectedly by chance (*I ran into the former chief economic advisor at the airport*).
  2. To experience or collide with difficulties, obstacles, or debt (*The infrastructure company ran into severe cash flow shortages*).
* **Run out of**: To exhaust a finite supply of something completely (*The island nation ran out of foreign exchange reserves, triggering an external debt default*).
* **Run down**:
  1. To disparage, criticize, or belittle someone unfairly (*It is unpatriotic to run down domestic scientific achievements*).
  2. To lose battery or mechanical power gradually (*The chronometer ran down*).
* **Run over**: To hit or drive over with a vehicle; to overflow the edges of a container.
* **Run through**: To rehearse briefly; to examine or read through quickly; to squander money rapidly (*He ran through his inheritance in three years*).

---

### 11. The SET Family

* **Set in**: (*Of seasons, weather, diseases, or economic cycles*): To begin and seem likely to continue for an extended period (*A severe agricultural drought set in across the plateau* / *Deflationary expectations set in*).
* **Set off**:
  1. To initiate a long journey or expedition (*The survey team set off at dawn*).
  2. To trigger an explosive reaction, chain of events, or panic (*The bank failure set off a nationwide run on deposits*).
* **Set up**: To establish an institution, business, committee, or physical apparatus (*The central bank set up an expert panel on climate finance*).
* **Set aside**:
  1. To annul, quash, or overturn a judicial ruling or order (*The Supreme Court set aside the High Court's injunction*).
  2. To allocate or reserve money or resources for a specific statutory purpose (*Ten percent of revenue was set aside for debt redemption*).
* **Set forth**: To state, explain, or outline principles or terms in a clear, systematic manner (*The white paper set forth the structural rationale for privatization*).

---

### 12. The STAND, TAKE & TURN Families

* **Stand by**:
  1. To remain loyal to and support someone during adversity (*The central bank stood by the beleaguered regional bank*).
  2. To remain on alert, ready for immediate operational deployment (*Emergency liquidity facilities are standing by*).
* **Stand for**:
  1. To represent or symbolize a principle or abbreviation (*The acronym FRBM stands for Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management*).
  2. To tolerate or endure (*The commission will not stand for procedural delays*).
* **Take after**: To resemble an older parent or relative in physical appearance, disposition, or character (*The young diplomat takes after his grandfather in eloquence*).
* **Take off**:
  1. To depart from the ground into flight (*The aircraft took off smoothly*).
  2. To become abruptly successful, popular, or commercially viable (*Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions took off exponentially*).
* **Take in**:
  1. To fully absorb, comprehend, and assimilate complex information (*It took months for market analysts to take in the implications of the landmark ruling*).
  2. To be deceived, swindled, or duped (*Vulnerable senior citizens were taken in by fraudulent high-return investment schemes*).
* **Turn down**:
  1. To reject, decline, or refuse a proposal, request, or applicant (*The credit committee turned down the high-risk unsecured loan proposal*).
  2. To reduce the volume, heat, or flow of a device.
* **Turn out**:
  1. To prove to be the case in the end; result (*The macroeconomic forecasting models turned out to be remarkably accurate*).
  2. To produce or manufacture goods (*The manufacturing plant turns out ten thousand electric vehicles annually*).
* **Turn up**:
  1. To arrive or appear unexpectedly, especially after being lost or delayed (*The missing audit files finally turned up in the basement archive*).
  2. To increase the volume, temperature, or speed.

## 7.3 The 10 Deadliest Phrasal Verb Traps

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 1: Call Off vs. Put Off**
> *Exam Trap*: *"Due to torrential rain, the match was called off until next Friday."*
> *Correction*: FALSE. **Call off** means **CANCEL permanently**. **Put off** means **POSTPONE to a future date**. Since a future date (*next Friday*) is specified, the event was **put off**, not called off!

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 2: The "Suddenly Came Across" Tautology**
> *Exam Trap*: *"Walking through the forest, I suddenly came across a rare medicinal orchid."*
> *Correction*: **Come across** already embeds the concept of meeting or finding suddenly and unexpectedly. Pairing it with *suddenly* is an illiterate redundancy: *"I **came across** a rare medicinal orchid."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 3: Give Away vs. Give In**
> *Exam Trap*: *"Faced with aggressive international sanctions, the rogue regime finally gave away."*
> *Correction*: FALSE. To surrender or yield to coercive pressure is **Give IN**. **Give away** means to distribute gifts or betray a secret. Correct: *"...finally **gave in**."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 4: Look Into vs. Look For**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The Central Bureau of Investigation was tasked with looking for the financial irregularities."*
> *Correction*: To conduct a formal judicial or forensic investigation into a complex matter is **Look INTO**, not look for. Correct: *"looking **into** the financial irregularities."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 5: Break Out vs. Break In**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The fire broke in the garment factory."*
> *Correction*: Fires, wars, epidemics, and civil riots **Break OUT** (erupt violently). *Break in* means to force entry into a building or domesticate a wild animal.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 6: Look Forward To + V1**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The Governor looks forward to meet the bilateral delegation."*
> *Correction*: In the phrasal construction *look forward to*, the particle *to* is a true preposition and commands a **GERUND (-ing)**: *"looks forward to **meeting** the bilateral delegation."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 7: Take After vs. Look After**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The daughter takes after her ailing grandmother in the hospital."*
> *Correction*: FALSE. To care for an ill person is **Look AFTER**. **Take after** means to resemble an ancestor in traits. Correct: *"The daughter **looks after** her ailing grandmother..."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 8: Set Aside vs. Set Off**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The High Court set off the illegal administrative transfer orders."*
> *Correction*: To annul, quash, or overturn a legal decision is **Set ASIDE**. **Set off** means to trigger an explosion or embark on a journey.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 9: Put Up With vs. Put Down**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The sovereign state will not put down with cross-border terrorism."*
> *Correction*: To tolerate or endure is **Put UP WITH**. Correct: *"...will not **put up with** cross-border terrorism."*

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 10: Make Out vs. Make Up**
> *Exam Trap*: *"The handwriting was so illegible that the clerk could not make it up."*
> *Correction*: To visually decipher or understand something difficult to see/hear is **Make OUT**, not make up. Correct: *"...could not **make it out**."*

## 7.4 The 60-Second Memory Skeleton (Rapid Recall)

* **Call off** = Cancel; **Put off** = Postpone (Never confuse them!).
* **Bring about** = Cause to occur; **Bring up** = Rear child / introduce topic; **Bring out** = Publish/highlight.
* **Come across** = Meet/find by chance (Never say *"suddenly came across"*).
* **Come by** = Acquire with difficulty; **Come of** = Result from.
* **Get along with** = Friendly relations; **Get away with** = Escape punishment; **Get over** = Recover.
* **Give in** = Surrender; **Give up** = Abandon habit; **Give away** = Distribute / reveal secret; **Give off** = Emit fumes.
* **Look into** = Investigate forensic matters; **Look after** = Care for; **Look down on** = Despise.
* **Look forward to** ──► Always takes **GERUND (-ing)**.
* **Make out** = Decipher/understand; **Make up** = Fabricate story / compensate loss / reconcile.
* **Put out** = Extinguish fire; **Put up with** = Tolerate; **Put down** = Suppress rebellion / record in writing.
* **Run into** = Collide / incur debt / meet; **Run out of** = Exhaust supply.
* **Set in** = Seasons/recession begin; **Set aside** = Quash judicial order / reserve funds; **Set off** = Trigger.
* **Take after** = Resemble parents; **Take in** = Understand / be swindled; **Take off** = Fly / rise rapidly.
* **Turn down** = Reject proposal; **Turn out** = Result in the end; **Turn up** = Arrive unexpectedly.

## 7.5 Active Recall Diagnostic Cards

#### Card 1 (Call Off vs. Put Off Temporal Nuance)
**Scenario**: In a corporate notice circulated to all commercial shareholders, the secretary writes:  
*"Notice is hereby given that the extraordinary general meeting scheduled for October 10 has been called off until November 15 due to pending regulatory audits."*  
Identify the critical semantic contradiction in this official corporate notice and explain the governing linguistic rule.
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
<strong>The Contradiction</strong>: The notice uses <strong>called off</strong>, which means the event has been <strong>permanently cancelled, abandoned, and liquidated</strong> from the calendar. However, the subsequent prepositional phrase explicitly provides a rescheduled future date (<em>"until November 15"</em>). You cannot cancel an event permanently while simultaneously rescheduling it to next month.<br/>
<strong>The Governing Rule</strong>: When an event is deferred, delayed, or rescheduled to a future date, the mandatory phrasal verb is <strong>Put off</strong> (or <em>deferred / postponed</em>). <em>Call off</em> is reserved exclusively for absolute cancellation without a replacement date.<br/>
<strong>Corrected Notice</strong>: <em>"Notice is hereby given that the extraordinary general meeting scheduled for October 10 has been <strong>put off until November 15</strong> due to pending regulatory audits."</em>
</details>

#### Card 2 (Particle Shift Analysis: Look Into vs. Look For vs. Look After)
**Scenario**: Explain how the changing particles in the following three sentences shift the entire semantic, jurisdictional, and procedural nature of the action:  
1. *"The Reserve Bank appointed an executive to **look into** the commercial lender."*  
2. *"The Reserve Bank appointed an executive to **look after** the commercial lender."*  
3. *"The Reserve Bank appointed an executive to **look for** the commercial lender."*
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
1. <strong>Look into</strong>: <em>Into</em> denotes deep penetration and forensic examination. The executive is appointed as an <strong>investigator or inspector</strong> tasked with scrutinizing financial ledgers, auditing books, and unearthing regulatory violations or fraud.<br/>
2. <strong>Look after</strong>: <em>After</em> denotes caretaking, protection, and supervisory stewardship. The executive is appointed as an <strong>administrator or custodian</strong> to manage operations, safeguard depositor funds, and steer a distressed lender through financial restructuring.<br/>
3. <strong>Look for</strong>: <em>For</em> denotes seeking out a missing physical entity. This sentence implies the commercial lender's branch or office has physically vanished and someone is physically searching the streets to find where it is!
</details>

#### Card 3 (The "Suddenly Came Across" Tautology Audit)
**Scenario**: Why will an evaluator in an RBI Grade B or UPSC essay examination penalize a candidate for writing: *"While analyzing macroeconomic survey datasets, the researcher suddenly came across an alarming structural anomaly"*?
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
<strong>The Pathology</strong>: The phrase contains a classic grammatical <strong>tautology (pleonasm)</strong>. By standard definition, the phrasal verb <strong>Come across</strong> means to encounter, discover, or meet something or someone by chance, without prior expectation or planning. The element of unexpected suddenness is already fully embedded within the lexical identity of the phrasal verb. Adding the adverb <em>suddenly</em> produces an illiterate redundancy equivalent to saying "completely surrounded on all four sides".<br/>
<strong>Corrected Sentence</strong>: <em>"While analyzing macroeconomic survey datasets, the researcher <strong>came across</strong> an alarming structural anomaly."</em>
</details>
