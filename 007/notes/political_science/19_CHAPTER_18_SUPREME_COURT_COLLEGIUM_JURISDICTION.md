## 1. Constitutional Epistemic Anchor: The Single Integrated Judiciary

Unlike the American federal system which operates a **dual court system** (Federal courts enforcing federal laws, and State courts enforcing state laws), the Indian Constitution established a **single integrated judicial system** inherited from the Government of India Act, 1935.
- The **Supreme Court of India** stands at the apex of the judicial hierarchy, exercising supervisory authority over High Courts and Subordinate Courts.
- It enforces **both Central laws and State laws** uniformly across the territory of India.
- **Inauguration**: The Supreme Court was inaugurated on **January 28, 1950**, succeeding the *Federal Court of India* (established under the 1935 Act) and replacing the British *Privy Council* as the highest appellate tribunal for India.

## 2. Composition, Strength & Qualifications

### A. Number of Judges & Legislative Evolution (Article 124(1))
- **Original Constitution (1950)**: Fixed the strength at **One Chief Justice and not more than seven other judges (1 + 7 = 8)**.
- **Parliamentary Power**: Under Article 124(1), Parliament is empowered to prescribe and increase the number of judges **by ordinary law** (*The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956*).
- **Strength Expansion Vector**:
  $$\text{1950: 8} \longrightarrow \text{1956: 11} \longrightarrow \text{1960: 14} \longrightarrow \text{1977: 18} \longrightarrow \text{1986: 26} \longrightarrow \text{2008: 31} \longrightarrow \mathbf{2019: 34 \text{ Judges}}$$
  *(Current sanctioned strength under the 2019 Amendment: **1 Chief Justice of India + 33 Puisne Judges = 34 Judges**).*

---

### B. Qualifications for Appointment as a Supreme Court Judge (Article 124(3))
A person must be a **Citizen of India**, and must satisfy **ANY ONE** of the following three alternative qualifications:
1. Has been for at least **five years a Judge of a High Court** (or of two or more such courts in succession); **OR**
2. Has been for at least **ten years an Advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more such courts in succession); **OR**
3. Is, in the opinion of the President, a **distinguished jurist**.

> [!WARNING]
> **EXAM TRAP: The "Distinguished Jurist" Category**
> A distinguished jurist can be appointed directly as a Judge of the **Supreme Court** under Article 124(3)(c). However, the Constitution does **NOT** provide for the appointment of a distinguished jurist as a Judge of a **High Court**!

## 3. The Collegium System: The Four Judges Cases & NJAC

The appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges has witnessed the most contentious separation-of-powers battle in Indian constitutional history.

```
                    CHRONOLOGICAL VECTOR OF THE COLLEGIUM DOCTRINE
                    
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1981: FIRST JUDGES CASE (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India)                  │
   │   - 7-Judge Bench held: "Consultation" under Art 124 DOES NOT MEAN      │
   │     "Concurrence". The President (Executive) has primacy.              │
   └────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                        │
                                        ▼
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1993: SECOND JUDGES CASE (SCAORA v. Union of India)                     │
   │   - 9-Judge Bench overruled S.P. Gupta.                                 │
   │   - "Consultation" MEANS "CONCURRENCE".                                 │
   │   - Primacy shifted to the Judiciary. Birth of the COLLEGIUM SYSTEM:    │
   │     CJI + 2 Senior-most SC Judges. CJI appointment strictly by Seniority│
   └────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                        │
                                        ▼
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1998: THIRD JUDGES CASE (Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 under Art 143) │
   │   - 9-Judge Bench expanded the Collegium:                               │
   │     • SC Appointments: CJI + 4 SENIOR-MOST PUISNE JUDGES.               │
   │     • HC Appointments / Transfers: CJI + 2 SENIOR-MOST JUDGES.          │
   │   - CJI's sole personal opinion without collegium consensus is void.    │
   └────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                        │
                                        ▼
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 2014: 99TH CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT ACT & NJAC ACT, 2014                │
   │   - Replaced Collegium with 6-Member National Judicial Appointments     │
   │     Commission (CJI + 2 Senior Judges + Law Minister + 2 Eminent Persons│
   └────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                        │
                                        ▼
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 2015: FOURTH JUDGES CASE (SCAORA v. Union of India)                     │
   │   - 5-Judge Constitution Bench struck down 99th Amendment & NJAC (4:1)  │
   │     as UNCONSTITUTIONAL & VOID.                                         │
   │   - RATIO: Judicial primacy in appointments is an inviolable facet of   │
   │     the INDEPENDENCE OF THE JUDICIARY, which is part of BASIC STRUCTURE.│
   │   - Collegium System was fully restored.                                │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 4. Tenure, Removal & Post-Retirement Restrictions

### A. Tenure of Supreme Court Judges (Article 124(2))
- Holds office until attaining the age of **65 years**.
- Any dispute regarding the age of a Supreme Court judge is determined by such authority and in such manner as **Parliament by law provides** (**Article 124(2A)**).
- May resign by writing under his hand addressed to the **President of India**.

---

### B. Removal Procedure: Article 124(4) & The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968
A Supreme Court Judge can be removed from office **only by an order of the President** after an address by Parliament.

### C. Post-Retirement Bar on Practice (Article 124(7))
- **Absolute Prohibition**: No person who has held office as a Judge of the Supreme Court **shall plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India**.
- *(Contrast with High Court Judges under Article 220, who can practice in the Supreme Court and other High Courts where they were not permanent judges)*.

## 6. Landmark Jurisprudential Mechanisms

### A. Special Leave Petition (SLP - Article 136)
- Article 136 is an **extraordinary, residuary, and discretionary power**.
- The Supreme Court may grant special leave to appeal from **any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India**.
- **The Sole Exception (Article 136(2))**: Does not apply to any judgment, determination, sentence, or order passed by any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the **Armed Forces (Courts Martial)**.

---

### B. The Plenary Power of "Complete Justice" (Article 142)
- Under **Article 142(1)**, the Supreme Court may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing **complete justice** in any cause or matter pending before it.
- Such decree or order is **enforceable throughout the territory of India**.
- *Historic Invocations*:
  1. *Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India (1989)*: Settlement in the Bhopal Gas Disaster.
  2. *Ayodhya Land Dispute (M. Siddiq, 2019)*: Directing allotment of 5 acres of alternative land to the Sunni Central Waqf Board.
  3. *Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023)*: Dissolution of marriage on the ground of *irretrievable breakdown* without waiting for the statutory 6-month cooling-off period under the Hindu Marriage Act.

---

### C. Curative Petition (*Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra, 2002*)
- Evolution: Review Petitions under Article 137 are heard in chambers. If a Review Petition is dismissed, can a party seek further relief?
- In *Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002)*, a 5-judge bench evolved the concept of the **Curative Petition** to prevent abuse of its process and cure gross miscarriages of justice.
- **Strict Preconditions**:
  1. Violation of principles of natural justice (party not heard), or judicial bias.
  2. Must be certified by a **Senior Advocate**.
  3. Examined by a bench of the **three senior-most judges** and the judges who passed the original judgment (if available).

---

## 7. The Power of Judicial Review (Article 13 & The Basic Structure)

Judicial review is the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of both the Central and State governments.
- **Constitutional Basis**: Articles 13, 32, 131–136, 142, 226, 245, 246, 372.
- **Status in Jurisprudence**: In *Minerva Mills (1980)* and *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)*, the Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review over legislative actions vested in the High Courts (Art 226) and Supreme Court (Art 32) is an **integral and untruncatable part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution**.

> ### 🔬 [SCHOLARLY DISCOURSE & EMPIRICAL METRICS: OXFORD HANDBOOK (2024)]
> *(Optional Deep Dive: Read for Mains Value-Addition & Academic Citations; Skip on Rapid First Read)*
>
> * **The "Polyvocal" Court and Structural Fragmentation (Dr. Ronojoy Sen, *Oxford Handbook of Indian Politics*)**:
>   - Sen contrasts the Indian Supreme Court with the US Supreme Court. The US apex court sits *en banc* (all 9 judges sit together on every heard case, hearing fewer than 80 cases a year).
>   - In contrast, the Indian Supreme Court operates as a **"Polyvocal Court"** fragmented into 15 to 17 two-judge and three-judge division benches. Because benches sit separately, different judges frequently deliver conflicting interpretations on the same legal principles (especially regarding bail standards under PMLA/UAPA, death penalty sentencing, and arbitration).
> * **The Master of the Roster Debate**:
>   - In *Asok Pande (2018)* and *CJAR (2017)*, the Supreme Court ruled that the Chief Justice of India (CJI) is the sole "Master of the Roster" with exclusive administrative discretion to constitute benches and assign cases.
>   - Sen highlights the academic critique: In high-stakes constitutional litigation (Electoral Bonds, Demonetization, Article 370), the CJI's bench-allocation power effectively determines judicial outcomes, generating urgent calls for an automated, rule-based case allocation mechanism.
> * **The SLP Docket Crisis**:
>   - Article 136 (Special Leave Petition) was intended as an extraordinary residual safety valve for gross injustice. Instead, SLPs now consume **over 80% of the Supreme Court's judicial time**. By transforming itself into a court of regular appellate correction for land and commercial disputes, constitutional bench hearings have been severely delayed, driving apex court pendency past **82,000 cases**.

## 8. Active Recall Diagnostic Vault

```
Q1. What is the current sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court of India, and under which statutory 
    enactment was it fixed?
A1. Exactly 34 Judges (1 Chief Justice of India + 33 Puisne Judges), fixed under the Supreme Court 
    (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019.

Q2. What are the three alternative qualifications required to become a Judge of the Supreme Court 
    under Article 124(3)?
A2. Citizen of India AND: (1) At least 5 years as a High Court Judge; OR (2) At least 10 years as an 
    Advocate of a High Court; OR (3) In the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.

Q3. What was the core ratio of the 5-judge bench in the Fourth Judges Case (2015) regarding the NJAC?
A3. The Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act and the NJAC Act as unconstitutional, 
    holding that judicial primacy in judicial appointments is an essential component of the 
    Independence of the Judiciary, which is part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution.

Q4. Does the Supreme Court have jurisdiction under Article 136 (Special Leave Petition) against orders 
    passed by a Court Martial?
A4. No. Article 136(2) explicitly excludes any judgment, determination, sentence, or order passed by 
    any court or tribunal constituted under any law relating to the Armed Forces (Courts Martial).

Q5. What is the fundamental difference between Clause (1) and Clause (2) of Article 143 (Advisory 
    Jurisdiction)?
A5. Under Article 143(1) (questions of law or fact of public importance), the Supreme Court MAY or 
    MAY NOT tender its advice (opinion is discretionary). Under Article 143(2) (pre-constitution treaties 
    and covenants), the Supreme Court MUST tender its advice (opinion is mandatory). In both cases, 
    the advice is purely advisory and non-binding on the President.
```
