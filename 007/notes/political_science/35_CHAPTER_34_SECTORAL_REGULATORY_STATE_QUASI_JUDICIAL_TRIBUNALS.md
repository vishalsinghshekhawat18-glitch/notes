## 34.1 The Genesis of the Modern Regulatory State in India

Prior to 1991, India operated as an interventionist, bureaucratic command economy (*License-Permit-Quota Raj*). The State performed a triple identity: **Policy Maker, Market Regulator, and Monopoly Producer**.

```
                        PARADIGM SHIFT IN STATECRAFT (1991)
                                         │
        ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                                 ▼
PRE-1991 COMMAND STATE                                          POST-1991 REGULATORY STATE
* State owns airlines, telecom, power, banks                    * State disinvests and unbundles monopolies
* Bureaucrats set tariffs and prices                            * Private capital enters critical infrastructure
* Severe conflict of interest: State regulates its own firms    * Independent regulators created to ensure a
* Bureaucratic discretion and market rationing                    level playing field and consumer protection
```

### 1. Why Were Independent Regulators Needed?
1. **Separation of Policy, Operation, and Regulation**: When the government owned BSNL or State Electricity Boards, it could not impartially arbitrate disputes between private competitors and state-owned entities. Independent regulators eliminate this structural conflict of interest.
2. **Technological & Financial Complexity**: Modern capital markets, algorithm trading, spectrum auctions, and renewable grid parity require continuous domain expertise that rotating generalist bureaucrats cannot provide.
3. **Depoliticization of Tariffs**: Setting electricity tariffs, highway tolls, and telephone charges involves long-term capital recovery. Left to elected politicians, tariffs are routinely suppressed for electoral popularity, bankrupting utility infrastructure.
4. **Investor Confidence**: Private and foreign capital requires predictability, fair competition, non-discrimination, and protection against regulatory expropriation.

### 2. The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC)
* **Genesis**: Established in **December 2010** following the recommendations of the **Raghuram Rajan Committee (2008)** on Financial Sector Reforms.
* **Institutional Nature**: An apex **non-statutory executive body** created to institutionalize inter-regulatory coordination across fragmented financial domains.
* **Composition**:
  * **Chairman**: Union Finance Minister.
  * **Members**: Governor of RBI, Finance Secretary / Secretary (Economic Affairs), Secretary (Financial Services), Chief Economic Adviser (CEA), Chairman SEBI, Chairman IRDAI, Chairman PFRDA, Chairman IBBI, Secretary (Ministry of Corporate Affairs), and Secretary (MeitY).
* **FSDC Sub-Committee**: Chaired by the **Governor of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)**, meeting frequently to address macro-prudential surveillance, systemic liquidity risks, and regulatory perimeter arbitrage.

## 34.3 Quasi-Judicial Tribunals: The Constitutional Scheme (Part XIV-A)

Part XIV-A was inserted into the Constitution by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, introducing two specialized provisions:

```
                          CONSTITUTIONAL TRIBUNAL DUALITY
                                         │
        ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                                 ▼
   ARTICLE 323-A                                                     ARTICLE 323-B
(Administrative Tribunals)                                    (Tribunals for Other Matters)
* Exclusive to Public Service disputes                         * Broad spectrum of socio-economic subjects:
* Established ONLY by Parliament                                 Taxation, Foreign Exchange, Land Reforms,
* Single Tribunal for Union (CAT) &                              Rent/Tenancy, Elections (except Parl/Assembly),
  individual or Joint Tribunals for States (SAT)                 Foodstuffs, Industry, Labor
* Hierarchy: Tribunal only (no sub-tribunals)                  * Established by BOTH Parliament & State Legislatures
                                                               * Hierarchical ladder of tribunals permitted
```

### 1. Distinction: Article 323-A vs. Article 323-B

| Dimension | Article 323-A (Administrative Tribunals) | Article 323-B (Other Sectoral Tribunals) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Subject Matter** | Recruitment and conditions of service of public servants (Union, States, Local bodies, CPSEs). | Taxation, Foreign exchange, Import-export, Industrial & labor disputes, Land reforms, Urban ceiling, Foodstuffs, Elections to local bodies, Rent. |
| **Legislative Competence** | **Parliament ALONE** has the constitutional power to enact laws creating 323-A tribunals. | **Both Parliament and State Legislatures** can create tribunals within their respective legislative competencies (Lists I, II, III). |
| **Hierarchy of Tiers** | Only one level of tribunal permitted: Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) or State Administrative Tribunal (SAT). | A **hierarchy of tribunals** (original, appellate, revision) can be statutorily constituted. |

### 2. The National Green Tribunal (NGT): Environmental Rule of Law
* **Statutory Genesis**: Established on **18 October 2010** under the *National Green Tribunal Act, 2010*. India became the 3rd country in the world (after Australia and New Zealand) to establish a dedicated environmental tribunal.
* **Constitutional Foundation**: Gives effect to the citizen's **Fundamental Right to a Healthy Environment under Article 21**.
* **Institutional Composition**:
  * Full-time Chairperson (retired Supreme Court Judge or Chief Justice of a High Court).
  * 10 to 20 Judicial Members + 10 to 20 Expert Members (domain scientists with PhD/master’s degree in environmental science and administrative experience).
  * Benches sit as balanced **Two-Member Benches**: 1 Judicial Member + 1 Expert Member.
* **Jurisdictional Reach**:
  * Enforces civil claims arising out of **7 Environmental Acts**: (1) Water Act 1974, (2) Water Cess Act 1977, (3) Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, (4) Air Act 1981, (5) Environment (Protection) Act 1986, (6) Public Liability Insurance Act 1991, (7) Biological Diversity Act 2002.
  * *Explicit Exclusions*: The NGT **does NOT have jurisdiction over the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972** and the **Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006**.
* **Guiding Principles**:
  * Not bound by the technical procedure of the *Code of Civil Procedure, 1908*; guided strictly by **Principles of Natural Justice**.
  * Statutorily bound to apply: (1) **Sustainable Development**, (2) **The Precautionary Principle**, and (3) **The Polluter Pays Principle**.
  * Mandated to endeavor for final disposal of applications within **6 months** of filing.
  * Direct appeal against an NGT order lies directly to the **Supreme Court of India (within 90 days)**.

## 34.4 The Landmark Constitutional Jurisprudence of Tribunalisation

The shift of adjudication from ordinary courts to specialized tribunals triggered intense constitutional friction regarding the separation of powers and judicial independence:

```
                     CONSTITUTIONAL JURISPRUDENCE EVOLUTION
                                        │
     ┌──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                     ▼
S.P. SAMPATH KUMAR (1987)                                             L. CHANDRA KUMAR (1997)
* Upheld exclusion of High Court                                      * OVERRULED Sampath Kumar
* Held CAT is an effective substitute                                 * Articles 226/227 Judicial Review is
* Subject only to Supreme Court (Art 136)                               part of the BASIC STRUCTURE
                                                                      * Exclusion of High Courts is UNCONSTITUTIONAL
                                                                      * All Tribunals subject to Division Bench of HC
                                        │
     ┌──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                     ▼
R. GANDHI / NCLT CASE (2010)                                          MADRAS BAR ASSOCIATION (2014–2021)
* Technical members must possess judicial expertise                   * Struck down executive dominance in selection
* Executive cannot control search-cum-selection                       * Mandated minimum 5-year tenure for members
* Tribunals cannot become bureaucratic post-retirement havens          * Struck down age limits crippling recruitment
```

### 1. *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)* (7-Judge Constitutional Bench)
1. **Basic Structure Doctrine Invocation**: The power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under **Article 226/227** and the Supreme Court under **Article 32** is an integral, indissoluble part of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**.
2. **Unconstitutionality of Exclusion Clauses**: Clause 2(d) of Article 323-A and Clause 3(d) of Article 323-B, to the extent that they excluded the jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227, were held **unconstitutional and void**.
3. **Tribunals as Courts of First Instance**: Tribunals continue to act as courts of first instance in their designated fields. Aggrieved litigants **cannot bypass the tribunal** and approach the High Court directly.
4. **Mandatory High Court Appellate Filter**: Decisions of all tribunals (CAT, SAT, NGT, CESTAT, etc.) are **subject to scrutiny before a Division Bench of the respective High Court** under Article 226/227 before approaching the Supreme Court under Article 136.

### 2. The *Madras Bar Association (MBA)* Trilogy (2014, 2020, 2021)
1. **Search-cum-Selection Committee Parity**: The Supreme Court struck down rules giving the executive a majority on selection panels. Committees must be chaired by the **Chief Justice of India or a nominee Supreme Court Judge**, carrying a casting vote.
2. **Tenure and Independence**: Struck down short 3-year tenures as crippling judicial independence; mandated minimum **5-year tenure** (or 4 years with re-eligibility) to insulate members from executive pressure.
3. **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**: Repeatedly directed the Union Government to establish an independent statutory NTC to supervise appointments, infrastructure, administrative budgets, and disciplinary oversight across all tribunals under a single non-executive umbrella.

## 34.6 Examiner Traps & Warning Vault

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 1: Believing the NGT Has Jurisdiction Over the Wildlife Protection Act and Forest Rights Act**
> *Exam Trap*: "An indigenous community filing a claim under the Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006 or an NGO challenging tiger habitat destruction under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 must file before the National Green Tribunal."
> *Correction*: FALSE. The NGT Act (Schedule I) lists exactly **7 statutes**, but **deliberately excludes the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and the Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006**. Claims under these statutes must be brought before ordinary civil/criminal courts or High Courts under Article 226.

> [!WARNING]
> **TRAP 2: Believing State Legislatures Can Create Administrative Tribunals under Article 323-A**
> *Exam Trap*: "The Rajasthan State Legislative Assembly has the constitutional power under Article 323-A to enact a law establishing the Rajasthan State Administrative Tribunal."
> *Correction*: FALSE. Under Article 323-A, **Parliament ALONE has the exclusive constitutional competence** to establish Administrative Tribunals for both the Union and the States. A State Government must request the Central Government, which then introduces an amendment or notification under the central *Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985* to establish a State Administrative Tribunal.

## 34.7 60-Second Memory Skeleton (Rapid Recall)

* **Post-1991 Regulatory Shift**: State moves from Monopoly Owner $\rightarrow$ Independent Market Regulator.
* **SEBI (1992)**: Regulates capital markets, insider trading, takeovers; appeals to SAT.
* **IRDAI (1999)**: Malhotra Committee; solvency margins, consumer insurance protection; appeals to SAT.
* **TRAI (1997/2000)**: Recommends spectrum policy, net neutrality; adjudication separated to **TDSAT**.
* **CCI (2002)**: Raghavan Committee; MRTP Act repeal; curbs cartels & dominant abuse; appeals to **NCLAT**.
* **FSDC (2010)**: Non-statutory apex coordination; Chaired by Finance Minister; Sub-committee chaired by RBI Governor.
* **Article 323-A vs 323-B**: 323-A = Public service only, Parliament alone, single tier; 323-B = Other matters, Parliament + States, hierarchical tiers.
* **NGT (2010)**: Article 21 foundation; 1 Judicial + 1 Expert Member; Sustainable Development, Precautionary, Polluter Pays; excludes Wildlife Act & FRA.
* **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)**: Judicial review under Art 226/32 is Basic Structure; exclusion clauses void; all tribunals subject to High Court Division Bench.
* **Madras Bar Association (2014–2021)**: CJI-chaired selection committees; minimum 5-year tenure; demand for National Tribunals Commission.

## 34.8 Active Recall Diagnostic Cards

#### Card 1 (Constitutional Law & Judicial Review)
**Question**: Can Parliament, by constitutional amendment under Article 368, completely exclude the jurisdiction of High Courts over quasi-judicial regulatory tribunals?
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
<strong>NO.</strong> In the landmark 7-judge bench ruling in <em>L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)</em>, the Supreme Court unequivocally held that the power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 is an inviolable part of the <strong>Basic Structure of the Constitution</strong>. Because the Basic Structure cannot be abrogated or damaged even through a formal constitutional amendment under Article 368 (as settled in <em>Kesavananda Bharati</em> and <em>Minerva Mills</em>), any statutory provision or constitutional amendment that attempts to exclude the supervisory jurisdiction of High Courts over tribunals is <em>ultra vires</em> and void.
</details>

#### Card 2 (Environmental Tribunal Jurisdiction)
**Question**: An industrialist constructs a commercial tourist resort inside a national park, causing the displacement of endangered Asiatic lions. An environmental activist files an original application before the NGT under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972. How will the NGT rule on maintainability?
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
The NGT will dismiss the application as <strong>non-maintainable for want of statutory subject-matter jurisdiction</strong>. Under Schedule I of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, the NGT is vested with jurisdiction to adjudicate civil environmental disputes arising strictly out of seven specified enactments: the Water Act (1974), Water Cess Act (1977), Forest Conservation Act (1980), Air Act (1981), Environment Protection Act (1986), Public Liability Insurance Act (1991), and Biodiversity Act (2002). The <strong>Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 is conspicuously absent</strong> from Schedule I. The activist must seek relief by filing a writ petition before the High Court under Article 226 or approaching the Supreme Court.
</details>

#### Card 3 (Regulatory Separation of Powers)
**Question**: What structural flaw in the original Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) Act, 1997 prompted Parliament to enact the TRAI (Amendment) Act, 2000, creating TDSAT?
<details>
<summary>View Rigorous Causal Answer</summary>
Under the original 1997 enactment, TRAI was vested with both <strong>regulatory policy functions</strong> (recommending licenses, fixing interconnect tariffs) and <strong>dispute adjudicatory powers</strong> (resolving disputes between service providers, and between service providers and the government). In <em>Union of India v. TRAI (1998)</em>, the Delhi High Court held that TRAI could not issue directions to the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) in its licensing capacity. Recognizing the inherent judicial impropriety of an agency sitting in judgment over disputes involving its own regulatory directives, Parliament enacted the 2000 Amendment, stripping TRAI of its adjudicatory functions and creating an independent judicial body—the <strong>Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT)</strong>—headed by a retired Supreme Court judge.
</details>
